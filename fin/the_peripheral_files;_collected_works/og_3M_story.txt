“The Dielectric Corridor”
by Thomas Wulfe

South Carolina, 1980.
3M's film extrusion facility ran night and day, its machines screaming like banshees as polypropylene spooled off rolls the size of compact cars. David Swenson arrived just after dawn. The line foreman had refused to enter Bay 12. He said the air inside “wasn't right.” That it pushed back.

Swenson, a static charge engineer, assumed it was nonsense. Electrostatic accumulation from film unspooling at high speed was a known hazard. He clipped his electrometer to his belt, passed the yellow tape marking the danger zone, and stepped into the corridor.

The reading spiked instantly. 200 kilovolts per foot — the max range of the meter — and then the device shorted out with a puff of ozone.

The space hummed. The air around him felt denser, like standing chest-deep in warm molasses. His every hair stood on end. When he reached forward, his arm stopped as if pressing against a membrane. He could see the space ahead — a blur, like heat rising off asphalt — but he couldn’t move through it.

And then, the glow began.

Pale blue at first. Then violet.

And in that flickering light, the air shimmered not with dust, but shapes.

—PRESENT DAY—

Dr. Alix Vale was digging through forgotten lab reports in the Department of Energy’s “Cold Events Archive” when she found the 3M incident file, misfiled under incomplete materials studies. It had been marked for destruction three times. Every time, someone had crossed that directive out in different handwriting.

There were attached notes — not just lab readings, but field memos, and photos of strange damage patterns in concrete. One set of prints showed a man’s shadow on the wall, but no corresponding source. Another: a blurry extrusion line with a silhouette inside the static field. But the silhouette... didn't match the man who took the photo. In fact, it seemed to have more than two arms.

She cross-referenced Swenson. Retired. Living under federal protection, albeit unofficially. When she tracked him down, he lived in a cabin with walls lined in copper mesh.

“I don’t talk about that day,” he said. “But I still hear it. That sound. Like the universe breathing in.”

“What was it?” she asked.

He hesitated. “It wasn’t static.”

Later, at her university lab, Alix reconstructed the 3M conditions: polypropylene film, unspooling at speed, under high humidity and low ventilation. She set up a Faraday cage and ran the machine overnight, filming everything in 4K thermal.

At 03:21 AM, the temperature plummeted.

A thin blue veil formed between the reels, flickering in patterns resembling language.

Then came the shapes again.

Not insects. Not human. Not... symmetrical.

She approached, arm out. The air stopped her. She pressed forward. The veil shimmered, parted—and for a heartbeat—she felt herself split. Not torn. Doubled.

She came back different.

At first it was little things: static shocks that arced feet across the room. Her voice caused oscilloscopes to shiver. Audio equipment failed when she spoke near it.

But the dreams were the worst. Vast factories. Asymmetrical towers. Machines unspooling material across reality like film. In one dream, a voice — like a wind through a million wires — whispered:

“YOU BREACHED. YOU ARE NOW A LENS.”

Weeks Later.

Buildings across campus began to show anomalies: corners folding into themselves. Rooms that were bigger inside than outside. A graduate assistant opened a drawer and never came out.

Alix was found unconscious in Lab 3, surrounded by suspended dust, like a reverse snowglobe. Her eyes were wide open, glowing faintly blue, her breath slow and syncopated. Electrostatic readings in the room climbed off the charts.

But what was strangest — was the tape she left behind.

She’d recorded everything with high-speed film. When played back, frame-by-frame, they revealed frames not of the lab, but of another place:

A space of endless corridors made of translucent film. Shapes moving behind the membranes. Some... stopping. Watching.

Waiting.
