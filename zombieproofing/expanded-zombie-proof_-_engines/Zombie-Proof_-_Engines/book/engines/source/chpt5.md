Chapter 5: The Family, and a Short History

Engines are a family, not a ladder. The one at the top is not the winner. The winner is the one that matches the fuel, the load, the scale, and whether your civilisation still has a depot or only a woodpile and a grudge.

| Type | What it converts | Why you might want it | Why it bites | Where it usually lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steam, reciprocating | Fuel heat into steam pressure into a piston | Many fuels, high torque, a repairable idea | Boiler bulk, warm-up, pressure, losses | Old rail, ships, stationary work |
| Steam turbine | Steam into a fast rotor | Smooth and powerful when enormous | Poor when small, fussy blades | Stations, ships |
| Petrol, Otto cycle | A spark-lit charge into rotation | Light, eager, easy to start | Knock, thirst, less efficient than diesel | Cars, small generators, tools |
| Diesel | Air squeezed hot enough to light injected fuel | Efficient, durable, strong low-speed pull | Heavy, fussy injection, noise | Trucks, tractors, ships, generators |
| Wankel rotary | Combustion pushing a triangular rotor | Compact, smooth, few reciprocating parts | Seals, heat, thirst, emissions | A few cars, small aircraft |
| Electric motor | Current into magnetic force into rotation | Efficient, instant torque, quiet | Needs a battery or a wire, and the mass to store it | Vehicles, tools, shops |
| Air or hydraulic motor | Stored fluid pressure into motion | Controllable; kinder in some explosive airs | Needs a compressor or a pump and a reservoir | Tools, mines, machines |
| Gas turbine or jet | Fire into fast gas into shaft power or thrust | Ferocious power for the weight, at speed and scale | Hot, loud, hopeless at low speed, precision-made | Aircraft, stations |
| Water wheel or turbine | Falling water into rotation | Renewable where the hill cooperates | Goes nowhere without the hill, and the hill needs works | Mills, hydro |

You can also sort them by what they hand you: torque, a push in a line, shaft power, or thrust. Naming the output stops you bolting a peacock to a plough.

## A history short enough to carry

Hero of Alexandria showed a little steam reaction device long before anyone had a factory worth running. It was a demonstration, not a power system. The practical steam engines came later, through engineers including Newcomen and Watt, and they pumped mines, turned factories, and moved ships and trains. They were not subtle. They were available, and availability is a kind of genius.

Early gas engines, Lenoir's among them, proved you could burn the fuel inside the working cylinder. Efficiency arrived when people learned to compress the charge and time the burn. Nikolaus Otto's compressed four-stroke became the pattern petrol engines still wear. Rudolf Diesel's compression-ignition idea bought efficiency and low-speed torque at the price of heavier parts and a fuel system with no patience for dirt. Felix Wankel's rotary replaced the piston and the rod with a triangular rotor on an eccentric. It is a beautiful oddity and a reminder that elegance does not pay your seal bill.

Electric motors grew up beside the first generators and took over wherever electricity was the easier fuel to deliver. Gas turbines and turbojets became practical when metallurgy, compressors, burners, and very fast bearings caught up with the idea. None of this is a procession toward a single correct machine. It is a record of people matching a prime mover to a job.

The recurring lesson, which the yard will repeat until you are tired of it: the best engine is the one whose fuel you can store, whose failures you can see, and whose parts you can still find after the catalogue has gone to compost. A magnificent machine with no gasket is a sculpture. A dull machine with a spare filter is transport.
