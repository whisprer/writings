Chapter 17: When It Will Not Run

Measurements beat folklore. A compression gauge, a tachometer, a thermometer, a multimeter, a current clamp, a pressure gauge, a leak tester, and a notebook will outperform a heroic afternoon of swapping parts at random. The notebook is the tool that makes the others a method rather than a pile.

## It cranks, and nothing happens

```text
fuel actually there? ── no → fix the supply
        │ yes
mixture honest? ── no → clean, meter, find the leak
        │ yes
compression? ── no → valves, rings, gasket, timing
        │ yes
spark, and at the right time? ── no → ignition, timing
        │ yes
flooding, a blocked exhaust, a sulking sensor, a safety interlock doing its job
```

Work down the tree. Do not start in the middle because the middle is more interesting.

## It runs, and it runs badly

- **Backfire through the intake:** timing, a lean mixture, valve timing, or a valve that no longer seals.
- **Black smoke:** too much fuel, not enough air, a carburettor or an injector telling lies.
- **Blue smoke:** oil in the chamber, or too much oil in the crankcase.
- **White smoke:** coolant, water, or a cold engine clearing its throat. If it persists, stop calling it throat.
- **A hunting idle:** a vacuum leak, a governor that cannot decide, a mixture that wanders, a control fault.
- **Overheating:** low coolant, a blocked radiator, a pump that quit, a lean mixture, timing, too much load, not enough air across the fins.
- **Knock:** the wrong fuel, too much compression or boost, timing too far advanced, heat, deposits, octane that is not up to the engine's pride.
- **Vibration:** a misfire, an imbalance, a loose mount, a bent shaft, a bearing on its way out, a rotating assembly you should not be standing beside.

If you change a part, write down what the measurement was before and after. "Better" is not a number. A number can be checked by the next person, who may be you, in the rain, with worse light.

## Weather, which is also a mechanic

Engines breathe. Air density changes with height, heat, humidity, and the barometer. Thinner air means less oxygen in each gulp, and usually less power, unless something — a control system, a turbo, a patient retune — compensates. Weather also changes whether the fuel will vapourise for a cold start, whether the battery can be bothered, how thick the oil is, how hot the cooling air already is, whether the intake is drinking water, how fast the filter fills with dust or ash, and whether a still evening will hold the exhaust against the ground.

Salt and damp are a slow disassembly. Lightning and wet ground make the electrical path and the fuel path worse neighbours than usual. Keep generators away from dwellings, windows, vents, and doors. Store fuel in lawful containers, away from ignition and away from sleep.

## If the world has actually fallen over

Prioritise machines by what they can do for the living, not by how they sound in a story.

1. Hand tools, and tools that measure.
2. Pumps, and a way to make water drinkable.
3. Small engines a person can actually repair.
4. Charging, and low-voltage electrics.
5. Generators, and guarded belt drives.
6. The machines that mill, saw, drill, and work the ground.
7. Transport, and the drivetrains that make transport more than a push.
8. Only then the large steam, the large diesel, the turbine, the industrial plant.
9. Precision manufacture, metallurgy, and machine tools, which are how the earlier items continue to exist.

Keep the service manuals and the parts drawings. Keep fasteners, seals, lubricants, plugs, filters, belts, hose, wire, fuses, bearings, and gaskets. Keep the meters. Keep the knowledge of welding, brazing, machining, casting, and heat treatment, on paper, because paper does not need a bootloader. Keep every measurement that worked, and every modification, in a notebook that is not stored in the same puddle as the engine.

The engine is half of a civilisation. The other half is the workshop that can measure, repair, align, clean, lubricate, and teach. A roaring machine and a silent workshop is a countdown.

## Numbers you may carry, and must not worship

| Item | The figure in the source notes | The caveat that keeps it honest |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol thermal efficiency, cited | About 30 percent | Load and design move it |
| Diesel benchmark, cited | About 40 to 45 percent | A band, not your nameplate |
| Petrol shelf life, cited | 3 to 6 months | Heat, air, the blend, and the can all vote |
| Oil in a dormant bore | 24 to 48 hours | A hard seizure may want longer |
| Carburettor bath | About 93 °C / 200 °F | Only parts that can stand it; watch the heat |
| Soak | About 20 to 30 minutes | Then look, do not assume |
| Ethanol jet example | About 20 to 40 percent on diameter | An example, then you measure |
| Ethanol timing example | Up toward about 22° before top dead centre | Copying it blindly is how knock starts |
| Wood-gas power | Roughly 30 percent down on petrol, in the notes | Gas quality rules |
| Simple-Fire band | About 2 to 20 horsepower | Experimental, not a certificate |

## What this edition will not pretend to contain

There is no safe from-scratch recipe here for crankshafts, rods, pistons, rings, or liners. None for a certified boiler or any other pressure vessel. None for high-pressure diesel injection. None for a jet, a ramjet, a scramjet, a rocket, or an afterburner you could mistake for a project. None for industrial distillation or a chemical plant. None for weapons. None for an unreviewed high-voltage lash-up. None for a universal limit of speed, temperature, pressure, or torque that would let you skip the manual for the engine in front of you.

Those things need specifications, fatigue, heat, tolerances, inspection, and a code. "The apocalypse made me do it" is not a pressure-vessel code. It is a sentence. Sentences do not hold pressure.

Start with a known engine. Make it safe. Make it turn. Make it breathe. Make it lubricate. Make it spark, or inject, at the right time. Make it cool. Then make it work.

Only after that should you ask it to run on the forest, pull a load, charge a settlement, or outlast the horde.

The dead may walk. The engine still needs oil. The oil is due in the morning, and the morning does not care whether you felt finished tonight.
