Foreword

Someone always asks, at the bad moment, whether it will start. They mean: can we move. They mean: can we pump, mill, charge, and leave.

This manual is an answer with oil on it. It begins where an engine actually begins, which is not with a roar but with a decision not to be poisoned, scalded, or shortened by a belt. Then it says what the machine is. A heat engine, a motor, a turbine, a wheel in a stream: energy in, motion out, and a load that has to be met without the machine becoming the emergency.

You will find the four-stroke told slowly, because most of the engines you will actually meet are that creature wearing a different coat. You will find fuels, including the tempting ones — ethanol, wood gas — treated as conversions, not as dares. You will find a sequence for waking an engine that has spent years plotting corrosion. Later chapters wander into steam you should not invent, electricity that does not burn, the path from crank to wheel, and a gallery of advanced machines described honestly enough that you will not try to build a ramjet out of a drainpipe.

The zombies are a convenience. They stand in for hurry. A seized ring, a vanished fuel depot, a person who wants the generator to be a personality instead of a tool. If you are reading this while the world is, against the odds, still selling filters, the zombies will not mind. The oil is the same on a Tuesday.

A word about the pages that mention pressure, producer gas, and high-voltage spark. Curiosity is not a licence. Do not build a boiler because a chapter explained the Rankine cycle in a friendly voice. Do not run a gasifier in a kitchen. Do not hold a plug lead to see if the night is still electrical. The jokes are jokes. A grid can fail and the rules about pressure vessels can still be standing. Carbon monoxide does not care that you labelled the drum.

What I hope you keep is not a single engine. It is the habit of a machine you understand: what it breathes, what it burns or refuses to burn, how it makes oil pressure, how it makes waste heat, and how you turn it off without heroics. Engines are poor company and excellent servants. They do not replace the person who maintained them. They only make it possible for the water to move in the morning.

Leave your name off the title page until you want it there. The machine does not require a byline. It requires a limit, a little discipline, and somebody who checked the oil.
