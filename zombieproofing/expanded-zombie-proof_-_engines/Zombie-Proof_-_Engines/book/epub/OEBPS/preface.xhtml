A note on the drafts

This manual was expanded from a foundation draft: a safety page, a family tree, the physics without the chanting, fuels, a revival sequence, field cards, and a firm refusal to pretend that boilers and jets are weekend projects. The draft was already careful. What it lacked was the yard. The jokes, the zombies, and the habit of saying a hard rule twice in a human voice were put in afterwards. The engineering was not loosened to make room for them.

Where the draft gave a number — a shelf life, a cleaning temperature, an example jet, a wood-gas composition — the number is still here, and it is still labelled as indicative. A historical starting point is not a recipe. If a later manual for your actual engine disagrees, the later manual wins, and this book will not be offended.

The projects at the back are teaching projects. The ones with code do not contain a flywheel. The one that mentions steam points at a certified model and then points, harder, at the cooker you will leave on the shelf. The gasifier is discussed as a theory and a hazard, not as a Saturday build.

You can read it straight through, or raid the contents the way you would raid a parts bin. The first part is how not to die of enthusiasm. The middle is what the machine is and what you feed it. The last part is how to wake one, how to keep it, and what this edition will not pretend to teach.

There is a dull warning, and it is dull on purpose. Do not run an engine indoors to see if the alarm works. Do not defeat a relief valve because it is noisy. Do not treat a humor book as a manufacturing certificate. The undead are a metaphor. A short-circuited fuel hose is not.

The dedication, the author’s name, and the publisher are blank on purpose. Write them in when you know them. Ink, not pencil. Pencil is for clearances.
