Foreword

Someone always asks, at the bad moment, whether the radio is on. They mean: is there a voice. They mean: are we still a we.

This manual is an answer with grease on it. It begins where radio actually begins, which is not with a brand of handset but with a wave that does not care about your afternoon. Frequency, wavelength, the stubborn speed of light. Then antennas, which are only metal arranged so the air will agree with you. Then the languages signals speak when they are trying not to be mistaken for noise. Then the unglamorous trinity: power, repair, and a place to put the thing so the rain, and the neighbors, do not improve it.

You will find projects. Some of them can be done with scavenged wire and a patience you may have to borrow. Some of them want a software-defined radio, a notebook, and the humility to read the band plan before you key up. Later chapters wander into pagers, contactless scraps, packet, radar-ish tricks, and the old secret of amateur radio, which was never secret: listen more than you talk, and sign your work.

The zombies are a convenience. They stand in for every reason a network fails. Panic, distance, weather, a dead battery, a person who will not stop transmitting their grocery list on the calling frequency. If you are reading this while the world is, against the odds, still answering its phone, the zombies will not mind. The physics is the same on a Tuesday.

A word about the pages that mention jamming, interception, and cleverness at other people’s expense. Cleverness is not a license. The book says so in its own chapters, and the copyright page says so again, because some readers skip to the interesting part. Be one of the ones who does not.

What I hope you keep is not a single circuit. It is the habit of a net: a time, a frequency, a way to say you are still there, and a way to say it shorter than your fear would prefer. Radios are poor company and excellent neighbors. They do not replace the person on the other end. They only make it possible for that person to be far away and still count.

Leave your name off the title page until you want it there. The air does not require a byline. It requires a carrier, a little discipline, and somebody listening at the hour you promised.

What Is Radio? (And Why Zombies Should Care)

Welcome, post-civ survivor! Whether you're scavenging K-pop t-shirts or building a bunker out of PlayStation cases, you’ll soon discover that radio is the real cheat-code for communication in the end times.

What Counts as "Radio"?

  • Radio is electromagnetic energy, part of the spectrum squeezed between sound waves and microwaves.
  • It’s how walkie-talkies, satellites, airport scanners, your Bluetooth speaker, and even some microwaves talk to each other.
  • Radios work by tossing electromagnetic waves (oscillating electric and magnetic fields) through space at the speed of light.
  • Radio = electric and magnetic fields oscillating together
  • Frequency (f), wavelength (λ), and the speed of light (c) are related by λ = cf.
  • Radio is strictly physical, not magic — and sometimes, not even digital!

A Brief Meme-Ridden History of Radio

  • In the 1930s-1940s, radios were living room centerpieces, bigger than most survivors' bugout bags.
  • Orson Welles panicked a nation with La Guerra de los Mundos (War of the Worlds) — proving radios could out-troll TikTok, even before trolls existed.
  • Old-time shows were like audio Netflix, with suspense, laughs, and science fiction all at the twist of a dial.

Why Bother After the End of the World?

  • When the grid dies and cell towers are overrun by zombies (or just out-of-service teens), only radio still connects people.
  • All modern “wireless” stuff — WiFi, Bluetooth, walkie-talkies, GPS, remote car keys, contactless credit cards — is just radio in a fancier wrapper.
  • True apocalypse survivors use radio for:
    • Long-distance memes and squad comms.
    • Weather alerts (to avoid mutant hurricanes).
    • Fox hunting (actually finding hidden transmissions, not animals).
    • Disaster coordination and hacking.
    • Trolling and eavesdropping on the unprepared.

Chapter 2

The Physics of Waves — How Radio Becomes Superpowered

Introduction: Why Physics Matters (Even in the Zombie Apocalypse)

Before you start building antennas, hacking signals, or jammin’ droids, you gotta understand what actually makes a radio wave. Think of radio not as magic but as pure electromagnetic (EM) energy traveling at light-speed in the chaos of the spectrum.

Electromagnetic Waves: The Core Concept

  • All radio signals are EM waves, oscillating electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields intertwined.
  • These waves are transverse, meaning the electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to the wave’s direction.
  • They carry energy across space, bouncing around like the chaos in a zombie horde.

Wave Parameters: Wavelength, Frequency, Speed

Speed of Light

  • All EM waves travel at c = 299,792,458  m/s.
  • Imagine them as zombies moving at the speed of light—nothing can outrun them.

Wavelength (λ)

  • Distance between two wave peaks.
  • λ = cf, where f is the frequency.

Frequency (f)

  • How many wave peaks pass a point per second.
  • Hz (Hertz) for normal radios, kHz, MHz, GHz for different piles of spectrum.

Example Calculation

  • A 100 MHz FM station has a wavelength:
    λ = 3 × 108108 = 3  m.

How Waves Propagate

Line of Sight (LOS)

  • In open terrain, waves travel straight like a laser beam—perfect for clear radio paths.
  • No hills or zombies in the way: simple, direct.

Reflection & Diffraction

  • When disruptive terrain (buildings, mountains, zombies) are present, waves bounce or bend.
  • Critical for communication across ruins: antennas and waveguides are modern-day magic tools.

Interference & Multipath

  • Multiple waves colliding may cancel out or amplify each other.
  • Your signal could be zombie-strong or zombie-weak, depending on the chaos.

Attenuation & Signal Loss

  • As waves travel, they weaken — similar to a meme losing punch the further it spreads.
  • Decibels (dB) measure how much the signal drops.
  • dB = 10 × log10(PoutPin)

Modulation & Data Embedding

  • Embedding info onto waves—like surreptitious radio memes or encrypted alien transmissions.
  • Types:
    • Amplitude Modulation (AM): Change the wave’s volume.
    • Frequency Modulation (FM): Change the pitch.
    • Phase Shift Keying (PSK): Shift the wave’s phase—great for hackers.
    • Frequency-Shift Keying (FSK): Switch between different frequencies.

Signal Spectrum: The Wild West

  • Different signals occupy different bands:
    • ELF (Extremely Low Frequency): Deep underground comms.
    • VLF (Very Low Frequency): Submarine comms.
    • HF (High Frequency, 3-30 MHz): CB, shortwave, pirate radio.
    • VHF, UHF (Very/Ultra High Frequency): TV, FM, cell phones, WiFi.
    • Microwaves (GHz): Radar, satellite links, microwave ovens.

How to Visualize EM Waves

  • Imagine a wave as a roller coaster: height = electric field, vertical length = magnetic field.

  • Wavelength is the distance from the hilltops, frequency is how often the roller coaster rides pass.

Chapter 3

Signal Science — Types, Strength, and Survival

Overview: Why Signal Matters in Apocalypse Radio

If you want reliable comms, hack the spectrum, or just yak with your crew while zombies roam, understanding signal types, behaviors, and strengths is a must. This chapter unlocks the physics, math, and practicalities of signals—your lifeline in the airwaves.

Signal Types and Modulations

  • Continuous Wave (CW): Simple on/off pulses, Morse code style, zombie classic.
  • Amplitude Modulation (AM): Vary signal strength to encode info; simple but noise-prone.
  • Frequency Modulation (FM): Shift frequency; less noise, better music and voices.
  • Phase Shift Keying (PSK): Hack-level encoding by changing phase of wave.
  • Frequency Shift Keying (FSK): Digital style frequency switches.
  • Spread Spectrum: Secret sauce for jam resistance and secure comms (includes FHSS and DSSS).

Signal Strength & Measuring Power

  • Signal power measured in Watts (W) or dBm (decibels referenced to 1 milliwatt).
  • P(dBm) = 10 × log10 left(P(W)1mWright).
  • Field strength (Volts per meter, V/m) tells actual wave voltage in air.
  • Free space path loss (FSPL) law describes how signal weakens with distance d:

FSPL(dB) = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) + 20 log10 left(4πcright)

  • Practical survival tip: antenna gain can boost received signal strength by degrees.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

  • How clean your signal is vs background noise.
  • High SNR = clear messages; low SNR = zombie static and confusion.
  • Techniques to improve SNR:
    • Use directional antennas.
    • Increase transmitter power or receiver sensitivity.
    • Use digital protocols with error correction.

Doppler Effect in Radio

  • Moving zombies and vehicles change perceived frequency.
  • Important for fast-moving drones or when tracking survivors.
  • Formula for shifted frequency:

f' = f × c + vrc + vs

where vr and vs are velocities of receiver and source.

Advanced Signal Handling

  • Filtering: Removing unwanted frequencies/noise.
  • Mixing and Heterodyning: Creating new frequencies by combining signals.
  • IQ Modulation: Complex amplitude and phase control for SDR and advanced radios.

Chapter 4

Antennas — Your Signal’s Best Friend (and Sometimes Worst Enemy)

Why Antennas Are the Game-Changers

No matter how powerful your transmitter or clever your code, an antenna is what throws your message into the wild and catches the buzz from your squad. Knowing antennas means knowing how to maximize range, minimize noise, and survive the signal jungle.

Antenna Basics

  • An antenna converts electric signals into EM waves for transmission and vice versa for reception.
  • It behaves as an electrical conductor matched to a wavelength or fraction thereof.
  • Types include dipoles, loops, yagis, ground planes, and fractals.
  • Resonance: Antennas must be tuned to their operational frequency for maximum efficiency.
  • Polarization: Vertical, horizontal, or circular polarization affect signal clarity and range.

Antenna Theory Highlights

Radiation Pattern

  • Describes how an antenna distributes energy in space.
  • Omni-directional antennas radiate equally in all directions.
  • Directional antennas focus energy in specific directions for longer range or less interference.

Gain

  • Measures antenna efficiency and focus compared to a theoretical isotropic radiator.
  • Expressed in dBi (decibels over isotropic radiator).
  • Higher gain = longer directed reach but narrower beamwidth.

Bandwidth

  • Frequency range over which the antenna operates effectively.
  • Wider bandwidth helps handle signal hopping and multipath fading.

Practical Antenna Tips For Survivors

  • Use the longest wire possible for simple dipoles.
  • Raise your antenna away from metallic surfaces to avoid signal loss.
  • Ground planes and radials increase effectiveness for vertical antennas.
  • Match antenna impedance (usually 50 or 75 ohms) to your radio gear to prevent reflections.

Chapter 5

Radio Protocols & Data — Speaking Zombie Fluently

Introduction: The Language of Radio Signals

Once you understand how radio waves travel and how antennas broadcast those waves, it's crucial to know how information is encoded and decoded. Radio protocols are the grammar and vocabulary of wireless communication — without them, your radio is just static.

Common Radio Protocols for Survivors

  • Morse Code
    Old but gold. A series of short and long signals (dots and dashes) used as a simple digital alphabet.

  • AM/FM
    Analog modulation; AM varies amplitude, FM frequency. Widely used for broadcasting voice and music.

  • Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)
    Digital method switching between frequencies to represent binary data.

  • Phase Shift Keying (PSK)
    Digital phase changes of the carrier wave. PSK31 is famous among radio hackers.

  • Packet Radio
    Transmits data in small packets with headers, checksums, and error-correction.

  • Wi-Fi & Bluetooth
    Higher-frequency protocols for local area networking and device communication.

  • LoRa / LoRaWAN
    Long-range, low-power digital modulation for IoT and survival sensor nets.

Protocol Layers Simplified

  • Physical Layer: How signals are sent (AM/FM, FSK, etc.)
  • Data Link Layer: Packet framing, error detection
  • Network Layer: Routing and addressing (mesh, IP)
  • Application Layer: Actual message content, commands.

Digital Encoding and Error Correction

  • Encoding schemes like Manchester, NRZ for reliable data recovery.
  • Error correction: Forward Error Correction (FEC), CRC codes to detect and fix errors.

Open Standards & OSS Radio Protocols

  • Popular open-source protocols you can hack on or implement for survival networks.
  • Includes APRS, AX.25, POCSAG pager codes, and much more.

Signal Timing & Synchronization

  • Clocking signals and synchronization maintain proper message decoding.
  • Important in spread-spectrum and frequency hopping protocols to avoid jamming.

Chapter 7

Advanced Radio Hacking, Jamming, and Security — Outsmart the Horde

Introduction: Going Beyond Survival

Real survivors not only build radios—they hack spectrum, defend comms, and exploit weaknesses. Welcome to the wild side where radio meets espionage, warfare, and digital trench battles.

Jamming and Anti-Jamming Tactics

  • Jamming: Intentional interference to block or confuse signals.
  • Types include barrage jamming, spot jamming, sweep jamming.
  • Anti-jamming:
    • Frequency hopping (FHSS) to jump across frequencies unpredictably.
    • Direct sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) to hide signals from interference.
    • Using directional antennas to avoid jammers.

Encryption and Secure Comms

  • Use simple ciphers (OTP, Caesar, substitution) for voice and morse.
  • Digital encryption standards in SDR and ham radio (AES, DES).
  • Steganography: embedding messages in noise or seemingly innocent data.

Protocol Hacking and Injection

  • Packet sniffing and injection for APRS, POCSAG, and pager systems.
  • Exploiting weak OTP reuse or poor key management.
  • Replay attacks: record and resend signals to gain unauthorized control.

Hardware Mods and Software Tools

  • Using SDR platforms (HackRF, RTL-SDR) to scan, decode, and inject.
  • Building jammers or spoofers from off-the-shelf parts.
  • Python and GNU Radio scripting to automate attacks or monitoring.

Ethical and Legal Warning

  • Many techniques are illegal or restricted.
  • In the apocalypse, “legal” may mean “survival,” but consequences exist.
  • Always understand your local laws and risks when experimenting.

Survival Tips

  • Keep your hackery gear hidden and mobile.
  • Use layered security in comms.
  • Stay updated on signals intelligence and jammer detection.

Chapter 8

Satellite Comms, Weather, and Space Survival Radio

Introduction: Beyond Earthbound — Talking Satellite and Weather Signals

When zombies crash your radio party, the sky’s the limit. Satellite and space weather radios open new doors — from tracking storms to reaching orbiting survivors.

Satellite Communications Basics

  • Satellites relay signals globally, bouncing EM waves over the curvature of the Earth.
  • Types:
    • LEO (Low Earth Orbit): Fast-moving, short range, low latency. Examples: Starlink, Iridium.
    • MEO (Medium Earth Orbit): GPS system satellites.
    • GEO (Geostationary Earth Orbit): Fixed position relative to Earth, used for TV, weather, and comms.

How To Receive Satellite Signals

  • Use directional antennas and tracking rotators for LEOs.
  • GEO satellites can be tracked with fixed dishes aimed at the right coordinates.
  • Be prepared for Doppler shifts and fading signals on LEOs due to rapid orbit.

Space Weather and Its Impact on Radio

  • Solar flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs) can disrupt HF and VHF propagation.
  • Effects include scintillation, ionospheric disturbances, and blackouts.
  • Space weather alerts available via NOAA and amateur shortwave broadcasts—vital info during survival.

Atomic Clock Basics & Synchronization

  • Satellite timekeeping involves atomic clocks critical for GPS functionality.
  • Understanding clock drift and synchronization helps in timing-sensitive comm protocols.

DIY Satellite Signal Projects

  • Build a simple NOAA weather satellite signal receiver using SDR.

Radio Wave Propagation & Environment Effects — Navigating the Zombie Jungle

Introduction: The Wild World of Wave Travel

Radio doesn’t just fly straight forever—terrain, weather, and even undead hordes mess it up. This chapter explores how signals travel, bend, fade, and bounce, so you can exploit or counteract those effects.

Propagation Modes

  • Ground Wave: Follows the Earth surface; good for LF/MF bands; limited by terrain conductivity.
  • Sky Wave (Ionospheric Reflection): Bounces off ionosphere layers; enables long-range HF comms but affected by solar activity.
  • Line of Sight (LOS): Direct path, essential for VHF/UHF and microwave.
  • Tropospheric Scatter: Uses troposphere irregularities to scatter signals beyond LOS.
  • Meteor Scatter: Exploits ionized trails of meteors for brief long-distance bursts.
  • Satellite Relay: Signals bounce via orbiting satellites.

Environmental Effects on Propagation

  • Terrain: Mountains, forests, buildings absorb or reflect waves.
  • Weather: Rain, fog, and humidity can attenuate or scatter signals.
  • Temperature Inversions: Cause ducting, allowing signals to travel unusually far.
  • Solar Activity: Sunspots and solar flares cause ionospheric disturbances.
  • Urban Interference: Man-made noise worsens reception.

Wave Polarization and Its Effects

  • Orientation of the wave's electric field affects signal clarity.
  • Mismatch in polarization between TX and RX antennas causes signal loss.
  • Circular polarization useful for satellite and helicopter comms.

Fading & Multipath

  • Signal strength fluctuates due to multiple path arrivals.
  • Causes distortion, phase shift, and signal dropouts.
  • Combat with diversity antennas and spread spectrum.

Practical Propagation Tips for Survivors

  • Scout terrain and choose antenna placement with clear horizons.
  • Use higher frequencies for line-of-sight; lower for long-distance.
  • Monitor solar conditions and plan HF comms accordingly.
  • Use weather forecasts; avoid storms during critical comm windows.

Introduction to POCSAG

POCSAG (Post Office Code Standardisation Advisory Group) is a widely used paging protocol for sending numeric or alphanumeric messages over radio channels. It’s foundational for pager communications.

Uses 512, 1200, or 2400 baud transmission rates.

Data is encoded using a BCH (Bose–Chaudhuri–Hocquenghem) error-correcting code.

Messages include address and function bits, followed by message data.

POCSAG Decoding

Receive the bitstream via a radio or SDR tuned to the pager frequency.

Synchronize to the sync codeword to align decoding.

BCH decoding corrects bit errors.

Extract pager address and message payload.

Payload parsing differentiates numeric and alphanumeric message types.

SMS (Short Message Service) Basics

SMS uses a store-and-forward system in cellular networks.

Text messages are encoded often in 7-bit GSM default alphabet or UCS-2 for Unicode.

Messages are packetized into Protocol Data Units (PDUs) with headers including sender, recipient, timestamp.

SMS uses signaling channels in GSM, but internet-based SMS uses SMPP or other push protocols.

SMS Decoding Essentials

Decode PDU blocks to extract text and metadata.

Handle message segmentation and reassembly for long texts.

Implement character set decoding based on GSM 03.38 or UCS-2 standards.

Ringtone Formats Overview

Early mobile ringtones were simple monophonic formats, often stored in RTTTL (Ring Tone Text Transfer Language) or MIDI.

RTTTL encodes music as note, duration, and octave in plain text strings.

Modern phones use polyphonic MIDI or audio file formats (WAV, MP3).

Decoding involves interpreting note commands or parsing audio waveform data.

The Protocol Zoo

Community. Build a network of fellow survivors. Share knowledge, resources, and support. Together, you’re stronger.

Afterword

The last chapter of a radio book is a temptation to get solemn. Resist it a little. The band is not a chapel. It is a bench you share with strangers, and the courtesy that keeps it usable is older than any of us: listen first, sign your work, leave a gap so someone else can answer.

If you have read this far you already know the unglamorous truth. Range is an antenna and a clear path more often than it is a bigger battery. A net is a promise about time as much as it is a piece of hardware. Encryption is a tool, not a personality. And the person who brings a spare connector will be remembered more kindly than the person who brings a speech.

The zombies, if you still need them, are only the name we gave the interruption. Weather is one. A dead coax is one. Panic on the calling frequency is one. You do not beat them by being louder. You beat them by being findable, brief, and still there at the hour you named.

Leave a frequency in the log. Tell someone else what it is. When you are done for the night, say so, and mean it, so nobody sits up listening to static out of loyalty.
