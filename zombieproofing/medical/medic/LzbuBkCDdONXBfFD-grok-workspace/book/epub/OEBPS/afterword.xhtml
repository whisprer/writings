Afterword

The last chapter of a medical book is a temptation to get solemn about civilisation. Resist it a little. The yard is not a chapel. It is a patch of ground you share with whoever needs a dressing changed, a drink they can swallow, or a watch taken so that somebody else can sleep, and the courtesy that keeps it usable is older than any of us: know what you are doing, stop when you do not, and leave a way to tell the next person that you did.

If you have read this far you already know the unglamorous truth. The horde was never the curriculum. The curriculum was soap, a note with the time on it, and the decision not to invent a procedure because the evening had become cinematic. The person who brings gloves that fit will be remembered more kindly than the person who brings a speech.

The zombies, if you still need them, are only the name we gave the interruption. A skipped hand-wash is one. A cupboard above the cooker is one. Panic that loosens a tourniquet to see what happens is one. You do not beat them by adding blades. You beat them by being able to say, on paper, what you saw and what you did.

Leave a note on the kit. Tell someone else where it is, what was used, and who is being watched. When you are done for the night, say so, and put the torch where the next hands can find it.

The signature under an afterword belongs to the person who stands by the book. That line is blank until you want it.

Check them. Then quiet.
