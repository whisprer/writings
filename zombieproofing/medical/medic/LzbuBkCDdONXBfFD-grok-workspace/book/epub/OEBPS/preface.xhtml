A note on the drafts

This manual was set from a complete civilian draft — seventeen chapters, already in the yard voice, already refusing the operating theatre — and from a thicker pile that arrived with it: an airway card for prolonged field care, a two-page cheat sheet, and an essay about which medicines to take from a hospital that is no longer answering.

The draft was kept. The pile was used as a boundary. Where the card described a surgical airway, a drip, or a drug a layperson cannot safely choose, this edition says what the danger is called and then stops. Where the essay described a route through a basement and a broken cabinet, this edition says: buy the dull things while the chemist is open, keep them cool and named, and do not steal a stranger’s care. Nigel, who climbs through the pharmacy window in chapter one, is the joke. He is not the method.

The first-aid numbers that remain — a depth, a rate, a boil, a fever threshold in a baby — are the public ones, and they are still subordinate to a course, a dispatcher, and the packet in your hand. Guidance moves. If this book and the Resuscitation Council, the NHS, or the label disagree, they win, and the book is a well-meaning stranger with a damp cuff.

You can read it straight through, or raid the contents the way you would raid a kit. The first part is how not to become the second patient. The middle is blood, dirt, weather, and the illnesses that do not need a horde. The cupboard comes after that, on purpose, so the shopping list does not feel like the point. The last part is the days after the rescue, which are most of the days.

There is a dull warning, and it is dull on purpose. Do not practise chest compressions on a person who is awake. Do not close a bite with a sewing kit. Do not share one person’s inhaler as if it were fruit. The undead are a metaphor. A tourniquet is not.

The dedication, the author’s name, and the publisher are blank on purpose. Write them in when you know them. The .tex beside this edition is the easy place. Ink, not pencil. Pencil is for observation sheets, which are supposed to change.
