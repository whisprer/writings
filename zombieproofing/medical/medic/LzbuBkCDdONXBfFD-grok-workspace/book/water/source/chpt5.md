Chapter 5: The Pipes That Have Been Waiting

A town is full of water that is not yours. The most tempting wrong example is the fire sprinkler. It is a pipe, it is full, and in a bad month it looks like a well that learned about ceilings.

It is not a well. Sprinkler lines are closed. The water in them does not circulate. It sits, sometimes for years, at room temperature, which is the temperature bacteria write home about. Anaerobic films grow. Iron-eating and sulphate-reducing organisms make slime and pinholes. Legionella and ordinary coliforms are not a surprise in that kind of stillness. Metals leach: iron, and more importantly lead, copper, and zinc, at levels the drinking-water limits were invented to forbid. In cold countries the pipe may also contain antifreeze — propylene glycol, ethylene glycol, or a corrosion inhibitor with a marketing department. High glycol is toxic. It is also, in a fire, not the thing you want to be casually decanting near a flame.

Bleach will not fix this. Chlorine is a weapon against many living things. It is not a magnet for dissolved lead, and it does not cancel a solvent. The notes in the pile are blunt: if you were going to try to make such water drinkable you would be talking about settling, activated carbon, and distillation, and even then some chemicals travel with steam or refuse to be a weekend. That is an industrial sentence. It is not a shopping list. This book will not walk you through a still. A still that is also a pressure vessel is how garages leave the street.

## Backflow is a door, and it should stay a door

There is a second lesson hiding in the sprinkler, and it is about the ordinary house. A sprinkler, a hose, a boiler, and a drinking tap should not be able to kiss. Backflow preventers exist so that a stagnant, chemically peculiar pipe cannot wander backward into the water you actually drink. If you are still in a world with inspectors, do not defeat those valves because a hose would be more convenient. If you are not, do not invent a shortcut that ties a fire main to a kitchen tap "just while we sort it." The shortcut is the contamination.

If the only water you can see is in a sprinkler riser, you are in a worse day than this chapter can repair. Look again for the roof, the bottled store, the neighbour, the kettle you can still boil a ditch in — a ditch is a miserable source, but it is a biological miserable, and biological miserable is a problem boiling understands. Glycol is not.

Leave the riser alone. Let the zombie try it. That is what the pictures are for.
