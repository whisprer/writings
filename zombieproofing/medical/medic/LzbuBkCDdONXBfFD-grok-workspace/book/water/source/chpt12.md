Chapter 12: The Dark Cupboard

Treated water has a brief career as safe water, and then the world tries to reapply for the job.

The container is half the method. Food-grade plastic — the bottles and jugs actually sold for water, the heavy polyethylene that is not a leftover from the shed — or stainless steel, or glass. Not a fuel can. Not a pesticide drum rinsed "until it seemed fine." Not a milk jug that has gone soft in the sun if you can help it, and never a jug you cannot close. Opaque, or kept in the dark. Light is how algae get a vote, and some of those blooms make toxins boiling is not a cheerful answer to.

Fill containers when life is boring, date them, and keep them off the concrete floor if the flood is a local hobby. A little air gap at the top is fine; a lid left cocked so the cat can investigate is not. Dip with a clean cup you do not then use to bail the ditch. Better, a tap at the bottom, so nobody's hands go swimming.

## The faint smell is a sentry, not a flavour goal

Water you disinfected with chlorine can keep a free residual, and that residual is what stops a casual recontamination from becoming a colony. The notes talk about a free chlorine residual on the order of two tenths to one milligram per litre as the sort of band public practice likes, which in a kitchen without a test kit means a faint smell, not a gasp. If the smell has gone and the water has been sitting, the same notes suggest a small top-up — a drop or two of ordinary household bleach per gallon — then a stir and a wait, and then you taste-check by smell again. If you have a pool test strip that reads free chlorine, this is one of the few times a pool gadget is welcome in the kitchen, used as a reader and not as a dosing philosophy. If you are unsure, boil. Boiling is always allowed to win.

Do not chase a stronger and stronger dose because the water "might as well be safe." Too much chlorine is its own unpleasantness, and it is not how you remove a chemical. Aerate, or start over from a better source.

Clean a container before you trust it for the season: wash, rinse, and a chlorine solution you then rinse out or, if you are storing water that will be drunk with a residual anyway, follow a published storage card rather than inventing a spa. Leave the cap loose nowhere. Label the jug with the date and the method, in ink. The next person, who may be you at 2 a.m., deserves a sentence they can read.

The cupboard is dark. The lid fits. The zombie leaves the cap on the floor, which is why the zombie is not in charge of the water.
