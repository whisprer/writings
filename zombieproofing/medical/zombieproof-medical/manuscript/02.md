# The Person Before the Problem

A first-aid bag contains useful objects. It does not contain an extra brain. That is why you need a simple order of work before the world becomes a cupboard full of falling saucepans.

Start with safety, response, help and breathing. Deal promptly with an obvious life-threatening bleed. If other people are present, allocate the jobs at the same time: one calls, one controls bleeding, one assesses the person. A memorable acronym is useful only if it gets the right action done. [R01, R02, R03]

## Is this person responding?

Speak loudly and gently touch an adult's shoulders. Ask them to open their eyes. Do not shake someone violently or test responsiveness by causing pain. If they do not respond, call 999 immediately, preferably on speaker. Ask a helper to fetch an AED. Assess breathing while the call connects; the current UK adult guidance places the call early. [R01]

Open the airway with a gentle head tilt and chin lift and check for normal breathing for no more than ten seconds. Occasional gasps, laboured irregular breaths and panting in an unresponsive person are not reassurance. Do not waste time looking for a pulse if you are a lay first aider. If breathing is absent or abnormal, or you are unsure, start CPR and follow the call handler. [R01, R02]

## Adult CPR: useful repetition

Place the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone in the centre of the chest, the other on top. Keep your shoulders over your hands and your arms straight. Press 5-6 cm deep at 100-120 compressions per minute, allowing the chest to rise fully between compressions. Minimise pauses. If the person is on a bed, begin there rather than delaying CPR to move them. [R01]

If trained and able, give 30 compressions followed by two rescue breaths, each just enough to make the chest start to rise. Otherwise give continuous chest compressions. Use an AED as soon as available: switch it on, expose the chest as required, follow its pad diagrams and prompts, and ensure nobody touches the person during analysis or a shock. Resume compressions promptly when instructed. An AED saying "no shock" does not necessarily mean the person is well. [R01, R02]

Continue until trained help takes over, the person shows signs of life and breathes normally, you cannot physically continue, or the scene becomes unsafe. Swap rescuers roughly every two minutes where possible without long pauses. Children and babies need age-appropriate methods, including a strong emphasis on rescue breaths; Chapter 15 gives an emergency summary. [R02, R17, R18]

## Unresponsive, but breathing normally

Call for help. In a person without suspected significant trauma, the recovery position helps protect the airway: roll them onto their side with the head supported and the mouth directed downward, then keep checking their breathing. Do not prop them up and leave. If breathing becomes abnormal, roll them onto their back and begin CPR. [R04]

With suspected neck or spinal injury, minimise movement and support the head in the position found. Airway and breathing take priority: if vomit or obstruction threatens breathing, move them enough to protect the airway, preferably with helpers keeping head, neck and trunk aligned. Do not force a recovery position merely to finish a checklist. Follow emergency-service instructions. [R03, R04]

## Awake is not the same as fine

An awake person can tell you where it hurts, but begin with what could kill them: major bleeding, severe breathing difficulty, collapse, confusion, chest pain, stroke symptoms or a rapidly worsening allergic reaction. Call emergency help. Do not give food, drink or tablets to someone who is drowsy, choking, vomiting heavily or unable to swallow safely.

Once immediate threats are addressed, ask what happened and what changed. Record allergies, usual medicines, relevant illnesses and the last food or drink. Note the mechanism: a fall from height, heavy crush or penetrating injury can matter more than an unimpressive mark on the skin. Ask the person what normal looks like for them. A disability or communication difference is not evidence of a new brain injury.

## Choking in an adult

If they can cough effectively, encourage coughing and watch closely. If they cannot speak, breathe or cough effectively, lean them forward and give up to five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades, checking after each. If unsuccessful, give up to five abdominal thrusts: stand behind, place a fist above the navel and below the breastbone, grasp it and pull sharply inward and upward. [R19]

Have someone call emergency help; if alone, call once the first cycle has failed and continue alternating blows and thrusts. Chest thrusts are used instead when abdominal thrusts are unsuitable, such as later pregnancy or when you cannot encircle the abdomen; learn these in practical training. If the person becomes unresponsive, lower them safely, call for help and start CPR. Never sweep blindly with a finger. Anyone who needed abdominal thrusts needs medical assessment afterwards. Infants require a different method. [R19, R17, R18]

> FIELD RULE: Observe, act, observe again. The patient is allowed to change their condition halfway through your excellent plan.

**Sources:** R01-R04, R17-R19. CPR and choking require practical training; follow dispatcher guidance when available.
