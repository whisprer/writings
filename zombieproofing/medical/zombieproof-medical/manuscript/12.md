# A Pharmacy Is Not a Time Capsule

The medicine cupboard is warm, damp, unlabelled and located above the cooker. Nigel calls it "future-proof". In fairness, it has already become a warning to the future.

Medicines and medical supplies have different storage requirements. Tablets, insulin, inhalers, auto-injectors, dressings and test strips are not interchangeable forms of a single substance called medical. Read the actual product and keep its instructions with it.

## The long-term store

Keep products in original labelled packaging, protected from children, unauthorised use, damp, direct sunlight, pests and excessive temperatures. Store away from sinks, cooking appliances, fuel and cleaning chemicals. Raise supplies above floors that might flood. Use a clean dry enclosure appropriate to the contents, without defeating ventilation or adding moisture. [R23]

A dark cellar may be damp; a buried cache may flood; an attic may overheat. "Cool" is a measured condition, not a feeling you had while carrying boxes. Use a thermometer, record significant excursions and organise the shelf so dates can be read without dismantling it. Keep manufacturer-supplied desiccants where directed and do not improvise contact between medicines and loose drying agents.

Keep three dates distinct: expiry, opening, and any permitted period after opening or preparation. Reconstituted liquids and opened products can have much shorter limits than unopened stock. Mark the opening time or date clearly. Do not pour old and new batches together to save a bottle.

## The fridge stops

Keep the door closed while finding out which products actually require refrigeration and what the interruption means for each one. Record the time, measured temperatures if available, and exposure history. Seek product-specific advice from a pharmacist, manufacturer or clinician. An insulated carrier helps only if its contents remain within the correct range; ice can freeze and damage medicines if used carelessly. [R25]

Insulin is especially important: storage depends on the particular product, container and whether it is in use. Some products permit a defined time at room temperature; that is not permission for indefinite warm storage. Avoid freezing and direct heat. Do not stop necessary insulin or substitute a different formulation on the basis of this book; obtain urgent professional advice and monitor according to the person's care plan. [R25]

An adrenaline auto-injector is not automatically a fridge item. NHS advice warns against leaving it too hot or cold, including in a fridge. Preserve its own instructions and check expiry. Treat all temperature limits as product-specific. The blanket rule "refrigerate anything important" is excellent for damaging some important things. [R26]

If power is available from a generator, run the generator safely outdoors. Temperature monitoring and a backup plan still matter; a connected extension lead does not prove a medicine refrigerator is maintaining its range.

## Expiry: evidence, not folklore

An expiry date marks the period for which the manufacturer supports quality under the labelled conditions. Poor storage can undermine a product before that date. An apparently normal tablet after expiry does not prove potency, purity or safety. The FDA advises consumers not to use expired medicines. [R23]

There is real research on extended shelf life. The US Shelf-Life Extension Program tests selected products in controlled federal stockpiles and can extend particular lots after stability assessment. It does not certify household stock or medicines recovered with an unknown history. The supplied 2024 antibiotic narrative review discusses possible extensions and important uncertainties; it is research discussion, not a dosing or scavenging protocol. [R24, R32]

In a true shortage, a qualified clinician or pharmacist may have to weigh specific risks and official emergency guidance. That is a product- and patient-specific decision. This book provides no universal extra number of safe years, no "all tablets are fine" rule and no visual test for degraded medicine. Separate uncertain stock and document what is known while seeking advice.

## Clean is not sterile

Cleaning removes dirt and material. Disinfection reduces specified microorganisms under defined conditions. Sterilisation requires a validated process capable of eliminating viable microorganisms, including resistant forms. Wiping a tool, boiling it in a saucepan or holding it over a flame does not establish a validated sterile instrument for an invasive procedure. [R29]

For ordinary first aid, prioritise hand hygiene, safe water and unopened single-use sterile dressings where indicated. Do not reuse needles, syringes or other single-use invasive equipment. Do not attempt home-made injectable fluids, compounded anaesthetics or surgical instruments processed by guesswork. A bottle of boiled water is useful water; it is not an intravenous product.

Follow the actual cleaning-product label for dilution, contact time and surface compatibility. Never mix bleach with acids, ammonia or other cleaning agents. Store cleaning chemicals separately and label every prepared solution. "Strong enough to smell impressive" is not a concentration.

## The five-line stock record

For each critical product, record identity and strength, quantity and lot, expiry/opening limits, storage requirements, and location/provenance. Add temperature incidents and decisions. Keep an easy route to move doubtful stock out of active use without losing its history. A person who notices a problem should be thanked, not encouraged to put it back quietly.

> FIELD RULE: You cannot store medical usefulness forever. You can preserve identity, packaging, appropriate conditions, records and a realistic replenishment plan.

**Sources:** R23-R26, R29, R32. No individual medicine's stability has been tested for this edition.
