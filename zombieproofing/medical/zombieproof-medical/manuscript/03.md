# Too Many Patients, One Nigel

Triage is what happens when the number of needs exceeds the help immediately available. It is not a contest in whose injury looks most suitable for the film trailer. It is a repeatable way to find urgent threats and organise care.

The supplied START references describe professional mass-casualty systems, including separate considerations for children. Those systems were designed for trained teams and particular response arrangements. This chapter explains the principles without turning a colour chart into permission to pronounce someone dead. [R30, R31]

## Begin outside the crowd

Check whether the incident is still happening. A collision can be followed by another collision. Smoke can drift. A structure can continue falling. Keep rescuers out of hazards they cannot control. Tell emergency services that there are multiple casualties and provide an estimated number, safe access and visible dangers. An estimate is better than silence; say when it is an estimate.

Choose an easily identified safe area for people able to move. Ask them to gather there, and appoint someone to stay with them. Being able to walk does not rule out serious injury, internal bleeding, smoke exposure or deterioration. Do not send the "walking wounded" away with congratulations and a biscuit-shaped diagnosis.

Allocate the first available helpers to obvious immediate needs: calling for help, severe bleeding, airway and breathing support, and guiding responders. Identify people who are unresponsive, struggling to breathe, bleeding heavily, or becoming confused. Follow dispatcher instructions and use first aid within your training. If somebody is not breathing normally, use the CPR guidance in Chapter 2; a lay colour assessment must not override it.

## Make one place remember

Give each patient a simple unique identifier and record where they are. If names are unknown, "Patient 03, blue coat" works until identity can be established. Keep the identifier consistent on notes and belongings. Do not attach anything tightly around the neck or an injured limb.

Record the time, main problem, care given and who is watching. Use a notebook even if a phone works. A dead battery should not erase the fact that someone received medicine or had a tourniquet applied. Personal details are for care, not for a public noticeboard. [R27, R30]

Keep family members and carers together where safe, especially children and people who rely on another person for communication or support. Write down where a person is moved and who accompanies them. Do not separate a child from their identifying information because the clipboard stayed on a table.

## Reassessment is the actual system

An initial impression is a snapshot, not a lifetime membership. Recheck people waiting for care. Notice breathing, responsiveness, bleeding, warmth and changes in pain. If a person is worsening, tell the coordinator and seek a higher level of help. A patient who becomes quiet may be tired, frightened or deteriorating; find out rather than celebrating improved manners.

Do not confuse immediate priority with personal worth. Familiarity, occupation, age alone, loudness and social status are poor substitutes for a trained clinical assessment. Explain delays honestly and reassure people that they remain under observation. A waiting area needs water, shelter, sanitation, information and somebody kind enough to repeat the same answer.

## A small incident you can organise

A shed roof collapses. One person is walking and talking, one has heavy leg bleeding, and one lies still. You stop anyone entering beneath the remaining unstable roof. A helper calls emergency services and describes three casualties. Another applies direct pressure to the bleeding. You assess the unresponsive person and follow the breathing/CPR pathway. The walking person waits somewhere safe with another helper, who watches for change and writes down names.

That is not the whole of disaster medicine. It is a useful beginning. A coloured tag cannot do any of those jobs without people, a safe space and repeated attention.

> FIELD RULE: Sort to organise help, then reassess. Never use this book's illustrations, fictional zombies or a single colour to decide that a real person should receive no care.

## Handing over without a speech

Give the receiving team the number of people, immediate threats, locations, identifiers, treatments and important times. Say what is uncertain. "Found unresponsive at 14:10; breathing was abnormal; CPR began immediately" is useful. "He was doing a sort of thing and then we became concerned" is less portable.

Take care of the helpers after the incident. Check for injuries, exposure, missing people and exhaustion. Replace used supplies, secure patient records and allow a brief factual review. People may feel upset later even if they seemed composed while working. There is no requirement to earn distress by having the most dramatic job.

**Sources:** R27, R30, R31. This is organisational guidance, not a substitute for a locally approved professional triage protocol.
