# How to Keep the Living Living

The apocalypse has excellent publicity and a disappointing understanding of hygiene. It promises a final battle, a spectacular vehicle and a meaningful coat. It seldom mentions that somebody will still need to clean the toilet, check a blood glucose meter, or get an injured person through the night without making them colder.

This volume begins with the person rather than the equipment. It explains the first useful actions, the small repeated jobs that support recovery, and the limits that remain when communications or transport fail. It is written for curious civilian readers and ordinary first aid, with a fictional disaster around the edges. The emergency numbers and main first-aid conventions are British; readers elsewhere should adapt contacts and follow their local guidance.

You can read it in order or use the contents and subject finder. The early chapters address immediate threats. The middle covers injuries, everyday illness and supplies. The later chapters address personal care plans, nursing, additional needs and distress. Chapter 17 contains short reminder cards and reusable record sheets. Keep the whole book with those cards: a reminder is useful because something fuller stands behind it.

## The pictures and the instructions

The illustrations are affectionate fiction. Their puzzled green inhabitants demonstrate the human fondness for doing something unnecessary with great confidence. They are decorative narrative scenes, not demonstrations of clinical techniques. A painted bandage, posture, thermometer or practice mannequin is not an instruction. Use the written guidance and proper practical training.

Humour belongs around the care, never at the patient's expense. The person in pain is not the punchline. Nigel, our recurring ambassador for preventable complications, is entirely invented and has been offered several opportunities to read the instructions.

## When the system is interrupted

Whenever professional help is available, use it. If it is delayed, keep doing the useful basics while arranging a safe route to it: protect breathing, control bleeding, reduce exposure, use safe water, observe change and preserve information. Never interpret a failed telephone call as a qualification for surgery.

The book deliberately does not provide invasive procedures, prescription drug regimens, home-made injectable products, a generic antibiotic selection chart or a timetable for fictional zombie infection. It does provide practical actions, decision limits and ways to preserve continuity. Those are things a reader can use without mistaking the chapter for an operating theatre.

Learn first aid while the world still offers courses. Print the care plan. Label the cupboard. Check the water. When the dramatic part arrives, you may discover that the most useful survivor is the one who has already done the boring part.
