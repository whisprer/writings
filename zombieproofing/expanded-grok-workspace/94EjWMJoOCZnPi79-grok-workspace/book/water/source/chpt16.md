Chapter 16: Wells, Lightning, and Other People's Jobs

Some water problems are real and still not yours to solve with a chapter.

A private well is a throat into the ground. In ordinary life it is tested, capped, and kept uphill and far from the septic tank, the fuel store, and the place you wash the tractor. After a flood, an earthquake, or a crack you did not order, it is suspect. Muddy water, a missing cap, a smell, or water that reached the wellhead are all reasons to stop drinking it. The pamphlet in the pile is short on heroics: drilled, driven, and bored wells are best disinfected by a well or pump contractor, because the owner cannot easily wash every surface the water will later touch. Sampling after any disinfection is the point, not the smell of bleach on day one. A boiled kettle from a fuel-soaked well is still a fuel-soaked well. Find another source until a test exists.

Shock chlorination recipes circulate widely, and some of them are in public pamphlets for shallow emergencies. They are easy to do wrong — too little and you grow confidence, too much and you have a hazardous waste in a hole, poured down a well that also connects to someone else's water. This edition will not print a "buckets of bleach per foot of casing" recipe. If a current local health sheet in your hand gives one, and you are the sort of well it applies to, that sheet outranks this book. If you are guessing, you are not disinfecting. You are seasoning.

## Lightning is an electrical trade

A metal roof, a full tank, a wet soil, and a storm are a serious neighbourhood. The long document in the research pile is a design project against a national lightning code, full of down-conductors, earth electrodes, and separation distances. That is the point of mentioning it. Protection that works is designed and bonded by people who do that work, to a code that is still a code when the lights are out. A cable you ran from the gutter to the cistern "so the lightning has somewhere to go" can be the path through the tap, the stand, and the person holding the cup.

In a storm, stay off the roof, stay out of the bath if the house is the tallest thing in a field and the storm is on you, and do not become the tallest wet object out of curiosity. Tanks can be bonded into a real system. They should not be bonded into a theory you had during the thunder.

Backflow testing, septic repair after a flood, and the question of whether a town main is poisoned are the same shape of problem. They have professions. A field manual can tell you to wait, to use the sealed water, to boil what boiling can save, and not to invent a joint between a fire main and a kitchen. It cannot sign the certificate.

Wave at the jobs that are not yours. Then go and put the lid back on the barrel, which is.
