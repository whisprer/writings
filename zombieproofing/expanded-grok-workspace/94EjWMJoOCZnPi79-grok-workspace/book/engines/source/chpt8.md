Chapter 8: Ethanol, Measured

Ethanol is not a personality. It is a fuel with less energy in each litre than petrol, a strong dislike of knocking, a habit of absorbing water, and a cold-start manner that can only be described as reluctant. It can be kinder to the burn in some engines. It can also eat the wrong rubber, the wrong metal, and your afternoon.

Because each litre carries less energy, the engine wants a richer flow of it. More liquid, to do the work the old liquid did. The guide this chapter leans on describes enlarging a carburettor's main jet by something like twenty to forty percent, and gives an example: a jet of about fifty-six thousandths of an inch opened toward about seventy-eight thousandths, a nominal forty percent increase in diameter. It also talks about advancing the timing toward roughly twenty-two degrees before top dead centre, and about heat for cold starts, and about whether the materials in the fuel path can stand the new diet.

Read that paragraph again, slowly, and then refuse to treat it as a spell.

Those numbers are a historical starting point for a particular setup. They are not a universal recipe. A jet does not flow "a diameter." It flows an area, under a pressure difference, with a fuel of a certain thickness and density, through a carburettor that has opinions of its own. Drill an irreplaceable jet because a book said forty percent and you have made a souvenir.

## The only honest order

1. Establish a baseline on the fuel the engine was built for. How it idles, how it pulls, what the plug looks like, what the oil looks like after a decent run.
2. Use jets you can swap, not holes you can only make larger. Larger is a one-way door.
3. Change one thing at a time. If you change the jet and the timing and the needle and your hopes, you will not know which one saved you or which one started the knock.
4. Watch exhaust temperature if you can, plug colour, oil condition, and the way the engine answers a load. A happy noise is not a measurement.
5. Stop at the first sign of detonation, overheating, a lean stumble under load, a leak, or a seal going soft. Pride is not a coolant.

Ethanol blends that have sat are especially good at collecting water and starting corrosion in tanks and carburettor bowls. If you are waking an old engine, assume the previous owner poured in whatever was cheap and then left. Treat the tank as guilty until it has been drained, inspected, and given fresh fuel of a kind you can name.

The plate has two jets, a pair of calipers, and a grape that was not on the parts list. The grape is the joke. The calipers are the chapter.
