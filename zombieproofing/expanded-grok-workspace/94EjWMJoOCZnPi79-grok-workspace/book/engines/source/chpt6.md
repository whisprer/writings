Chapter 6: Torque, Knock, and the Heat You Lose

Torque is a twist. Power is how fast you can keep twisting. The zombie spinning in the plate has torque for a moment and then has the ground. Speed is not a personality. It is a bill.

\[
P = \tau \omega
\]

Power \(P\) in watts, torque \(\tau\) in newton-metres, angular speed \(\omega\) in radians per second. For the sort of sums you do with a notebook and a suspicious tachometer:

\[
P_{kW} = \frac{T_{Nm} \times RPM}{9550}
\]

The same torque at twice the speed is twice the power, and also a sharper invitation to friction, heat, and parts leaving the chat. Piston speed is roughly:

\[
v_p = 2 \times stroke \times \frac{RPM}{60}
\]

Big engines use long strokes and heavy pieces, so piston speed and inertia talk them out of extreme revs. Small engines can spin, because the pistons are light and the stroke is short. Their little bearings still have to survive the party. Do not confuse "it can" with "it should, today, on old oil."

## Compression, which is a promise and a threat

\[
r = \frac{V_d + V_c}{V_c}
\]

\(V_d\) is the swept volume, the room the piston travels through. \(V_c\) is the clearance volume, the room left when the piston is as high as it goes. A higher ratio can buy efficiency and power. It also raises temperature and the chance of knock. Octane is a fuel's reluctance to knock in a spark engine. It is not a score for how much energy is in the tin. A high-octane fuel is not a stronger fuel. It is a more patient one.

## The ways a burn goes wrong

A useful mixture needs fuel, oxygen, enough temperature, and enough time. The failures have names, and the names are cheaper than the pistons:

- **Lean:** too much air or too little fuel. Overheating, misfire, exhaust valves that age in public.
- **Rich:** too much fuel. Waste, a sooty plug, carbon, a smell that stays in your coat.
- **Knock, or detonation:** the flame has started properly, and then the rest of the unburned charge lights by itself. Pressure waves hammer the piston and the bearings. It sounds like a handful of gravel in a tin, and it is not a sound to "drive through."
- **Pre-ignition:** the charge lights before the spark, because of a hot spot, glowing carbon, or a plug that has been living too hot. This is worse. The burn starts too early and the piston is still coming up the stairs.
- **Valve float:** the springs can no longer make the valves obey at that speed. Breathing falls apart. On the wrong engine, piston and valve meet.
- **Blow-by:** combustion gas sneaks past the rings into the crankcase, which is bad for oil and bad for power.

Do not "cure" knock by adding fuel, timing, and compression in a hopeful pile. Look at temperature, mixture, fuel, ignition timing, deposits, cooling, and the general health of the machine as one animal. It is one animal.

## The heat you do not get back

A small conventional petrol engine may turn something like a quarter to a third of the fuel's energy into shaft work when conditions are kind. Diesels can do better, especially held at a sensible load. The rest leaves as exhaust heat, coolant heat, friction, the work of pumping air, noise, and the tax of accessories. The notes under this book use about thirty percent as a rough petrol figure and forty to forty-five percent as a broad diesel benchmark. Treat both as rumours with good manners, not as the number stamped on your particular lump of iron.

Steam adds a furnace, a boiler, pipework, condensation, and every place heat can change its mind. Its gift is fuel that can be almost anything solid or cheap, and stationary work at a scale. Its gift is not compactness, and it is not forgiveness.
