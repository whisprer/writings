Chapter 10: The Engine That Has Been Plotting

An abandoned engine is not dead. It has had time. Time, in a sump, is a conspiracy of moisture, old fuel, and rings that have glued themselves to the bore out of loneliness. Treat it as a machine with a plan, not as a corpse you can surprise with a key.

```text
identify → inspect → lubricate → turn by hand → fresh fluids
→ clean the fuel path → test the electrics → prove the timing
→ test compression → first start, outdoors → watch → service again
```

Do not start at the right-hand end because the yard is noisy and you would like a victory. Victories that skip the left-hand end become connecting rods in unexpected shapes.

## Name it before you touch it

Find the engine number, the fuel it was built for, the firing order, the oil grade, the timing marks, the compression ratio if anyone wrote it down, the wiring, and the limits the maker published. Photograph every linkage and every hose before you take them off. Label the parts. Future-you is not psychic, and future-you will be tired, and tired people put the float back in upside down and then blame the horde.

## Make it safe enough to learn

Disconnect the battery. Remove anything that can spark while you are still in the fuel system. Look for nests, seized accessories, cracked castings, heavy corrosion, and liquid sitting in the cylinders. A cup of water in a bore is not a detail. It is a hydraulic lock waiting for someone brave and incorrect.

On a spark engine, take the plugs out. Put a small amount of a suitable penetrating or assembly oil into the bores. Give it a day or two — the notes say twenty-four to forty-eight hours, and a badly seized engine may want longer. Then turn the crank by hand, with a socket that fits, not with hope and a long bar you found in the grass. If it stops hard, you stop. You do not convert a stuck piston into a sculpture.

The restoration notes under this book name the catastrophic versions of ordinary neglect: dry rings, dry bearings, a valvetrain that has seized, and timing that is simply wrong. Those are not mysteries. They are what happens when an engine is asked to run before it has been introduced to oil and to the correct century of its own camshaft.

## Throw the old fluids out

Drain the stale petrol. Three to six months is the rough life the source material gives it before varnish becomes the story, and a decade in a shed is not a vintage. Replace the oil and the filter. Flush coolant that has become a science experiment. Replace fuel filters. Look at the sediment in the tank, and believe it. Ethanol blends that sat are suspects for water and corrosion until proven otherwise.

The mouse in the plate is not your enemy. The mouse moved out. The rust stayed, and the rust has been patient, which is more than most of us manage. Photograph the linkages. Then, and only then, think about whether this engine deserves a bath, a spark, and a first start in the open air. That is the next chapter. It is not a shortcut back to this one.
