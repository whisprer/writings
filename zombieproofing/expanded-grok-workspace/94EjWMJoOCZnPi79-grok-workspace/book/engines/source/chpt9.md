Chapter 9: Wood Gas and the Guest You Cannot See

Gasification is controlled incomplete burning. You are not making a cheerful campfire. You are making a gas that an engine can breathe, and the gas does not love you. It can be rich in carbon monoxide. It can become explosive if air leaks in where the mixture is wrong. It can carry tar that turns an engine into a sticky relic. Do this in your head, on paper, or in a yard that is not attached to a bedroom. Do not begin with a live producer-gas system because the chapter made it sound knowable. Knowable is not the same as safe, and safe is not the same as indoors.

A simplified path, which hides a dozen ways to get it wrong:

```text
biomass → drying → pyrolysis → oxidation → reduction
        → hot producer gas → cooling → cyclone → filter
        → a measured mix of air → the engine
```

The material under this book sketches a producer gas somewhere near twenty percent hydrogen, twenty percent carbon monoxide, fifty to sixty percent nitrogen, and a little methane. Nitrogen is ballast. It takes up room that petrol would have filled with something keener. Power falls. The Simple-Fire notes talk about something like thirty percent less power than the same engine on petrol. Gas quality and the honesty of your filtering decide whether "less" means "usable" or "a long walk home."

## Principles that are not optional

- The fuel must be dry, and of a consistent size. Wet wood is a steam lecture, not a gas supply.
- Tar and water have to come out before the engine sees them. A filter is not a wish.
- Filters need to be drained and looked at. A forgotten drain is a small lake of things you did not want in a cylinder.
- The gas path must be tight where a leak would let the combustible mixture meet a surprise, and it must not be a sealed bomb. Relief behaviour is a design, not a hope that the lid is loose.
- A flare test shows that something will burn in the open. It is not a certificate. Certificates are duller and harder to get, which is why they exist.
- Outdoors. Upwind of the people. A carbon-monoxide alarm that you believe, and a way to walk away.

An illustrative table in the source material ties fire-tube diameter, very roughly, to engine power: on the order of two inches for five horsepower, out toward fourteen inches for a hundred and sixty. These are empirical sketches from a particular tradition, not a design standard you can take to a fabricator and call finished. The same notes put a Simple-Fire device in a band of about two to twenty horsepower, and call it experimental. Experimental means you are not the customer. You are the test.

The plate puts the drums outside, a small flame where it can be seen and left, the survivor upwind, and two neighbours downwind looking woozy at a wisp they cannot actually see. That wisp is the joke and the warning. If you cannot see the hazard, you do not get to stand in it.

If you want the educational version, model the mass, the moisture, the filter, and the power loss on paper or in code. Chapter 16 will point you at that kind of project and then refuse, again, to hand you a weekend gasifier. The refusal is the safety equipment.
