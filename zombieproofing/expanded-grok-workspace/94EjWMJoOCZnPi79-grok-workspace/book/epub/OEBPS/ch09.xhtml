Chapter 9

Bleach You Can Name

Sometimes the fuel is gone and the germs are not. Then you want a disinfectant you can point at, which means unscented household chlorine bleach, not the lavender one, not the "splashless" one, not a bottle that boasts of being gentle.

The card in the source pile, the one meant to be taped inside a cupboard, says this for a bottle in the five-to-nine percent range: eight drops, or a little less than an eighth of a teaspoon, in one gallon of clear water. If the water is cloudy, sixteen drops, or a quarter teaspoon — but you should have settled it first, so that you are not spending the dose on mud. Stir. Wait thirty minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine. If it does not smell at all, the dose was eaten by dirt or by an old, tired bottle; repeat it and wait again. If it smells like a swimming pool's angry cousin, pour it between clean jugs and let it stand open to air until it is only a hint.

An older table, still in the pile, splits the dose by percent, and it is worth knowing because bottles wander:

Around four to six percent: about two drops per quart, or eight per gallon, which is the same neighbourhood as the card. Around one percent: much more, on the order of ten drops per litre. Around ten percent: less, on the order of one drop per quart. If you truly cannot read a percent, the old note says ten drops per quart or litre of water you have already filtered and settled, then the smell test. Doubling for cold, cloudy, or coloured water shows up in both traditions. Cold means roughly below ten degrees, when the chemistry dawdles.

These numbers are indicative. The label on the bottle and the health department currently in charge of your county outrank a book. Some modern "concentrated" bleaches sit near eight percent; the five-to-nine card was written to cover that band with one instruction. If your label prints its own water recipe, use the label.

What chlorine will not do

Chlorine, given time, is excellent against a great many bacteria and viruses. It is a poor oath against Cryptosporidium. That parasite is why the kettle still outranks the bottle when you can light a fire, and why a filter that is actually rated for cysts still matters if chlorine is your only tool and the source is a ditch. Chlorine also does nothing useful about lead, fuel, or pesticides. A faint chlorine smell means you have residual disinfectant, not that you have removed a solvent.

Bleach itself gets old. Heat and light spend it. A bottle that has sat in a shed for a year may be a ghost of the percent on the label. Keep it cool, dark, and capped, and do not trust a half-remembered jug the way you trust a new one.

Dry calcium hypochlorite — pool shock, high-test hypochlorite, the granules that last for years if they stay dry and cool — is a stockpile chemical, not a pinch. The old recipe makes a stock: about one heaping teaspoon, around seven grams, stirred into two gallons of clean water, giving a solution near five hundred milligrams per litre. You then add that stock at about one part to a hundred parts of the water you are treating: a pint of stock in about twelve and a half gallons, or the same ratio in whatever vessels you actually own. Mix, wait thirty minutes, look for the faint smell. Never toss the granules into the drinking water neat. Never store the granules next to fuel, oil, or a cloth that wants to become a fire. It is an oxidiser. It has hobbies.

Iodine, if it is all you have, is the spare the cards allow and the one this book likes least. Five drops of two-percent tincture per quart or litre of clear water, ten if cloudy, thirty minutes, is the figure in the notes. Tablets follow their packet and not your memory. Do not make iodine a daily habit, and do not hand it to a pregnancy or a thyroid as if it were salt. Taste lingers. So can trouble.

Chlorine dioxide tablets, if you bought them for this exact job, are used as the packet says. The packet is the dose. A blog is not.

Thirty minutes is part of the chemistry. Drinking it at minute two is how you meet the germs socially before the introduction is finished.
