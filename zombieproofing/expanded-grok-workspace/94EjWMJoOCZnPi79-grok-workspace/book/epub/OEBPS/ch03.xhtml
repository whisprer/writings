Chapter 3

The Roof That Pays Rent

Rain is the most polite large source you are likely to meet. It arrives already fallen, it does not send a bill, and it has not, usually, spent a decade in a steel pipe making friends with bacteria. It has, however, met your roof, and your roof has a biography.

The yield people actually use is a small piece of arithmetic, and it is indicative, not a prophecy:

gallons, roughly, equal 0.623 times the roof's footprint in square feet, times the rainfall in inches, times a runoff coefficient.

The 0.623 is the dull conversion from a square foot of one inch of rain into U.S. gallons. A thousand square feet and one inch, if every drop behaved, is about six hundred and twenty gallons. It will not. Wind, splash, and the first wash you ought to throw away steal some. Real systems often land around three quarters to five sixths of the theory. Metric cousins exist; if you think in litres and millimetres, use a local sheet and do not convert twice.

The coefficient is the roof's honesty. A smooth pre-painted metal roof is the good pupil, often around nine tenths, and it sheds less grit. Asphalt shingles are worse company for drinking water: they hold back more, and they can lend the wash heavy metals, petroleum crumbs, and grit. Clay and concrete tile drink a little of the rain themselves and grow moss when the mood takes them. If you can choose a surface for a drinking cistern, choose metal, and still treat the water. "Better roof" is not "sterile roof."

The first wash is not a guest

A dry roof is a plate. Dust, soot, leaves, and the digestive opinions of birds sit on it until the rain edits them into your gutter. That first water is the dirtiest water the storm will offer. Letting it into the cistern is how you grow a biology experiment and then ask a teaspoon of bleach to fight a mud pie.

A leaf screen or a rain head takes the twigs and the tennis balls. A first-flush diverter takes the filthy volume itself. The usual picture is a standpipe that fills with the opening wash until a ball floats up and seats, and the cleaner rain goes on to the tank. A small bleed hole at the bottom empties the pipe over a day or two, so the next storm finds it ready. If the bleed is clogged, you do not have a diverter. You have a statue.

How much to divert depends on how rude the roof is. Open country, little canopy: on the order of one to five gallons per thousand square feet. Trees, birds, a city that sheds soot: more like five to ten gallons per thousand square feet. The research notes also give a pipe's appetite, so you can build the volume on purpose: about a quarter gallon per foot of three-inch pipe, half a gallon per foot of four-inch, and a bit under one and a half gallons per foot of six-inch. These are workshop numbers. Measure your actual pipe if the civilisation of measuring is still available. Do not drink the diverter. It is doing a job by being discarded.

What the barrel is not

A rain barrel for the garden can be a charming mess. A cistern for mouths wants a lid, a screen on every opening a mosquito could consider a nursery, an opaque wall or a jacket against the light, and food-grade materials. Light grows algae. Algae are not a seasoning. The inlet should not dump onto the outlet, and the outlet should not sit in the sludge at the bottom. A first-flush that you never empty is just a smell with plumbing.

Roof water is soft and useful and still untreated. Bird lime does not become safe because it fell from a height. Chapter 8 and chapter 9 are not optional for drinking. The roof pays the rent. You still boil the tea.
