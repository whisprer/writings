Afterword

The last chapter of a water book is a temptation to get solemn about civilisation. Resist it a little. The yard is not a chapel. It is a patch of ground you share with whoever needs a drink, a pot washed, or a garden persuaded through a dry week, and the courtesy that keeps it usable is older than any of us: know where it came from, treat it before you trust it, and leave a way to tell the next person that you did.

If you have read this far you already know the unglamorous truth. Clear is not the same as safe. Boiling is a kindness to germs' enemies and a uselessness against solvents. The question here is not how rustic the barrel can look. It is whether the next person can pour a cup, explain what was done to it, and put the lid back without becoming the incident. The person who brings unscented bleach with the percent still readable will be remembered more kindly than the person who brings a speech.

The zombies, if you still need them, are only the name we gave the interruption. A skipped boil is one. A fuel can reused as a jug is one. Panic that drinks the first wash off the roof and walks away is one. You do not beat them by adding gadgets. You beat them by being able to say, on paper, what the cup is supposed to be when the morning comes back.

Leave a note on the barrel. Tell someone else the date, the method, and where the lid lives. When you are done for the night, say so, and cover it on purpose, so nobody sits up listening to a drip out of loyalty.

The signature under an afterword belongs to the person who stands by the book. That line is blank until you want it.

Boil it. Then quiet.
