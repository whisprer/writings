Foreword

Someone always asks, at the bad moment, whether it is safe. They mean the tap. They mean the barrel. They mean the puddle the street has offered, which is not an offer.

This manual is an answer with a lid on it. It begins where water actually begins after a failure, which is not with a clever filter but with a decision not to be poisoned by the thing you were trying not to die of thirst over. Then it says where water comes from when the mains have gone quiet: a roof, a foggy hill, a pipe that has been stagnant for years and should be treated as a rumour, and the weather, which lies.

You will find boiling told slowly, because it is still the method that does not require you to guess the strength of a bottle. You will find bleach, because sometimes the kettle is a luxury, and the bottle must be the one you can name. You will find slow sand, pocket filters, and storage, each with the thing it does not do written in the same size as the thing it does. Later chapters wander into gravity, clay pots for gardens, and hot water, and then stop, on purpose, before they become a plumbing licence.

The zombies are a convenience. They stand in for hurry. A boil-water notice nobody read, a roof wash drunk because it looked clear, a person who wants the barrel to be a personality instead of a container. If you are reading this while the world is, against the odds, still selling unscented bleach, the zombies will not mind. The germs are the same on a Tuesday.

A word about the pages that mention doses, wells, and heat. Curiosity is not a licence. Do not drink fire-sprinkler water because a chapter explained why it is filthy. Do not build a wood-stove coil because a chapter explained why the tank has to sit above the fire. Do not pour a sack of pool chemicals into a well because the grid is having a mood. The jokes are jokes. A water main can fail and the rules about infants, pregnancy, and chemical contamination can still be standing. Cryptosporidium does not care that you labelled the jug.

What I hope you keep is not a single gadget. It is the habit of a cup you understand: where it came from, what you did to the living things in it, what you could not do to the metals and the solvents, and how you stored it so tomorrow's cup is not a fresh argument. Water is poor company and an excellent servant. It does not replace the person who treated it. It only makes it possible for the morning to include a drink.

Leave your name off the title page until you want it there. The kettle does not require a byline. It requires a minute, a little discipline, and somebody who did not skip the page.
