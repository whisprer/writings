Chapter 6

After the Weather

Weather does not only take the roof. It takes the meaning of the tap.

After a flood, the water in the street has been a mixture of sewage, soil, fuel, and whatever the upstream shed was storing. It is not a source. It is a reason to wash your hands with water you already trust, and to stay out of it if you can. Homes that have been under water are a different hazard again: electricity that may still be lively, mould, and the quiet damage to a private septic tank or a well. A flooded well should be assumed contaminated. Shock-chlorinating a drilled, driven, or bored well is a contractor's job in the EPA's own pamphlet, because the owner usually cannot get the disinfectant everywhere it has to go. Do not drink it until someone tests it. Do not "just boil a little" from a well that has also met a fuel tank. Boiling is not a spell against petrol.

Hurricanes and tropical storms knock plants offline and push seawater and debris into intakes. The honest posture is the one the health cards repeat until it sounds like a proverb: use sealed bottled water if you have it; otherwise boil, or disinfect with a method you can name; and obey the local notice if a local notice exists. A tap that runs clear after a storm has not been interviewed. Clarity is still a compliment.

Wildfire is sneakier. Ash, fire retardant, and the burned contents of a shed can land on roofs and in open tanks. A cistern without a lid is a bowl. If the fire came through, assume the roof wash is chemical until you have a reason not to, and do not let the first rains after a burn go straight into the drinking tank. Empty the diverter. If the tank itself was open to the ash, do not try to bleach the ash into politeness.

Earthquakes break pipes and mix the potable main with the rest of the ground. Tsunamis are salt, sewage, and ruin; the sea is not a cistern you can filter into a cup. Tornadoes throw the container across the county and leave you with whatever sealed bottles survived the cupboard. In every one of these, the pattern is the same. The infrastructure's promise has paused. Your nose is not a laboratory. Fuel-smell, chemical-smell, and "it came out of the flood" are all stops, not puzzles.

Septic tanks do not become charming

If dirty water has washed over a septic tank, a soakaway, or the lid of either, keep people and animals off it, and do not flush your way back to normal. A system that has been under water can push sewage the wrong direction. That is a plumber and a public-health problem. It is also a reason not to drink from a shallow well downhill of it, no matter how rustic and honest the well looks in the evening light.

The zombie wades. You do not. When the weather has had an opinion, the cup you want is the one you sealed before the opinion started.
