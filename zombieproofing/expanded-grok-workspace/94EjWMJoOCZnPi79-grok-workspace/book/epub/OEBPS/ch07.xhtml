Chapter 7

Clear It Before You Trust It

Disinfection is a conversation with living things. Dirt interrupts.

Cloudy water hides microbes inside particles and eats chlorine before the chlorine can do its job. The public cards all say the same dull thing first: if it is cloudy, let it settle, or pour it through a clean cloth, a coffee filter, or a proper prefilter, and draw off the clearer water for the boil or the dose. Settling is not purification. It is manners. An hour in a clean bucket, the sludge left behind, the clearer top poured off gently, is already a different liquid from the one you dipped out of a ditch in a panic.

A cloth has holes the size of gossip. It will stop twigs, some grit, and the more theatrical insects. It will not stop bacteria, viruses, or cysts. If you tell yourself the T-shirt was the treatment, you have joined the zombie, who is currently wringing the same T-shirt into its mouth.

What you are allowed to call "clear"

Clear enough to treat means you can see through it in a glass, more or less, and it is not the colour of tea made from a road. It does not mean potable. After you settle, you still boil or you still dose, unless the water was already from a source you trust. Colour from leaves is not the same as colour from a factory, and your nose gets a vote: fuel, solvent, a sharp chemical edge, and you stop. No cloth fixes that. You find another source.

Keep the settling bucket food-grade and boring. Rinse the cloth with treated water if you have any, and do not use the cloth that wiped the sump. Label the "dirty" bucket and the "clear" bucket so a helpful person cannot merge them in an act of hospitality.

If you have a proper sediment filter, of the sort sold to go in front of a drinking system, use it as the manufacturer says, and replace it when it clogs rather than when it becomes a pet. A clogged filter is a bypass waiting for an impatient person.

The schmutzdecke and the pocket hollow-fibre come later. They are finer tools. This chapter is only the refusal to dose a mud puddle and call it chemistry.

Let it sit. Pour off the top. Then, and only then, introduce it to the kettle.
