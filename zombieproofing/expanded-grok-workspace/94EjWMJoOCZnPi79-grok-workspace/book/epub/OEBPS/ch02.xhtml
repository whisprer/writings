Chapter 2

How Much, and From Where

Thirst is a poor planner. It arrives late, speaks loudly, and will try to promote the nearest puddle.

A person at rest in mild weather needs on the order of two and a half to four litres of drinking water a day, more if they are working, more if the day is hot, more if they are ill. That is the cup you argue about. It is not the whole bill. Cooking, washing a wound, washing hands, and not living in your own smell all ask for more, and a garden asks for a shocking amount if you water it like rain that has forgotten how. The survival number and the civilisation number are different. Write both down when you are calm, in litres, not in vibes.

The order of trust, when you still have a choice, is dull and correct.

Sealed water you packed before the trouble. Then water you treated yourself by a method you can explain. Then water you are only using to wash things that are not mouths. Last, and preferably never, water that has been industrial: flood water, sprinkler mains, a sump, a ditch that has met a road.

What "safe" is allowed to mean

Safe, in this book, means safe from the living hazards you can actually do something about: the bacteria, the viruses, and the parasites that boiling, a proper chlorine dose, or a filter rated for them can address. It does not mean safe from lead, fuel, pesticides, or a fire system's antifreeze. Those require you not to use that source, or to use a treatment this book will not pretend you can build on a Saturday. If you cannot say which of those two sentences you are in, you are in the first one. Do not drink it.

Surface water — rivers, ponds, the dramatic puddle — should be assumed to contain faeces, because the world is full of animals and people and both of them are honest about that. Roof water should be assumed to contain the roof: dust, soot, bird lime, and whatever the shingles are made of. Fog water should be assumed to be a small, proud trickle that still needs the same manners as a roof. Tap water, after a flood, a hurricane, a wildfire near the intake, or a plant that has lost power, should be assumed to be a rumour until someone whose job it is says otherwise.

A small map

When you look at a place, look for water in this order, and write it down so the thirsty version of you cannot innovate.

The bottles and jugs already in the house. The hot-water cylinder, if you can drain it cleanly and you are sure it was potable when it filled — it is a tank, not a miracle, and it may be rusty or hot enough to scald. The rain barrel, if you built it for drinking and you are willing to treat it. The roof you could collect from, if you have a clean container and a way to throw away the first wash. A foggy ridge, if you live on one and you have mesh, and you have made peace with modest yields. A neighbour's treated store, if the neighbour is still a neighbour.

Not on the map: the flood in the street, the sprinkler riser, the chemical store, the ornamental pond that has been a bird's entire civilisation, the water from a radiator.

A zombie will drink from the puddle. That is how you know it is a zombie, or in a hurry, which for the purposes of this chapter is the same profession.

The rest of the book is the methods. This chapter is only the decision to have a method before you have a mouthful.
