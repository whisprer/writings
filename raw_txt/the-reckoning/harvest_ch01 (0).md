# Untitled

Mountain View, California

---

## Chapter 1

The tarp had come loose again in the night.

Mira saw it from the sink, through the glass that never quite cleaned — a hard white California morning already on the backyard, the three framed walls, the slab the colour of old gum. The blue poly snapped and settled, snapped and settled, like something trying to get up and being talked out of it. Rebar rusted in the sun. A city sticker on the fence had faded to the same pink as everything else they mailed her and she did not read.

She ran the tap until it was cold and did not drink.

By the door: his shoes. The left one still crushed down at the heel because he never used the back of it. She had not moved them. She had not moved the other pair either, the ones that belonged to a man she did not say, lined up for a year and a half in the same place until they stopped being an insult and became furniture. These new ones had not had time to become furniture. She looked at them only as long as you look at a pan left on the stove. Then she looked at the coffee.

Mountain View did what it always did. A leaf blower two yards over. A car door. Someone’s shuttle idling on the next street with the patience of a machine that did not live here. The hills were out. The air smelled faintly of warm pavement and whatever they were frying on Castro already. It was a decent day. That was the part she could not forgive on a schedule.

She put bread down and did not eat it. She opened the laptop because the district did not pause for weather, or for glass, or for a backyard that had been a later and was now a rectangle of dirt with a tarp for a mouth. A calendar invite sat on the screen with her name spelled right. She accepted it.

When she passed the door again she stepped around the shoes. Not over. Around. The way you walked around a person who had fallen asleep on a floor you still needed.

She did not say his name. She had practice.

Outside, the tarp found a gust and lifted enough to show the dark underneath — the unused room, the unpoured rest of it — then dropped, as if embarrassed. Mira left the crust in the sink and took her keys. The house, behind her, kept all of its extra shapes. It was good at that. It had been training for years.

The Civic started on the second try. It had been starting on the second try since February and she had not taken it in. There was a list like that. The porch light. The filter. The form in the envelope under the fruit bowl. She reversed out past the neighbour’s bins and did not look at the yard.

Whisman to the yard office was nine minutes if the light on Central held. It held. She sat in the tick of the indicator anyway, both hands on the wheel, the same three songs in the same order because she had not touched the playlist since the week she stopped touching things that required a decision. The second song had a drum fill she now experienced as a kind of weather. When it came she swallowed. When it passed she was still at the light.

The yard smelled of dust and cut grass and the particular metal of school district summer, even though it was not summer. Portables on a truck. A man she had worked beside for eleven years lifted two fingers off a clipboard without coming over. She lifted two fingers back. That was the whole conversation and it was the correct one.

Inside, fluorescent. The coffee in the break room had been sitting since before she left the house. She poured it anyway. Her inbox was the same inbox. Work orders. A portable at Bubb that needed a latch. A complaint about a gate at Landels that did not close unless you lifted it, which everyone who used that gate already knew. She assigned the latch. She left the gate. She could still do this part. The doing was not a sign of anything. It was the last ritual that did not ask her to talk.

At ten past ten Helen from the preschool office came down with a manila folder and a look she was trying not to wear.

“You eating?” Helen said.

“I ate.”

Helen put the folder on the corner of the desk and did not take her hand off it. She was sixty-something and kept the snack closet like a person keeping a border. She had buried a husband and a sister and did not do speeches. That was why Mira could stand her in a doorway.

“List for spring,” Helen said. “Baby room’s thin. I’m not asking you to fix it.”

“I’m facilities.”

“I know what you are.”

Helen took her hand off the folder. She looked, once, at Mira’s hair, which had been a thing Mira used to have done on Thursdays and was now a thing that happened or did not. She did not mention the hair. She mentioned the latch at Bubb instead, which was already assigned, and then she left, and the air she left behind was the air of a person who had clocked a building with one light on and had decided not to ring the bell.

Mira opened the folder because it was on the desk. Names. Ages in months. A column for siblings. The list was shorter than last year’s list. She closed it. She put it in the out tray so it would become Helen’s again without passing through any sentence of hers.

The portable latch took until lunch. Lunch was a protein bar from the glove box, eaten in the Civic with the engine off and the same second song started over because the playlist looped and she let it. She watched a woman walk a dog along the fence line of the corporation yard and did not know the woman. Mountain View was full of women walking dogs along fence lines. The town kept its manners. It kept its light. It kept producing Tuesdays.

In the afternoon a custodian called about a smell in a boys’ bathroom at Castro and she drove over because driving was a shape her body still remembered. The bathroom was a bathroom. She wrote *trap* on the work order and stood for a minute in the corridor while a class went past, all knees and noise, and the noise went through her without catching. She had known, once, how to stand in a school corridor and feel the fact of children as a kind of weather she lived in. Now it was just sound. She walked back out into the glare and put on her sunglasses and sat in the Civic until the heat made the steering wheel too sharp, and then she drove.

At four-thirty she shut the laptop. She said goodbye to the man with the clipboard by lifting the same two fingers. She did not stop on Castro. She did not need milk. She had milk. She had everything you needed to not die in a night.

The house was as she had left it. Shoes. Glass. Tarp. The bread on the counter had gone rigid. She put it in the bin and washed the plate from yesterday and poured wine because the wine was how the evening became a place you could stand in. Not a lot, at first. Enough to take the edge off the fact of the rooms. She turned the television on and sat in the chair that faced it and did not follow what the people on the screen were upset about. Their mouths moved. A commercial played a song that was not one of her three and she waited it out with her face still. When her own second song started in her head anyway she let it.

The wine went down to a level she would not have admitted to anyone who still asked. No one still asked.

At some point the light in the yard went the colour of a bruise and the tarp stopped snapping. She did not go out to tie it. She had not gone out to tie it in three winds. The neighbour’s motion light came on and went off. A plane into San Jose drew a line she did not watch to the end.

She cried at a thing on the television that was not sad. A man opening a car door. That was all. She cried the way you paid something you owed in cash so there would be no record. When it was done she rinsed the glass and set it upside down and did not look at the door.

In bed she lay on her side of a mattress that had been a negotiation and was now just a mattress. The other side had been empty long enough that the emptiness was not news. The news was the second emptiness, which had not had time to become furniture, which still sat by the door in the shape of shoes.

She did not sleep so much as wait in the dark with her eyes closed. The third song played itself without the speakers. She knew every bar. She had not chosen it. Choice had been one of the first things to go, after speech, after the invitations she stopped answering, after the person she had already stopped naming before there was a second person not to name.

Outside, Mountain View made its small night noises. Someone laughed on the next street, brief, not her business. The tarp lifted once, weakly, and dropped.

In the morning she would accept the calendar again. She would step around the shoes. She would run the tap and not drink. The town would produce another decent day, and she would go into it like a person clocking in, and anyone looking — Helen, the man with the clipboard, the woman with the dog if the woman had looked — would see what there was to see: a life reduced to the errands that kept a body moving through a place that had not stopped.

She did not call it anything.

The house, which was good at extra shapes, kept them all.

## Chapter 2

The shoes were already wrong then.

Not furniture. Not a shrine. Just the way he came in — heel crushed, bag dropped, fridge open and not closed. Mira came home from the yard on a Thursday and they were there, which meant he was there, which meant the house still had a direction.

“You’re early,” she said.

“Class got cancelled. Some guy’s sick.” He was in the doorway of the kitchen eating cold rice from the carton with a fork from the drawer that stuck. Nineteen and a half. Foothill in the mornings, a shift at the place on El Camino that sold phones and cases and the particular boredom of other people’s upgrades. He still had the look of a person who had not decided if he was staying in this town or only failing to leave it.

“Use a plate.”

He used the carton. She did not fight him. Fighting required a kind of future she was already rationing.

The tarp in those days was newer. Still blue the way they sold it, not faded to that cheap sea colour. The slab had been poured in November, before the papers were finished, when she was still able to sign for concrete as if concrete were a sentence you could aim. Three walls had gone up in a weekend because the contractor had a gap and she had a cheque that was half spite. Then the gap closed. Then the cheque was just money. Then she stopped answering the contractor. The tarp went on so the city would not write her another letter, and the letters came anyway.

Eli stood at the glass with the carton.

“You going to finish it?”

“When I finish it.”

“That’s not a time.”

“It’s a time.”

He made a noise that was almost a laugh and was also the sound of a kid who had learned, in the last year and a half, which rooms in his mother were closed. He did not ask about the other pair of shoes. He had asked once, in October, and she had looked at him as if he had put his hand on a burner, and he had not asked again. He stepped around those shoes the same way she did. Training was contagious.

Dinner was whatever was in the fridge that could be heated without becoming a project. They ate at the counter because the table still felt like a place a second adult might sit down. The television was on. He watched it. She watched the side of his face, which was a habit she would have denied. He had her mouth. He had the other person’s hands. She did not think the word for the other person. She thought *hands* and moved the salt.

“You going out?” she said.

“Nah.”

He used to go out. Not a lot. Enough that the house had hours in it that were only hers and were not yet a problem. Since the winter he had started staying in more, which she told herself was money and units and the job, and which was also the way a barely adult person orbits a parent who has already sunk, without knowing that is what he is doing. He put his feet on the rung of the stool. The left shoe, when he toed it off later, would sit crushed at the heel.

She poured wine because Thursday was a day that ended. He looked at the glass and did not comment. He had commented in January. She had said *it’s just a glass* in a voice that ended the subject. He was learning the map of her reductions: the playlist that never changed in the car, the invitations she let die on the phone, the way she could work a full day and then sit in the chair and not hear the programme. He did not have a name for it. He had a mother who still packed him a thing that counted as lunch if he forgot, who still asked *class* and *shift* and *you going out*, who still existed in the specific direction of him. That was the field continuing. He did not know he was the field.

At nine he was in his room with the door half open and the same game in the headphones. She washed the carton because he would not. She tied the tarp because in those days she still went into the yard. The concrete was cold through her socks. The three walls made a room that was not a room. She stood in it and tried to see a desk, a bed, a later in which he came back from the first attempt at leaving and this was the place that meant she had not lost both things. The picture would not stay. She went back in and locked the glass.

He came out for water and found her at the sink.

“You okay.”

Not a question that wanted an answer. A knock on a door he knew was shut.

“Go to bed.”

“I am.”

He drank from the tap. He left the shoes where they were. In the morning she would step around them on the way to the Civic and the light on Central would hold or it would not, and Helen would come down with some folder, and the baby list would still be a normal length, and Mountain View would produce a decent day that she could almost use because he would text at lunch *u want anything from that place* and she would write *no* and mean *come home*.

She did not know that this was the year the season would get a story.

She knew the house had extra shapes. She knew one of them still walked through the door and crushed the heel. She knew she could not afford another empty pair.

She went to bed on her side of the mattress and listened to him move in the other room — chair, tap, the particular thud of a bag off a bed — and she held onto the sound the way you hold a rail on a train you have not admitted is moving.

When she slept, she slept. That was still allowed. The shoes by the door were only shoes. The tarp was only a tarp. The later was still, barely, a place a person could point.

## Chapter 3

Friday he was still in the kitchen when she left.

That was the wrong shape of a morning. Usually the house spat him out later, headphones on, the crushed heel already walking away from her. Today he was on the stool in a work shirt that had not been work-slept in, scrolling, eating cereal from a mug because the bowls were in the sink and neither of them had done the bowls.

“You’re up.”

“Shift at ten. Class is dead again. I’m just going in.”

“They cancel two days?”

“I guess. That guy’s still out or whatever. They put it in the thing.” He turned the mug and did not show her the thing. “Can you drop me? Caltrain’s being stupid.”

There it was. The whole of it. A cancelled lecture as an excuse to sit in her Civic and not take the train. She said yes because saying no was a conversation and she did not have conversations before coffee.

They did the bowls anyway, badly, because she could not leave the sink like that with him watching. He dried one and put it in the wrong cupboard. She left it there.

In the car the playlist started at the beginning, as it always did. He reached for the knob.

“Don’t.”

He took his hand back. He had learned that one too. They sat in the tick of the indicator at Central and he watched a girl on the corner with a backpack and did not wave. Mira watched the light. The second song’s drum fill arrived. She swallowed. He pretended the window was interesting.

“You can change it after I drop you.”

“It’s fine.”

It was not fine. It was the rail. He sat on his hands.

El Camino was already the usual grind — glass, signs, a man selling strawberries from a truck that had been selling strawberries from a truck since Eli was in a car seat. The phone place sat in a strip with a nail salon and a vape shop and a dentist whose banner had faded to the same pink as her city stickers. She pulled into the loading spot that was not a loading spot.

“You want me to pick you up.”

“I’ll get the train.”

“You just said the train was stupid.”

“It’ll be less stupid at six.” He had the bag on his knees. He looked at the shop and not at her. “They’re doing some sale. It’s going to be annoying.”

“Eat something that isn’t a mug.”

“Okay.”

“Eli.”

“What.”

She had not planned a *what*. The name had come out because he was about to close the door and the hours after that would be the house without a direction again, which she could do, which she did every day, which was still worse when he had been on the stool first. He waited with the patience of a person who had been *Eli*’d and knew a sentence might follow.

“Text when you’re off.”

“I always text.”

He did not always text. He texted enough that she could call it always. He shut the door with his hip. The crushed heel hit the pavement and he went into the fluorescent mouth of the shop without looking back, which was not cruelty. It was twenty years old and a shift at ten.

She sat in the loading spot until a car behind her made the noise cars make. She pulled out. She did not go to the yard yet. She drove one extra block and sat in the lot of the place that sold bulk paper and pens, engine off, the second song starting over because she had not touched the knob either, and she thought about the form under the fruit bowl — units for spring, a date, a signature that said he would still be a person who needed a school in this town. She had not signed it. He had not asked her to sign it this week. The cancelled class had given him a Friday morning in her kitchen and a ride and nothing else. That was the size of the news.

At the yard the man with the clipboard lifted two fingers. She lifted two fingers. Helen did not come down. The latch at Bubb was done. The gate at Landels still needed lifting. Mira assigned a hinge she could have assigned last month and ate the glove-box bar in the Civic at noon and did not write *u want anything* first, because he was in the shop, and at 12:17 he wrote *they have that sandwich thing next door* and she wrote *no* and meant *I dropped you. That was the anything.*

After work she went home to the shoes that were not there. The other pair was there. She stepped around those. She poured the wine earlier than Thursday because Friday was allowed to be earlier and because the house without the crushed heel was a different quiet, an older quiet, the one she had already learned and had not agreed to learn twice.

He texted at 6:41. *train fine. u want thai*

*No,* she wrote.

*ok,* he wrote, and then, a minute later, *getting the thing with the peanuts anyway.*

She put a plate out. She did not put two glasses out. When he came in he smelled like the shop and the street and peanuts and he toed the shoes off wrong and the left one sat crushed at the heel, and she heated the container because he would eat from it with a fork over the sink if she did not, and he said the sale had been annoying, and she said good, and he said that guy was still out so Monday might be dead too, and she said mm, and the television did the thing with mouths, and neither of them followed it.

Monday might be dead too. She put it with the strawberries and the banner and the train being stupid. A man she had never met was out. Classes cancelled. Her son in the kitchen. The form still unsigned under the bowl.

She tied the tarp after he went to his room, concrete cold, three walls, no desk yet. In the unused air she stood long enough to feel foolish and then she went in and locked the glass and washed the fork.

The shoes were by the door. Both kinds. She stepped around all of them, and the stepping was already a habit, and habits were how you got through a Friday that was only a Friday.
