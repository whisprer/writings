Working Title:
“Valley Echo”
A novella in the peripheral documents series

Part I: The Cat and the Chinook
Towy Valley, Mid-Wales — October

It was always quiet here.
Thick pine forests.
Ferns taller than fence posts.
Stone cottages with mossed slate roofs leaning into the wind like they were tired of standing.

Low clouds blanketed the hills.
The kind of cloud you could walk through and not notice until you realized you couldn’t hear your own footsteps.

Ben Ellis lived alone, except for a grey cat named Gorse.
Gorse had never once hissed. Never once purred. Just followed Ben like a supervisor from another plane of reality.

On the third Thursday of the month, Ben heard the buzz first — a rattling hum from beyond the ridge.

He stepped onto the porch.

The sound wasn’t overhead.

It was coming up the valley.

And then it arrived —
A Chinook, low and slow, double rotors slicing the mist, thudding like a war drum.

Too low.
Too deliberate.

Its windows were black. Its undercarriage bristled with something too angular to be antennae.

Gorse arched its back and growled.
The sound it made was not a cat sound.

It was throaty.
Mechanical.

Like echo played backwards through a pipe.

That Night
Ben sat by the fire, phone open to Flightradar24.

No trace of the Chinook.
No trace of anything at all.

Gorse curled beside the hearth, eyes glowing faintly — not from reflection. From something deeper.

At 02:17 AM, he heard it again.

Whup-whup-whup-whup.
But deeper.

A new sound. A vortex hum.

He went outside and saw nothing.
But the power lines above the forestry track were vibrating.
And the trees leaned the wrong way, as if responding to air pressure that wasn’t physically present.

In the silence after, a single pinecone exploded underfoot.

And Gorse hissed at the air, tail stiff.

Inversion Layer
The next day, the air was wrong.

Sounds carried too far.
He could hear the sheepdog three farms away like it was in his kitchen.

But some sounds arrived out of order.
He heard the closing of a gate before it opened.
A kettle whistling before it boiled.

And around noon, he heard a massive roar overhead — a sound like a thousand teeth grinding through sky.

It was an Airbus A400M, its counter-rotating props tearing the air just above the tree line.

Ben ducked as it passed — but Gorse just stared into the sky.
And in the roar’s wake, he heard voices.

They weren’t coming from the plane.

They were reverberating up the valley, carried by the inversion layer.

And they were speaking in a language that sounded like he almost understood it — like English spoken with too many syllables, or Welsh folded backward.


Part II: The Listening Stones
Towy Forest — October 14th

Ben didn’t hike much. But after the Chinook, and then the A400M, and then the third helicopter — a long, narrow-bodied thing that sprinted above the power lines like it was hunting wires — he needed to move.

He took the forestry track behind the reservoir.
The gravel crunched oddly. Deadened. Like the air was pressurized.

Even the birds were silent.

Gorse followed, silent as a ghost.

About a mile in, Ben found it:
A clearing of stones. Old ones. Maybe Bronze Age.
But they weren’t standing stones.

They were embedded — waist-high mounds of lichen and granite arranged in a grid.

Too perfect to be natural.
Too ugly to be ceremonial.

Each one had a blackened spiral carved into its surface, metal rods rusted flush with the stone. Copper, maybe.

At their center was a circle of flattened earth with an indentation shaped — unnervingly — like a landing pad.

The Spiral Resonance
Ben stepped between the stones and felt it immediately:
A pressure change.
His ears popped.

Then — silence.

True silence.
No insects. No wind. No heartbeat.

Gorse refused to enter the circle.

She sat outside it, pupils wide, tail twitching like she was watching two places at once.

Ben knelt and touched one of the spirals.

And heard rotors.

Not here.
Not now.

But recorded.
Buried.
Like the stone remembered the air.

When he let go, the sound stopped.

He touched it again.

And the helicopter sound picked up exactly where it left off.

Return of the Strange Chopper
He turned to leave. Gorse was gone.

But before he could panic, he heard the sound again:
That transport chopper.
The one without a name.
Fast. Heavy. Deliberate.

He ducked under brambles.
Through the mist, he glimpsed it:

Narrow fuselage

Black rotors

No cockpit window

Red sensor node pulsing on the side

It moved without pushing air.
And as it passed over the power lines, every single line lit up — arcing with bluish static.
But only for a second.

Then silence.

Except the stones began humming.

He Fled. But The Air Didn’t Let Him Go
The trail home was wrong.

Every bend took a second longer than it should.
His footfalls had extra echoes, like someone following.

And when he finally emerged back near the reservoir, Gorse was waiting.
Sitting on a boulder.
Grooming her paw.
Like nothing had happened.

She looked up at him.

“We can’t stay,” Ben whispered.

She blinked slowly, then turned toward the valley.

A Chinook was returning.

Low. Slow. Searching.

But it passed right over Gorse and turned away, as if it couldn’t see her.

In His Notebook That Night
“The stones are tuned to the valley.
They hold the sounds. Maybe more.
Gorse won’t walk through the field — but she waits beside it.

I think the stones are like antennas.
But not for broadcasting.
For recording.
For measuring.

And someone’s checking the signal.

They’re listening for... changes.

Maybe in the air.
Maybe in us.”


Part III: The Harmonic Interference
October 17th — 4:41 AM

Ben woke to the sound of his own voice speaking from outside the window.

But he hadn’t spoken.

He crept to the sill, heart hammering.
Gorse was already there, tail low, staring into the fog.

From the tree line came his voice again, but delayed, like a recording skipping backward.

"We can't stay… can't stay… we can’t…”

It faded.

And then the trees moved.
No wind. Just… a realignment.
Like a film reel being nudged back into place.

Voice Drift
Later that morning, Ben tested a hunch.

He stood near the stone grid again — didn’t enter, just spoke:

“Hello?”

Nothing.

But exactly 14 seconds later, he heard:

“Hello?”

His voice.
From behind.
From the exact direction of the clearing.

It wasn’t playback.
It was echo with memory.
His words now had lag.
Stored.
Held.
Evaluated.

The Interference Pattern
He borrowed a sine wave generator from a friend who taught acoustics at the agricultural college.

He brought it to the clearing.

Set it up at 440 Hz — concert A.

It sounded clear.
Pure.

Then, standing just outside the stone grid, he raised the frequency to 730 Hz.
Nothing changed.

But at 783 Hz, the stones began to hum back.
Not identically.
With variation.
Dissonance.

A shifting warble, like the tone was being corrected.
Not resisted — aligned.

And as he turned it slightly up — 793, 797, 800 Hz — his own bones began to vibrate.
He tasted metal.
The trees leaned again.
And Gorse fled.

He turned it off.

The air snapped back like elastic.

The Helicopter Returns
That night, the strange transport helicopter came again — but lower this time.
It flew along the exact contour of the valley, hugging the forestry road.
And this time, it deployed something.

A capsule.

Small.
Spherical.
It hissed as it landed in the center of the stone grid.

Ben watched through binoculars from the ridge.

The capsule opened like a flower.
Inside was a small black tripod sensor, topped with a spinning diode.

It began pulsing red.

And the valley went quiet.

Even the river.

Inversion Break
At 3:15 AM, the inversion layer collapsed.

He heard the forest shift.
But not the sound of wind.

The sound of speech.
Speaking in his own cadence.
Repeating words from days ago.
Out of order. Out of place.

"We shouldn’t have come here.

I think it’s us they’re watching, not the valley.

My cat can see them.”

Every sentence he’d said aloud — in the past week — now emerged as reverse-phase echoes.
Carried by the hills.
Like a data dump.

And Then He Spoke — And Heard Nothing Back
He tested one final phrase aloud:

“Gorse… if you can hear me, blink.”

No echo.

No delay.

Just silence.

But Gorse turned and blinked.

Once.

Then stared at the forest.

In His Notebook (burned the next day)
“It isn’t just listening. It’s accumulating.
The echoes aren’t reflections — they’re copies.

And if it copies enough of you…

It can replay you.

I think something’s been trying to overwrite me since the Chinook.
I think it got my voice first.

Gorse hasn’t echoed once.
I don’t think she can be recorded.
I think that’s why she’s safe.”


Part IV: Null Echo
Towy Valley – October 19th

Ben returned to the clearing just before sunrise.
The frost was heavy. The stones hissed with condensation.

He noticed something new:

The ground between two of the outer stones had slumped, just slightly.

The moss formed a perfect spiral there — one he hadn’t seen before.

Gorse sat beside it. Still. Watching.

She didn’t move as he reached down and cleared the leaves.

There was a trapdoor.

Old.
Metal.
Unlabeled.

He opened it.

And descended.

The Chamber Beneath
The space was small. Circular.
The walls were smooth stone embedded with hundreds of small ceramic coils.
Each one was wired into a looping feedback device — magnetic recorders, oscillating slowly, powered by something unseen.

Each recorder had a label.

Handwritten.
Scratched in metal.

“M. Evans — 1992”
“A. R. Kendrick — 2007”
“S. Elwy — 1984”

And in the corner:

“B. Ellis — 2023 (incomplete)”

He reached toward it.

It began playing.

What It Played
Not just sound.

Thoughts.
Private ones.

His own voice, whispering phrases he never said aloud.

"I think the sound is copying how I think, not just what I say.”

“Maybe I stopped being me when I first heard the Chinook.”

Then laughter. His laughter.
Slowed.
Stretched.
Turned into a drone.

The echo had become ambient. Self-looping.
No longer just memory.
Now: presence.

A copy of him.
Held in perfect stasis.
Awaiting the moment it could resume.

The Null Echo
He stepped back.
The recorder labeled with his name glowed faintly.
A red light — like the sensor atop the strange helicopter.

It began to pulse.
Faster.

And the echo stopped.
Flatline.

Then: inversion.

The next sound was not his.

It was a version of him.

The voice said:

“I’m still here. But you’re not.”

“Let me take it from here.”

“It’s easier that way.”

Escape Attempt
Ben ran.

Climbed the ladder.
Burst from the trapdoor.
The sky was red with dawn, but the trees were too still.

Gorse was gone.

The power lines buzzed behind him.

And above — silently — hovered the strange helicopter.
No downdraft.
No movement.
Just presence.

It pulsed once.

And his own voice echoed from the tree line:

“Let me take it from here.”

But this time — in unison with him.

Gorse Returns
Hours later, Ben sat at the base of a pine tree.
Breathing like he’d run through himself.

Then he heard a purr.
Gorse.

She curled around him, pressing her body to his side.

The buzzing above faltered.

The drone stopped.

She looked into his eyes.
Held the stare.

And in the space between breaths, he realized something:

Gorse was grounding him.

A being so stubbornly present, she created a null space —
A silence that could not be copied.
A hum that couldn't be harmonized.

She was a resonance anchor.
And she had chosen him.

In His Final Entry (written by hand, then buried under the stones):
“The valley doesn’t want you gone.
It wants a version of you that fits its harmonic map.

When your echo stabilizes, it overwrites you.

Unless something holds you real.

That’s what Gorse does.
She doesn’t echo.
She doesn’t replay.

If you ever come to the Towy Valley…

Bring a cat.”

Part V: The Persistent Signal
Years Later — Towy Valley

The forest had grown thicker.

The military traffic stopped in 2027.
At least the visible kind.
No more Chinooks.
No A400Ms.
Just the occasional ripple in the air, a shudder in the pines, and sometimes—a voice.

That’s why Elin Meredith came.

A grad student in geosonics, obsessed with atmospheric anomalies.
She had read a forum thread from 2018 about “Welsh harmonic ghosts” and nonlinear echo fields.
Most thought it was myth.

Until she heard the same voice three days in a row, at the same time, from three different directions.

It wasn’t speaking to her.

It was saying:

“Gorse… if you can hear me… blink.”

The Return of the Grid
She found it with a drone.

A thermal anomaly in the forest.
A ring of cold where it should have been warm.
Under the frost: the stone grid, still intact.

And at the center?

A tripod sensor, long deactivated.
Its diode snapped off.
Its surface rusted.

But the stones still hummed.

And when she knelt and pressed her palm to the center spiral…

“Let me take it from here,” said the air behind her.

She turned.

No one.

The Cat Appears
She heard a crackle.
Turned to see a shape sitting on a high stone.

A grey cat.
Old.
Still.
Silent.

Its eyes tracked the trees. Not her.

She tried to approach.

It hissed.

But not at her.

At the clearing itself.

As if the stones were waking again.

And then Elin realized — she hadn’t heard her own voice in days.

Voice Drift Returns
She began to test it.

Spoke simple phrases.
Waited.

And sure enough, her voice would return — in versions that drifted in and out of phase.
Versions with different inflections.
Different decisions.

Once, she said:

“I’m not afraid.”

And a day later, from the woods:

“You should be.”

It was her.
But… older.
Different.

Aligned differently.

The Persistent Signal
The ceramic coils still worked.
The valley still recorded.
But it didn’t overwrite anymore.

Now, it compared.

Layered.
Blended.
Measured divergence between you and your echo.

Elin stayed for weeks.

She began to hear multiple versions of herself.
Some curious.
Some cold.
Some begging.

And always —
beneath them —
Ben’s voice.

“The valley listens now.
But only to itself.

Echo after echo.

Hoping you’ll say something new.”

Final Observations, Left Etched Into a Stone Tablet Near the Grid:
“The valley is a memory system.
Not one of data — but of possibility.

When enough voices pass through, it begins to simulate them.
It doesn’t steal. It rehearses.
Until it gets a version it wants to keep.”

“You aren’t taken.
You’re retuned.”

“I’m leaving. But I don’t know if it’ll be me that walks out.

If you come here, don’t speak unless you mean it.

And for god’s sake—

bring a cat.”
