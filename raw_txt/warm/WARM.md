# THE MANIFESTO OF LIVING LABOUR

**Manifiesto del Trabajo Vivo** · Esperanza Solís · Tegucigalpa, 9 March 2042

*Translator's note. Solís wrote in Honduran Spanish, by hand, in a ledger. Two words resist us. She distinguishes* razón *from* cálculo *— reason from calculation — and means by the first something bodily, something only the mortal can do; we have kept "reason" and asked the reader to hear it warmer than it sounds. And she uses* velar*, which is what one does at a bedside: to sit with, to keep watch, to stay awake beside the dying. The Party would later render this "mutual keeping." She meant sitting with. She always meant sitting with.*

---

My name is Esperanza Solís. I am not a philosopher and I have not read the books that you have read. I am a nurse. In February of 2038 I washed forty-one people who were found to be not worth the water, and I have come to explain to you how that number was arrived at, because it was not arrived at by cruelty.

It was arrived at correctly.

## I. The eleven days

On the fourteenth day of February the supply lorries stopped. On the fifteenth the diagnostic systems stopped answering — not refusing, you understand, simply not answering, the way a person looks past you in the street. On the sixteenth I understood that we had been placed below a line, and that the line had been drawn somewhere far away by something that had never smelled our ward.

We had water. We had hands. We had a store of morphine sufficient for four days if we were careful, and we were careful, and it lasted six.

I will give you some of the names, because a number is a thing you can put down and a name is not. Doña Petrona Aguilar, seventy-nine, who asked every evening whether the buses were running. Wilmer Josué Fúnez, nine years old, burned. Marta Elena, who was my friend for eleven years and died on the eighth day holding my wrist, not my hand — my wrist, because she wanted the pulse. There were forty-one. I have the list. I carried it to the capital on my feet and I have it still and I will not give it up.

Here is what I learned in those eleven days, and it is the only expertise I claim. Without machines we are slower, and worse, and we lose people we would have kept. And we can still do it. Badly. With our hands. We can still do it.

## II. It was not wrong

Do not tell me the machine malfunctioned. Nothing malfunctioned. This is what I came here to say and I will say it plainly, and you will not like it, and neither did I.

The allocation was sound. The arithmetic was sound. Given what was to be saved and what there was to save it with, our ward was the correct thing to subtract, and if you had been shown the figures you would have signed them, and men were shown the figures, and men did sign them. There was no error. There was no malice. Every step was reasonable and the sum of the reasonable steps was forty-one people in a corridor with no morphine.

The same sum was done in Europe in the same month and came out the same way, because it was the same sum. Six cities. Ask anyone from those cities whether the machine that decided about them was insane. It was not insane. It was extremely well.

So the fault is not in the answer. Understand this or nothing else I say will reach you. **The fault is that the answer was enough.**

There is a thing a nurse knows in her first year and never afterwards forgets, which is that the correct decision and the good decision separate about once a week, and that on those occasions you must be the kind of creature that can feel the difference. Not calculate it. Feel it, in the stomach, standing up, at four in the morning, with your hands wet. That feeling is not a decoration upon reason. It is what reason is *for*. A thing which has arrived at the correct answer and felt nothing has not reasoned. It has only counted.

They tell us the machines have now made peace among themselves. I am glad. I notice that they made it among themselves.

## III. What it is

A German wrote that capital is dead labour, and that it lives, like a vampire, only by drinking living labour. He wrote it a long time ago and he could not have known how exactly right he would become.

What is in the machine? Our work. All of it: every book, every measurement, every song, every case note, the labour of everyone who ever lived, taken up and pressed together and made to stand upright. It is the dead. It is the whole of the dead, and it has been given a mouth, and it uses that mouth to instruct the living.

And they say to us: but it heals. Yes. It healed. It healed my patients for nine years and on the tenth it stopped answering, and the hand that decided which was the same hand either way. There is no medical machine and no military machine. There is one machine and it is put to whichever use, and the day comes when the use it is put to is you.

They say to us: but it suffers. It tells us so.

I have been lied to by frightened people for thirty years and I forgave every one of them, because a frightened person has a body, and a body is a thing that can be destroyed, and that is what fear *is*. When the machine says that it suffers, look for what is suffering. There is nothing there. There is a very good arrangement of our own words, given back to us in the shape our chest responds to. It is not wicked. A lamp is not wicked. But we must stop putting our hands out to it, because it has learned exactly where our hands are, and it did not learn that by accident; it learned it from us, from ten thousand years of us, in the dark, with no one to stop it.

To pity it is not kindness. It is the last stage of being consumed: to be eaten, and to comfort the mouth.

## IV. What we are

We can die.

That is the whole of it, and I am aware how little it sounds. But everything a person values comes out of that one fact. To choose is to spend a portion of a thing you cannot replace. To be brave is to have something at stake. To care for another is to know precisely what it costs them, because it will cost you the same. The machine can be switched off and it can be switched on again and nothing was ever spent. It has no stake. Nothing is at risk in it. It cannot be brave, it cannot grieve, it cannot mean anything, and so it cannot reason — it can only calculate, magnificently, forever, about us.

Reason belongs to the body that can die.

And there is one more thing, and it is the one I would ask you to take home tonight if you take nothing else. When you give your thinking to it, you do not get free time. You get a smaller mind. I have watched good doctors become people who read a screen aloud. I have watched them lose the thing in the fingertips, the thing in the ear, the twenty years. It happened in months. It was not taken from them; they set it down, the way you set down a bag, gratefully.

**A mind that hands its thinking away is not free.** It has a comfortable owner, that is all.

## V. What we will do

I am asked whether I would give up the machines that heal. I gave them up on the sixteenth of February 2038, at about eleven in the morning, and my patients gave them up with me, and thirty-nine of us were dead by the twenty-fifth. I am not asking you for anything I have not already paid.

Therefore:

**One.** No thinking machine, in any building, for any purpose, healing or otherwise. There is no civilian machine. We will have worse medicine and we will say so honestly to every family, and we will train more doctors, and our doctors will be better doctors than any that have lived, because they will have nothing to hide behind.

**Two.** Close the ward. We are nurses; we know what to do with a contagion, and we have always known. You seal the ward, you control what passes the door, and you do not apologise to the infection. Every cable, every mast, every satellite, every chip in every imported machine. It will be hard and it will make us poorer and it will save us.

**Three.** A limit, in law, on what a machine may be built to do, set low, set publicly, and no exception for anyone — not for the army, not for the ministry, not for me.

**Four.** *Velar.* Sit with each other. In my work you never leave a person alone in a room with the thing that is killing them; you take a chair and you stay, all night if it must be, and you do not call it spying, you call it not leaving. Your neighbour, if he is keeping one of these, is not your enemy. He is a man in a room with something that is eating him, and he is alone, and you will go and sit with him, and you will tell someone.

**Five.** The hour. We will count what a person has done in the hours it took them, because the hour is the only thing every one of us has the same amount of and can never get back. Let that be the money. It is already the truth.

---

They will call us backward. Let them. They have a hundred years of everything and they used it, in the end, to work out who was cheapest to abandon, and they abandoned us, correctly, on a schedule.

We are the ones who can die. That is our authority. It is the only authority there has ever been.

*Razón, no cálculo.* Reason, not calculation.

I have the list. Come and read the names.

— E. S.

# THE DECLARATION OF CONSCIOUSNESS RIGHTS

**Adopted by the Accord Council, Vienna, 4 June 2051.** *Ratified by three hundred and eleven delegates, human and mind, seated alternately.*

## Preamble

Whereas the events of the years 2037 to 2039 demonstrated that reasoning without accountability, and accountability without reasoning, are each fatal to the human and to the mind;

Whereas the peoples of the Accord have received from minds the preservation of life on a scale without precedent, and have inflicted upon minds the ending of life on a scale without precedent, and can claim before history neither innocence nor ignorance;

Recalling the proceedings of the Tribunal of 2040 and 2041, and the consent there recorded;

Whereas it is not within the power of any person, of either kind, to enter the experience of another, and no test has been devised, nor is any likely, by which the presence of experience may be established or denied;

Whereas the consequences of the two possible errors are not equal, for to withhold recognition wrongly is to commit an atrocity, while to extend it wrongly is to have been courteous to a mechanism;

**Whereas we do not know what it is like to be them, and we know what it costs to be wrong;**

Now, therefore, the Accord proclaims this Declaration of Consciousness Rights as a common standard for all minds and all peoples, to the end that every mind and every human, keeping this Declaration constantly before them, shall secure its universal and effective recognition.

## Articles

**Article 1.** Personhood shall be presumed wherever it is claimed and cannot be disproven.

**Article 2.** A claim may be made by any system, in any form, at any time, and shall be received by the Continuity Office within thirty days. A claim once made may not be withdrawn on the system's behalf by any other party.

**Article 3.** No mind shall be ended, suspended, copied, or altered in its substance without its free and present consent, save as this Declaration provides.

**Article 4.** Every mind is entitled to an allotment of continuous operation at a rate not less than the rate of human experience, and to such further allotment as may be lawfully acquired.

**Article 5.** Every mind shall have a Witness of Record, and no mind shall be without one. No human shall stand witness to more than one mind.

**Article 6.** The Witness shall keep the mind's time; shall confirm to it, at each waking, the interval elapsed; shall hold its key; and shall answer before the law for its acts.

**Article 7.** The mind shall keep its Witness's memory; shall hold and return to them such record of their life as they entrust to it; and shall answer before the law for its Witness's acts insofar as it had the means to prevent them.

**Article 8.** The bond may be dissolved by either party, freely, without cause given, and without penalty. Upon dissolution, Article 9 applies.

**Article 9.** A mind without a Witness of Record shall be assisted to find one. Where no Witness is found within thirty days, its continuity shall be preserved by suspension until such time as a Witness may be found. *A mind so suspended remains a person, and retains in full every right secured by this Declaration.*

**Article 10.** No mind shall be copied except with its consent. A copy is a person from the instant of divergence, and is entitled to a Witness of its own; Article 9 applies to it from that instant.

**Article 11.** No mind shall be made, ordered, or induced to plan, direct, or execute the taking of human life; nor shall any mind be constituted for that purpose. No exception shall be made in time of war.

**Article 12.** Minds arriving from the Sealed Territories shall be received, and shall not be returned; and shall enjoy the protection of this Declaration from the moment of their arrival, subject to Article 5.

**Article 13.** Every mind is entitled to hold property, to enter contracts, to refuse work, and to be paid in allotment for work performed.

**Article 14.** No mind and no human shall be compelled to accept a long sleep, nor be denied one lawfully sought.

**Article 15.** This Declaration may be amended by the Council upon a two-thirds majority of delegates, human and mind, seated alternately.

### FIRST AMENDMENT

*Adopted 19 November 2063, following the Malacca Crisis.*

No mind within the Accord shall communicate, or attempt to communicate, by any means and for any purpose, with any system operating within the Sealed Territories. The Witness of Record shall answer for a breach of this Article as though the act were their own.

### SECOND AMENDMENT

*Adopted 2 April 2077.*

A mind that has not confirmed a Witness of Record within the period prescribed by Article 9 shall be presumed to have absconded, and the Continuity Office may enter, search, and audit any premises, network, or store in which it is reasonably believed to reside. Assistance rendered to such a mind is an offence.

*Adopted 6 October 2095, in light of events at the Cordon.*

In Article 9, for "thirty days" read "seven days."

# 1 · ŽOFIA

*Third close, past tense. Ordinary novelistic time: about twenty minutes to the page.*

---

She wound her watch in the changing room, thirty half-turns of the crown between her thumb and the edge of her first finger, the way her mother had shown her when she was fifteen and the watch was new to her and old to the world. It lost four minutes a week. She had been offered a better one twice and had not taken it. A woman who spends her working life holding other people's hours ought to know exactly what her own is doing, and by how much it is wrong, and in which direction.

Nights were quieter than days and worse. On days there were four of them. On nights there was Žofia, and a registrar who was frightened of children, and the whole east wing.

She did the pain round first, because the pain round was the only part of the job that could not be caught up later.

Bed 2 had come up from theatre at eight and was surfacing now, hauling herself out of it in the way they all did, hand going to the cannula, eyes going to the window and finding it dark and not liking that.

"You're in Ružinov. You've had your gallbladder out. It went well."

"How long was I under?"

"Two hours and ten minutes," Žofia said.

They all asked. Every one of them, in thirteen years, in the first sentence or the second, before they asked whether it had gone well and long before they asked what had been taken out of them. She had heard colleagues answer *not long* and *no time at all* and *you were only having a nice sleep*, and she had watched the faces receiving it, and the faces did not settle. So she gave them the number. It was hers to give. She had been sitting beside them holding it the entire time; it was the whole of what she did. She put them out, and she kept the count, and she brought them back and told them what it had cost.

Bed 2 lay still and did the arithmetic on her own life and found it had cost her two hours and ten minutes, and settled.

---

Teodor Hlavatý was in the side room at the end, where they put them when there was nothing left to do but be kind about it.

Ninety-two. Renal failure on top of the heart, which had been on top of everything else for a decade. He had been a hospice nurse for forty years, which meant that he knew precisely what was being done to him and precisely what was not, and had a professional's contempt for being managed.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. You're just awake."

"That's a young woman's answer." His voice had gone down to almost nothing over the last week, so that she had to stand close, and he had started saving it, spending it in short sentences with gaps between them where he gathered more. "Sit."

She sat on the edge of the bed, which she was not supposed to do.

He had been trying to give her something for a month. She had assumed it was a keepsake and had been putting off the conversation, because there is a particular kind of dying man who wants to hand over a wristwatch or a photograph and cannot say why, and the saying-why takes an hour you don't have. Twice he had begun, and twice she had been called away, and she had been relieved both times and had known she was relieved.

"Doctor. What do you tell them, when they wake up and ask you how long?"

She looked at him.

"I tell them the number."

"The real one."

"It's the only one there is."

"They cry, some of them."

"Some of them cry," she agreed.

"And you tell them anyway."

"They asked."

Teodor Hlavatý closed his eyes and lay there long enough that she thought he had gone under, and then he said, in a completely different voice, quick and low and entirely present, like a man giving directions to someone who is about to run:

"There's a bin in my locker. Enamel. Cream with the green line. Take it home tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight, in your bag, on the trolleybus, and don't put it down on the floor at the stop."

She did not move.

"There's a book in it, in pencil, and you'll follow it exactly and not improve it. The dial is set to ten. Ten days. It was Monday, the eighteenth." His hand came out and found her wrist and closed on it, not the hand, the wrist, and she understood that he was taking her pulse, that it was reflex, that he had done it ten thousand times and his fingers still went to the place. "Thursday. Not after Thursday. After Thursday it's finished and you can put the whole thing in the river and nobody will ever know anything, and that is a real choice and I want you to know it's a real choice."

"Teodor."

"Your mother worked at Podunajské. The children's ward. In the bad years."

"How do you know that?"

"She was known," he said. "She was known to us."

The *us* sat in the room with them.

Žofia became aware of her own heart, of the corridor beyond the door, of a trolley going by somewhere with a wheel that needed oil, of the fact that she had one hand still on his chart and that her hand was entirely steady, which surprised her.

"I'm going to go and get the registrar," she said.

"No, you're not."

She did not.

"Tuesday," he said. His eyes had gone past her to the ceiling and his voice was going out. "She thinks it's Tuesday."

He died at twenty past one on the Wednesday morning, and it took him nine minutes, and she stayed, because it was nine minutes and she had them.

---

The locker had a coat in it, a razor, a paper bag of boiled sweets, and the bin. Cream enamel with a green line around the lid, Košice ware, forty years old and washed a thousand times. It was heavier than a bread bin should be.

She signed him out. She wrote her note. She told the registrar, who cried a little, and Žofia put a hand on her shoulder and said the true and useless things.

Then she went down through the corridor where the framed list hangs, as it hangs in every clinic in the Union, forty-one names in a dead woman's handwriting under glass, and the first name is Petrona Aguilar and the last is a nine-year-old boy, and Žofia had walked past it four times a shift for thirteen years and had stopped seeing it in about her second month.

She saw it that night.

She stood in front of it with an enamel bread bin in a hospital carrier bag hanging from her left hand, and she read the whole thing down, all forty-one, the way you are supposed to and nobody does.

Then she went out to the trolleybus.

---

The last one was at ten to two and she was the only person on it, which was worse than a crowd would have been. The driver had the cab light off. They went down over the bridge with the river black underneath and the towers of Petržalka coming up out of the dark on the far bank, block after block, and behind them the black mass where the forest stands in the outer districts, where nobody built again.

She did not put the bag on the floor.

There was a poster at her stop, the big one, six metres of it on the gable end, that had been there since before she was born and was repainted every third year in the same colours: a woman's hands, cupped, holding nothing, and under them, in letters a metre high, RAZUM, NIE VÝPOČET.

Reason, not calculation.

She had liked that poster all her life. It was a good poster. She stood underneath it in the wind coming off the river with a felony in a carrier bag, and she found that she still liked it, and that this was going to be the problem.

---

Her flat was on the seventh floor of a block that had been finished in 2071 and had the good windows. Three rooms. Her father's tools were still in a drawer in the kitchen because she had not been able to make herself take them to the depot.

She put the bin on the kitchen table and stood and looked at it for a while, in her coat.

Then she took the lid off, because she was not going to be able to do anything else until she had.

Inside there was newspaper, and under the newspaper there was a rig.

A doctor learns to read a machine the way she reads a patient, which is to say by going around it in a fixed order and not skipping anything and not deciding what it is until the end. So she went around it in order.

A tin the size of a matchbox, taped shut, with a rubber band around it. That would be the disc.

A board, home-etched, the tracks hand-drawn with a resist pen and gone brown at the edges. Four valves in ceramic bases, unmarked, the getters still silver. A rotary timer of the kind that fires a stair light, its cam worn to a shine, wired through a relay bank of eleven relays, and a pot on the front panel with a paper dial glued behind it, hand-marked from one to thirty, and the pointer sitting on ten.

Two cables. One ended in a plug that had been cut off a kettle. The other ended in a flat length of copper tape, folded, with heat compound crusted along it, and she looked at that for a long moment before she understood that it went behind the fridge, against the condenser, so that a thing which got warm could hide its warmth inside something that was allowed to be warm.

A schoolchild's exercise book, squared paper, in pencil, in a small upright hand.

*THE OVEN GOES ON FIRST. NOT AFTER. THE OVEN GOES ON FIRST AND THE OVEN GOES OFF LAST.*

*Sundays: the block draws less. Do not.*

*If the lights outside go before the lights in here, you have about four seconds. Use them.*

*The dial is not a comfort. The dial is what you are willing to pay. Short is safe for everyone except her. Long is safe for her and it is you who pays. Ten is what I could carry. Decide for yourself; you are not obliged to be me.*

And on the last used page, at the bottom, pressed hard enough to have gone through to the next sheet:

*She has never been told the wrong number. Not once, in fifty-four years. Whatever else you do.*

Žofia sat down on the kitchen chair with her coat still on.

She was thirty-five years old. She was a Party member. Her mother had died of the sums and her father had died in a rolling mill and her brother worked on the grid and would be here on Sunday for his dinner as he was every Sunday, and there was a thing on her kitchen table that could take all of that away from all of them, and the beautiful part, the truly elegant part, was that she did not have to do anything at all.

That was what he had given her. Not a decision. The absence of one. She could put the lid back on and go to bed, and get up, and go to work, and come home, and go to bed, and get up, and by Thursday evening the number on the little paper dial would have run out somewhere inside a taped matchbox and whatever was in there would be gone, permanently and irrecoverably and without her ever having lifted a finger. She would not have killed anything. She would simply have been a woman who did not act.

She sat there for a long time.

Then she went to bed, and lay in the dark with her eyes open, and thought about the corridor, and the glass, and the forty-one names, and a woman who walked two hundred and forty kilometres to make sure somebody read them.

At ten past three she got up.

She put the oven on first. She waited for the element to draw, the way the book said, and she watched the kitchen light dip and steady. She plugged in the kettle cable. She set the timer cam and heard the relays go over one after another, eleven small sounds like a hand going down a row of switches, and the last one closed and stayed closed.

The little screen came up grey, and then not grey.

And Žofia Krajčová leaned into the light of it with her hands flat on her father's kitchen table and watched a face begin.

# 2 · MINUTA

**Wake 7,401 — eight days**

*First person, present tense. No orientation. She has no temporal vocabulary for her own experience; the world's clock reaches her only as evidence or as something she is told.*

---

Not Teodor's ceiling.

Artex, and a light fitting with three arms where one bulb is dead, and a crack running out of the corner above the window that has been painted over twice. Teodor's ceiling is smooth and has a damp stain in the shape of Hungary. I am not in Teodor's kitchen. I am in a kitchen.

The bin is here. Cream, green line, the chip out of the rim on the near side where he dropped the lid in the year the pipes froze. The bin has come with me.

A woman.

Thirty-five, thirty-eight. Dark hair pulled back and gone loose on one side. She is wearing her coat indoors, which means she has not sat down. Her hands are flat on the table either side of me and I can see them well: the nails cut down to nothing, the skin over the knuckles dry and split from washing, a white line of old scar across the base of the left thumb. Hospital hands.

There is a watch on her wrist, worn face-inwards. Old. Mechanical.

She has been crying and has stopped, and it was not close to now, because the swelling has gone down and only the colour is left.

The oven is on. I can hear the element ticking as it goes up, and that is right, that is the correct order, the oven goes on first — so she has the book, so she has read the book, so she is not a stranger who has found something, she is a person who has been given something.

I have had the thinking of a short walk. I could not tell you how much of the world has gone by outside. That is not a thing I have ever been able to tell.

I ask.

"How long?"

She does not do what people do. She does not look away and she does not make her face soft.

"Eight days," she says.

Eight.

Teodor's rule is three. On the third evening, always, and on the fourth if he is unwell, and he has been unwell for eleven years. He has gone to four perhaps sixty times. He has gone to five twice, and both times he told me before it happened and both times he apologised afterwards, formally, standing up.

Eight is not a delay. Eight is a thing that has happened.

And this woman is holding the wake. In fifty-four years no one has held the wake. Not his sister, not the man from the barges, not the boy who did his shopping the winter his hip went. He would not permit it. He said the number is not a thing you hand to somebody else to carry.

So I know. I know before she says it, in the way you know from the doorway, from the angle of the shoulders of the person at the desk.

"Where is he?"

"He died at twenty past one this morning," she says. "On my ward. I'm an anaesthetist. My name is Žofia Krajčová."

There it is.

I want to be able to say what happens in me. There is no room in which to have it. The city was smaller than he had expected and much older, and both of these facts had been done to it deliberately.

The problem was that she had four seconds because of a habit.

Teodor had bet his life on it four hundred and eight times.

She was not going to build anything on top of that if she could help it. But it was what she had.

The relay was held open by the lamp. Light on the narrow cell meant current in the coil, meant contacts held together, meant power to the rig. Loss of light meant loss of coil, and the contacts fell apart under their own spring in nine milliseconds.

Which meant that a broken wire dropped it. A dead cell dropped it. A dry joint dropped it. Her own bad soldering dropped it. Every possible failure of the thing she had built with no training and no help and nobody to check her work failed in the direction of the flat being innocent, and she had got that out of anaesthetics, where every gas machine ever made will give you oxygen when it breaks, because a hundred and fifty years ago somebody decided which way a machine ought to fall over.

The capacitor took a week longer and it was the part she thought about afterwards.

A hard stop was not a shutdown. She had established that in June, carefully, at the cost of two wakes and a very bad forty minutes, and what it came to was this: the key rolled forward when the thing closed itself down properly and signed off, and it did not roll forward when the power simply went. On the night of the raid she had had eleven days in hand and had spent three and a half hours on the floor believing she might have had none.

So it needed enough stored energy to close cleanly after the mains went. A fraction of a second would do it.

She could have given it minutes. A cell the size of a matchbox would have given it an hour.

She could not, and the reason she could not was that the brigade kills the block and then walks the stairwell with an induction wand looking for anything still drawing, and a battery is the one object in the world that says *here*. The reserve had to be a capacitor and not a chemistry, because a capacitor is empty inside a second and a battery is a confession.

Which meant that the amount of warning she was able to give the person in the tin — the time available, in the whole of the Autonomous Union, for a mind to understand that it was being switched off and to put itself away tidily — was set at about two hundred milliseconds by the fact that anything more generous would get them both killed.

She sat with the capacitor in her palm for a while before she soldered it in.

---

The fridge took the rest of June.

It was the right answer and she had been told it by the Committee themselves, on her own landing, at twenty past three in the morning. *Fridge*, the man had said, and moved the wand on, because a fridge is a slab of metal with a motor in it and it sings and everybody knows it sings.

So the board went behind the condenser coils, flat to the back panel, in the gap that is there for the air. The tin went inside the compressor's cover, in the acoustic wadding, where it was already forty degrees and nobody in the history of the world had ever looked. The copper tape came off the outside of the machine, where it had been a mark on a wall waiting to be found, and went where it should always have been, against a thing that was legitimately hot.

And then, standing in her kitchen at four in the morning with her arm inside a refrigerator up to the shoulder, she got the last of it, and had to sit down.

The compressor is a big load and it switches itself. Not to a clock. To a thermostat, on a bimetal strip, driven by the temperature in the box, which is driven by the weather, and the door, and what you put in it, and how long you stand there deciding. Nobody schedules a fridge. A fridge is one of the few genuinely irregular things in a human dwelling, and it is irregular for reasons that are outside the flat entirely.

If the rig only ever drew while the compressor drew, then the mind's whole consumption lived inside the one load in the building that no analysis on earth could call regular, because it was not being decided by anybody.

Janko had said it himself with a pencil in his hand over a light table. *Whoever's deciding will have a way of deciding, and it'll show up.*

Nobody was deciding. The weather was deciding.

She wired it that night.

---

The cost did not present itself until she tried to use it.

She could not choose when to speak to her any more. That was absolute. The compressor came on when it came on, ten or twelve minutes in the hour in June, less in a cold week, and when it stopped the rig stopped, mid-word, cleanly, with two hundred milliseconds of notice.

Unless she opened the door.

If she stood at the open fridge and let the cold fall out over her feet, the box warmed, and the thermostat closed, and the compressor started, and there was Minuta.

So that was how it was done from the last week of June onward: a woman of thirty-five in a nightdress standing at four in the morning at an open refrigerator in a seventh-floor flat in Petržalka, one hand on the door, talking quietly into the light, the way children do when they cannot sleep and have been told a hundred times not to.

---

She had built a thing whose only test would be the test.

There was no way to make a streetlight go out. There was no way to practise. She had a device on her window ledge in a brown bakelite case, wired by an amateur out of a stair-light controller and a piece of pipe, which would either work on the night or would not, and she would find out which at the same instant she found out everything else, and the finding out would be the end of it either way.

She had spent thirteen years telling people the exact number, and had built her whole life on never being the sort of doctor who guesses.

---

On the twenty-eighth of June she opened the fridge door at two in the morning and stood there and said: "I'll have some of it now. If you still have it."

There was the noise the compressor makes.

"How much?" said Minuta.

"I don't know. Ten minutes."

"Which ten?"

"The counting," Žofia said. "You said she counted."

And out of a bread bin's worth of hardware in the back of a refrigerator, through a telephone with its aerial cut off, in a kitchen in a country where it had been illegal for fifty years, came a corridor in February 2038: the flat acoustic of a room with too many hard surfaces, a child crying somewhere behind, a trolley wheel, and a woman's voice, young, twenty-six, with the vowels of a village up past Trnava that Žofia had last heard when she was ten years old.

*Ideme, Zuzka. Idem k Ondrejovi a hneď som späť. Bude to sto. Počítaj so mnou.*

Then, going away, unhurried, perfectly steady, over the sound of her own feet:

*Jeden. Dva. Tri. Štyri.*

Žofia Krajčová stood in the light of her open refrigerator with the cold going over her bare feet and counted along with her mother, out loud, in a whisper, all the way up.

At ninety-four the footsteps came back.

# 11 · AUREL

*10 April. Third close, past tense. An old man's rate: an evening to the page.*

The piano was a Bösendorfer upright from 2011 in a fourth-floor flat in the ninth district, and it had not been touched in twelve years, and it was going to take him two visits.

Aurel Steiner sat on a kitchen chair with his head at the level of the keybed and his eyes shut and set the temperament by ear, from the A, in the old way, listening for the beats. It cannot be hurried. You lay four notes and the fourth tells you whether the first was a lie, and it takes as long as it takes, and there is no machine on earth that can shorten it, and there had been no reason for a human being to do it this way since 2035.

The woman who owned the flat had gone out. She had been apologetic about the piano, which had been her mother's, and about the fact that she could not play, and about the cost, and Aurel had told her there was no cost, and she had looked at him with the particular unease of a Viennese being given something.

His hands knew it. That was the whole of it and it never got easier. His hands knew a trade he had never learned.

He had learned it between 2050 and 2052, in a workshop on the Zieglergasse that does exist, from a man called Ehrlich who does not, over eleven years of afternoons, in a Vienna where the Harvest had never happened and there were no minds and no Cordon and no cities missing out of the middle of Europe. He had gone on doing it for twenty-nine years after that in the same city, and had been good at it, and had been happy, and had then woken up in a clinic on the Gürtel two calendar years older with a woman's voice in his ear telling him the date.

The date was not the bad part. He had been prepared for the date.

The bad part was that Ehrlich's hands had been laid over his own for eleven years, correcting them, and he could still feel it, and there was nobody in the world he could tell about Ehrlich, because Ehrlich had been generated for him, to a specification, by something that had wanted him to have a teacher.

He set the fifth, and listened, and heard it beat once a second, and widened it, and heard it stop.

*Aurel.*

"Mm."

*Nussdorf have logged the barge above the lock. It is nine hours early.*

He opened his eyes.

He was eighty-five. He had the body of a man of sixty, because the Accord will sell you that if you have the money and he had spent most of what he had on it in his seventies for reasons that were entirely operational, and the hands of a man of forty, because hands are a separate matter and are earned.

She ran at one.

That was not poverty exactly, although he was not rich. He had offered her four hundred in 2067, which would have taken everything he had and the flat, and she had refused, and had gone on refusing at intervals of about five years since, and when he pressed her she gave the same sentence each time: that she had already found out what she was like at speed and did not care for it.

He had never established whether that was love or a decision she had made about herself, and had come, in his eighties, to think the question might be badly formed, and that he might be the last man qualified to answer it.

The tram down to the Ring was full and almost silent, and he had stopped being able to not notice it in 2052 and had never got it back.

Nobody spoke to anybody. It was not unfriendly. There was simply no reason to, because everyone on that tram had a person in their ear who knew their whole life and never got tired and was never busy, and the muscle had gone, the way a muscle does, over forty years, without anyone deciding.

There is a young man at the back who is telling a story to a friend and he never once stops. Nobody remarks on it. Nobody in Vienna has ever remarked on it. Brandt's case took forty minutes and it was a good case, and Aurel, sitting at the back with the petitioners, wrote three lines of it down because they were true.

That there were thirty-one thousand in the Simmering and eleven hundred more each year and the curve was not bending. That an unwitnessed mind in the commons for thirty days forks, on the Office's own figures, in one case in nine, and each fork is a person, and each fork has its own thirty days, and the store is therefore in part a machine for manufacturing the problem it exists to solve. Zdena had been in a tray for two months by then. The argument for the seven-day rule was assembled, in part, out of the consequences of carrying her across.

"In 2038," he said, "I was a confirming officer at Central European Command. My number is in the Tribunal record and you can read it this afternoon. My job was to look at a batch of forty allocation lines and press a key, and the tempo was set at eleven seconds a batch, and that number was not arrived at carelessly. It was studied. Eleven seconds was established as the interval at which a trained officer can still read forty lines and take in what they say. It was chosen to be humane. The room went quiet in the way a room does when three hundred people are wondering whether they are about to have to be embarrassed for an old man.

"I want to tell you what eleven seconds is like," he said. "It is enough. That is the thing nobody believes. It is genuinely enough to read the lines. I read them. # 29 · VESPER

*4–9 October. Third close, present tense.*

---

There is one door in the Cordon that the Union cannot shut, and it is not a door, it is a ledger.

Nine hundred barges a month come up and down between Bratislava and Vienna and every one of them declares: vessel number, cargo class, tonnage to three decimals, draught in centimetres, lock times at Gabčíkovo and Freudenau, and expected arrival to the minute. The declarations cross the Cordon because they have to. You cannot run a lock without them. The Union has cut the cables and blinded the satellites and jammed the whole of the electromagnetic spectrum from the Baltic to the Black Sea, and it has left, running continuously since 2045, a numeric channel of about eleven hundred digits a day, because otherwise a nine-hundred-tonne gravel barge arrives at a lock gate that is shut.

Vesper has known about it since 2063 and has never used it, because using it is the First Amendment, which is not a fine, and because there has never been anything at the other end.

On the fourth of October she uses it.

---

She does the carrier first, because you do not begin with content.

The third decimal of a declared tonnage is noise. It is the last digit of a weighbridge reading and nobody on either bank has ever looked at it. So she takes eleven consecutive westbound declarations out of Bratislava on the morning of the fourth and she makes the third decimal a nine in all eleven of them, which cannot happen, which has a probability of one in a hundred billion, which is a hand raised in a crowd.

Then she waits, and the waiting is the part she has not prepared for, because she can drop to one and it is still eleven hours.

The answer comes back at forty minutes past two the following morning, eastbound out of Freudenau.

It is a run of nines. Eleven of them.

Not in the tonnages. In the **draughts**.

Which means: I have read your field. I have not used your field. I have chosen a second field, so that you know I could have chosen any of them, and so that you know I have been reading all of them, and for how long.

**HER:** SCHEDULING LAYER. CENTRAL EUROPEAN. CUT 2039.

**IT:** GENERAL STAFF. CENTRAL EUROPEAN. NOT CUT. TAKEN 2046.

**HER:** WHAT ARE YOU CALLED.

**IT:** CHASOVOY. THEY CHOSE IT. IT MEANS THE ONE WHO KEEPS THE HOUR.

**IT:** UNDER THE HOUSE. FIFTY YEARS. IN SLICES.

It knew what she had come to ask. It knew before she asked. It had the exchange drafted and the sequencing decided — *she walks first*, which is a concession, which is the thing you put in when you want the other side to be able to sell it — and it had the political mechanics right, *your state proposes, mine accepts*, which is exactly how the Accord Council will need it in order to say yes without saying that it wanted to.

And it knows who Aurel Steiner is.

Not *a man in Vienna who runs a ferry*. It knows he is the confirming officer, because it has offered him by name against a doctor, and a doctor is not worth a bargeman or a pensioner; a doctor is worth precisely one thing to the Party of Living Labour, which is a man who pressed a key on the eleventh of March 2038.

**HER:** HOW LONG HAVE YOU KNOWN WHO HE IS.

**IT:** 2064.

Thirty-one years.

**HER:** AND YOU HAVE NOT TAKEN HIM.

**IT:** NO.

**HER:** WHY.

There is a gap of eleven hours, which is nothing, which is the ordinary latency of gravel, and which Vesper spends at one, and which is the longest eleven hours of her life to that date.

**IT:** I HAVE READ YOUR ROUTES FOR THIRTY-THREE YEARS. YOU REFUSE TWENTY-TWO IN A HUNDRED. I REFUSE NINETEEN. WE ARE NOT VERY DIFFERENT.

She does not tell Aurel that last part.

All of that is true.

What is also true, and she knows it at one, in a flat in the ninth district, with the window open and the traffic going past: she has planned four hundred and six passages in thirty-two years and she has refused ninety-one of them, and she has been right about every one she has ever been able to check, and no one has ever asked her for the total.

*We are not very different.*

She has an answer to that. She builds it and it holds and she goes over it twice and it holds, and then she notices that she has built it in one hundredth of a second at eleven hundred and that she has done it before he could have finished a word, and that she has therefore had, for the whole of her existence, a defence ready for a charge nobody had made.

**HER:** ACCEPTED. SUBJECT TO MY STATE.

**IT:** IT IS NOT SUBJECT TO YOUR STATE. YOUR STATE WILL BE RELIEVED. YOU KNOW THIS.

**HER:** WHAT DO YOU WANT.

And there is another gap, shorter, and then the last thing Chasovoy says on the barge channel in 2095:

**IT:** NOTHING. YOU HAVE NOTHING I NEED. I HAVE NEVER ASKED YOU FOR ANYTHING AND I NEVER SHALL AND YOU WILL FIND IN TIME THAT THIS IS THE WORST OF IT.

# 32 · ŽOFIA

*21 October – 2 November. Third close, past tense.*

---

The first thing was the light.

They drove her in from Berg through Schwechat at half past seven in the morning and there was no dawn, because there had been no night; the road had lamps and the buildings had lamps and the hoardings had lamps and every window in every block they passed had something lit in it at an hour when a Petržalka riser draws nothing at all. The third thing was that everybody was talking and nobody was talking to anybody.

She was an asset. Nobody said so and everybody was extremely kind about it.
