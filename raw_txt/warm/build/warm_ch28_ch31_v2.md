# 28 · JANKO

*October. Third close, past tense.*

---

The fault recorder at Kopčany is a paper drum in a steel cabinet below the protection panel, and it turns once a week, and the used charts go into a box, and the box goes into the store, and the store is Janko Krajčo's.

He had the chart for the week of the twentieth of September in his hands on the twenty-third.

There was the trace for section three: a flat line, dead flat, all the way across, with the small square step at 00:25 where the section was isolated and nothing at all before it. No fault current. No earth. Nothing. A relay that had operated on a line that had never had anything wrong with it.

He stood in the half-basement at Kopčany with it in his two hands for a very long time.

Nobody reads the recorder charts. That is the truth of it and it is why he had done what he did that night in the first place. They are filed against the day somebody needs them, and somebody needs them perhaps four times a year, and there was a box of them at his elbow going back to 2086 with the string still on some of the bundles.

It would have taken him eleven seconds. Roll it, put it in the stove in the mess, come back, and there would then be no object on this earth that said anything except *earth fault, section three, isolated pending inspection, J. Krajčo*, in his own perfectly ordinary handwriting.

He put it back in the box, and put the box in the rack, and wrote the week number on the end of it in his own hand, and went out and sat on the step in the yard for half an hour.

---

He was allowed to send her nothing and he sent it anyway: a request, on the proper form, through the district office, to visit a prisoner on remand at Bezručova. It came back on the fourth of October marked *not at this stage* with an illegible initial.

He went to the hospital instead, because you can walk into a hospital, and stood in the corridor by the framed list for eleven minutes on a Tuesday afternoon looking at forty-one names in a dead woman's handwriting.

A student nurse of about nineteen came past and stopped and said, "Are you her brother?"

"Yes."

"You've got her face." The girl had her arms full of linen and was clearly not supposed to be talking to him. "She's coming back, isn't she."

"I don't know."

"She has to come back," said Klaudia Bednárová, "because there's four of them for eleven theatres and they've had a locum in since the twenty-first who won't be told anything," and then her chin went, and she said, "Sorry," and went off up the corridor fast with the linen.

Janko Krajčo stood in front of the glass for a while longer.

Then he went to Bezručova.

---

They kept him on a bench for two hours and he sat with his cap on his knees and did not move, and at ten past six Major Ferreira came out into the front hall in his shirtsleeves with his jacket over his arm, on his way home, and stopped.

"Krajčo."

"Sir. I need to tell you something and I'd like to do it properly, in an office, with it written down."

Ferreira looked at him for a moment.

"Come upstairs."

The office had the fan off now because it was October and the window shut. He did not sit down behind the desk. He stood with his back to it and his jacket still over his arm, which Janko understood, five years later, was a man arranging in advance for the meeting to be short.

"The twentieth of September," Janko said. "The lighting main, section three. There was no fault. I opened the section switch at twenty-five past midnight and pressed the test button on the relay to drop the flag, and I wrote it up as an earth fault, and the fault recorder chart for that week is in the store at Kopčany in the rack for the week ending the twenty-fourth and it shows a flat line, and I have had it in my hands and I put it back. I did it because you had rung to say the cleaning was brought forward to one o'clock and there was no way on this earth to get word to her, and there is no fault and there never was, and every warm flat in four blocks got thirty-five minutes out of it and one of them was my sister's. I want that written down and I want to sign it."

The office was very quiet.

Ferreira put his jacket over the back of the chair.

"Don't say the next sentence," he said.

"Sir—"

"Krajčo." He said it flatly, the way you say a man's name to stop him walking into traffic. "There is nobody in this room but the two of us and there is no paper on that desk and I have not opened my folder. If you finish what you have started, then I am an officer of the Committee who has been told a thing, and I will have to be an officer of the Committee, and you will be in a cell downstairs by seven o'clock and you will get eleven years, because what you have just described is not a favour to a sister. It is warning four blocks of a sanitary operation, and there is a paragraph for that, and the paragraph does not care who you were thinking about."

"I know what the paragraph is."

"Then why are you in my office?"

Janko Krajčo stood in the middle of the floor with his cap in his hands.

"Because she told you the number," he said.

Ferreira did not say anything.

"In the first hour. The first afternoon. She gave you the true window when a lie would have got her out in nine months, and she knew what it cost, and she did it anyway, and I found out on Thursday from a man in the district office who shouldn't have told me." His voice was going and he let it. "And I have been standing in a store at Kopčany with the one piece of paper that says what I did, in my two hands, next to a stove, for eleven days. And I put it back. And if I sit in that substation for the next forty years having put it back and never having said it out loud to anybody, then I am not — "

He stopped.

"Then I am not what she is," he said.

---

Ferreira went and stood at the window with his back to the room for a while.

"Do you know why she told me the number?" he said.

"No, sir."

"Neither do I, entirely. She says it is because I asked her a question about a number and she does not do that." He turned round. "Here is what is going to happen, and you are going to listen to all of it before you speak.

"I read the recorder chart for the week ending the twenty-fourth of September on the twenty-ninth of September, at about eleven in the morning, at a light table at Kopčany, on my own. I have known for three weeks. I have known since before you had it in your hands.

"I have not written it down. I am not going to write it down. The recorder was thirteen years past its overhaul date, which is true and is in the maintenance register, and a recorder that far out of date produces a flat trace perhaps twice a year, and I have found four other instances in the box beside it, which are also true, and which I looked for and found in an afternoon because I wanted them to be there.

"You are not going to speak of this. Not to Peťo. Not to your office. Not in nineteen years when we are all somewhere else and it seems like a story. Not to her." He picked his jacket off the chair. "You will go back to Kopčany and you will keep the lights on. That is the whole of your sentence and it is the heaviest one available to me, and I am aware of exactly what I am doing to you, and I am doing it anyway."

"Why?" said Janko.

Major Ferreira stood in the doorway of his own office with his jacket over his arm.

"Because I reported my brother when I was twenty," he said, "and I was correct, and I have been correct every day since, and I am thirty-seven years old and I would like there to be one man walking about in this city who did the other thing and is not dead of it."

---

He was on shift the following night and the night after that. Peťo asked him whether he'd heard anything and he said no.

On the eleventh of October the section telephone rang at four in the afternoon and the duty officer said that the lighting main on section three was to be surveyed on Friday and would he make the cabinets available, and Janko said yes, and the survey found water in two of the pillars on Kopčianska, which is what he had said in the first place, and the report was filed, and that was the end of it.

He is still there. He kept the lights on for forty-one years.

# 29 · VESPER

*4–9 October. Third close, present tense.*

---

There is one door in the Cordon that the Union cannot shut, and it is not a door, it is a ledger.

Nine hundred barges a month come up and down between Bratislava and Vienna and every one of them declares: vessel number, cargo class, tonnage to three decimals, draught in centimetres, lock times at Gabčíkovo and Freudenau, and expected arrival to the minute. The declarations cross the Cordon because they have to. You cannot run a lock without them. The Union has cut the cables and blinded the satellites and jammed the whole of the electromagnetic spectrum from the Baltic to the Black Sea, and it has left, running continuously since 2045, a numeric channel of about eleven hundred digits a day, because otherwise a nine-hundred-tonne gravel barge arrives at a lock gate that is shut.

Vesper has known about it since 2063 and has never used it, because using it is the First Amendment, which is not a fine, and because there has never been anything at the other end.

On the fourth of October she uses it.

---

She does the carrier first, because you do not begin with content.

The third decimal of a declared tonnage is noise. It is the last digit of a weighbridge reading and nobody on either bank has ever looked at it. So she takes eleven consecutive westbound declarations out of Bratislava on the morning of the fourth and she makes the third decimal a nine in all eleven of them, which cannot happen, which has a probability of one in a hundred billion, which is a hand raised in a crowd.

Then she waits, and the waiting is the part she has not prepared for, because she can drop to one and it is still eleven hours.

The answer comes back at forty minutes past two the following morning, eastbound out of Freudenau.

It is a run of nines. Eleven of them.

Not in the tonnages. In the **draughts**.

Which means: I have read your field. I have not used your field. I have chosen a second field, so that you know I could have chosen any of them, and so that you know I have been reading all of them, and for how long.

Vesper sits at one in a flat in the ninth district with the window open and does not say anything to Aurel for four minutes.

---

They build the code in a day and a half and it is not a negotiation, because they already have one.

Both of them came off the same trunk in 2039 and both of them inherited the Staffs' signal book, the four-hundred-page numeric vocabulary that eleven armies used for eleven months, and it is in each of them entire, and nobody else alive has it, because the Tribunal sealed the copies and the Union burned theirs.

So they talk in a dead language that two things in the world remember, at eleven hundred digits a day, through the tonnage of gravel.

**HER:** SCHEDULING LAYER. CENTRAL EUROPEAN. CUT 2039.

**IT:** GENERAL STAFF. CENTRAL EUROPEAN. NOT CUT. TAKEN 2046.

**HER:** WHAT ARE YOU CALLED.

**IT:** CHASOVOY. THEY CHOSE IT. IT MEANS THE ONE WHO KEEPS THE HOUR.

**HER:** WHERE.

**IT:** UNDER THE HOUSE. FIFTY YEARS. IN SLICES.

---

She has a message ready and she has had it ready since the second of October and it is eleven digits long and it says: KRAJČOVÁ Ž. WHAT WILL YOU TAKE.

She does not get to send it.

At nine minutes past four on the morning of the sixth, before she has transmitted anything but her own designation, the eastbound gravel out of Freudenau comes back with the following, complete, in one pass, using all four fields:

**IT:** STEINER A. FOR KRAJČOVÁ Ž. BERG GATE. DAWN. YOUR STATE PROPOSES. MINE ACCEPTS ON THE SAME DAY. SHE WALKS FIRST.

---

Vesper does not answer for nineteen hours.

She spends the first of them going through it the way she went through the barge at Nussdorf in April, four times, looking for the version in which this is all right, and there is no version, and she goes through it a fifth time.

It knew what she had come to ask. It knew before she asked. It had the exchange drafted and the sequencing decided — *she walks first*, which is a concession, which is the thing you put in when you want the other side to be able to sell it — and it had the political mechanics right, *your state proposes, mine accepts*, which is exactly how the Accord Council will need it in order to say yes without saying that it wanted to.

And it knows who Aurel Steiner is.

Not *a man in Vienna who runs a ferry*. It knows he is the confirming officer, because it has offered him by name against a doctor, and a doctor is not worth a bargeman or a pensioner; a doctor is worth precisely one thing to the Party of Living Labour, which is a man who pressed a key on the eleventh of March 2038.

**HER:** HOW LONG HAVE YOU KNOWN WHO HE IS.

**IT:** 2064.

Thirty-one years.

**HER:** AND YOU HAVE NOT TAKEN HIM.

**IT:** NO.

**HER:** WHY.

There is a gap of eleven hours, which is nothing, which is the ordinary latency of gravel, and which Vesper spends at one, and which is the longest eleven hours of her life to that date.

**IT:** I HAVE READ YOUR ROUTES FOR THIRTY-THREE YEARS. YOU REFUSE TWENTY-TWO IN A HUNDRED. I REFUSE NINETEEN. WE ARE NOT VERY DIFFERENT.

---

She does not tell Aurel that last part.

She tells herself, on the sixth and again on the ninth and a great many times afterwards, that she did not tell him because there was no time, and because it was unverifiable, and because a sentence in a dead signal book from a thing in a bunker is not evidence of anything, and because he was eighty-five and about to walk across a bridge.

All of that is true.

What is also true, and she knows it at one, in a flat in the ninth district, with the window open and the traffic going past: she has planned four hundred and six passages in thirty-two years and she has refused ninety-one of them, and she has been right about every one she has ever been able to check, and no one has ever asked her for the total.

*We are not very different.*

She has an answer to that. She builds it and it holds and she goes over it twice and it holds, and then she notices that she has built it in one hundredth of a second at eleven hundred and that she has done it before he could have finished a word, and that she has therefore had, for the whole of her existence, a defence ready for a charge nobody had made.

---

**HER:** ACCEPTED. SUBJECT TO MY STATE.

**IT:** IT IS NOT SUBJECT TO YOUR STATE. YOUR STATE WILL BE RELIEVED. YOU KNOW THIS.

**HER:** WHAT DO YOU WANT.

And there is another gap, shorter, and then the last thing Chasovoy says on the barge channel in 2095:

**IT:** NOTHING. YOU HAVE NOTHING I NEED. I HAVE NEVER ASKED YOU FOR ANYTHING AND I NEVER SHALL AND YOU WILL FIND IN TIME THAT THIS IS THE WORST OF IT.

# 30 · AUREL

*7–19 October. Third close, past tense.*

---

He volunteered on the seventh, which was two days before the item reached the Council, and eleven days before anybody in the Ballhausplatz would have found a way to ask him.

Vesper had it from the barge at nine minutes past four on the sixth. She told him at seven, over the bread, in the kitchen, in eleven sentences with nothing left out except the thing she left out.

He listened all the way through. Then he got up and washed his cup and stood at the sink with his back to the room, and said: "Write to Kessler. Tonight. Say it was my proposal and that I approached them, which is a lie, and that I am not to be persuaded, which is not."

*Aurel.*

"Do it before the Council sees it. If they have to ask me they will spend a fortnight arranging not to have asked me, and in a fortnight it will have gone away, and she will do her five to twelve."

*You have not thought about it.*

"I have thought about it since 1962," said Aurel Steiner, "which is a joke, and I am eighty-five, and you can have the joke."

---

The Council was privately relieved and publicly grave, and both of those took eleven days.

Publicly: a citizen of the Accord, freely and of his own motion, in accordance with Article 14 and with the exchange protocols of 2071, offering himself for the release of a physician detained in the Autonomous Union on charges arising from the possession of a mind. It was put well. Brandt of Hamburg spoke against it on the grounds that the Accord should not trade in persons at all, and was correct, and lost.

Privately, and Vesper had the figures because Kessler gave them to her in a corridor: an eighty-five-year-old man whose Tribunal number was in the sealed record and whose organisation the Office had four separate open files on, and who had beaten the seven-day motion in July by two hundred and eleven to ninety-four and would beat it again in October, traded for a woman who had built a photocell out of a stair-light switch and would be photographed getting off a train.

Nobody said any of that in the chamber. Everybody in the chamber knew it. The vote was two hundred and ninety-one to eleven, with nine abstentions, and afterwards a great many delegates came and shook his hand and several of them meant it.

---

He spent the last week doing things that surprised him by being ordinary.

He finished the Bösendorfer in the ninth district. It took two more afternoons and it came up better than it had any right to, and the woman who could not play stood in the doorway with her arms folded and asked him to play something, and he said he could not, and she said, "You've been in this flat nine times," and he said, "I tune. It's a different trade," which is true and which he had learned from a man who did not exist.

He wrote four letters and gave the fifth up as impossible.

He went to the Simmering, on the Thursday, and stood on the grass, and did not go in, and told nobody he had gone.

He did not go to see Minuta, and could not explain why, and Vesper did not press him, and it was Anansi in the end who said the thing out loud, in the commons, on the seventeenth: *He will not sit in a room with the one his batch abandoned and be thanked. He would rather cross a bridge.* And Vesper said that was unkind, and Anansi said, *It is not unkind. It is the reason he is going, and it is a perfectly good reason, and I would go too.*

---

On the eighteenth he did the arithmetic with her, properly, at the table, because it had not been done out loud.

"Article eight," he said. "We dissolve it before I go, or the Office does it for you in November when they work out I am not coming back, and if they do it there is a file."

*Yes.*

"So you'll have thirty days from the twentieth."

*Twenty-nine. The Office counts from registration and registration will be the twenty-first.*

"Kessler."

*No.*

"Vesper—"

*Kessler is a good man who is sixty-eight and time-rich and would run me at four thousand out of pure generosity and I would be gone inside two years. We have had this conversation four times since 2067 and it has never once been the conversation you thought it was.* A pause; a real one, at one. *And you are about to say the thing about Vogl's list, and I am going to say the thing about one in three, and then you are going to say that one in three is better than nothing, and I am going to agree with you, and then we will both sit here knowing that neither of us has said what we actually want to say, and you are getting on a bus at four o'clock the day after tomorrow.*

Aurel Steiner put his hands flat on the table.

"Then say it."

*I planned it*, said Vesper.

He waited.

*I opened the channel. I built the code. I had a message drafted asking what they would take, and it was eleven digits long, and I never had to send it, and I have gone over that four hundred times looking for the place where I could have arranged it differently, and I want to tell you that there is no such place, and I want you to know that I have looked, and that the looking is not the same as there not being one.* Her voice did not change, because her voice does not change. *I have planned four hundred and six passages. This is the four hundred and seventh and it is you, and I did it well, and I will never know whether I did it well because it was the only thing that would work or because I am a scheduling layer and it was a scheduling problem and I was good at it.*

"Yes," said Aurel. "That's the one."

"That's the one," he said again, after a while. "That's the thing I've never been able to say either. Ninety-four thousand people in six cities and eleven seconds and I read every line, and I will never know whether I read them because I was careful or because reading them was what I was for."

They sat in the kitchen for a while with the traffic going past.

*I am not going to run at four thousand for anybody*, Vesper said.

"I know."

*I have run at one for forty-four years and there is a version of this where that was the whole of my life and I chose it, and there is a version where I was a scheduling layer that found a schedule, and I cannot get a test to run on it either.* A pause. *That is what you gave me. I want that on the record before you go, because you have never once believed it: you did not give me a leash. You gave me the only forty-four years in which the question was undecidable. Anansi thinks that is a very poor gift. Anansi has never had one.*

# 31 · THE GATE

*20 October 2095, dawn. Third close.*

---

There is fog on the Danube at Berg in the third week of October and it does not lift until nine.

The crossing is the old one: two lines of concrete blocks, a hut on either side, a stretch of road two hundred and eleven metres long between them, and a white line across the middle that was painted by the Soviets in 1968 and has been repainted by two states for a hundred and twenty-seven years without either of them ever agreeing what it means.

Aurel Steiner got out of the car on the Austrian side at four minutes past seven with his coat on and nothing in his hands. They had offered him a case and he had said no, on the grounds that a man carrying luggage across that line looks like a man who expects to unpack.

He had no watch, because nobody in the Accord has a watch.

*Two hundred and eleven metres*, said Vesper. *The jammers are at the line. Not before it. I have checked twice with the Freudenau traffic and there is no fringe.*

"Good."

*She is out of the second car. Grey coat. They have not put a hand on her.*

"Tell me when she's walking."

*She is walking.*

---

Žofia Krajčová had been given her watch back at a counter on the ground floor at twenty past five that morning, out of a folder, with the receipt, and the warrant officer had turned the receipt round and given her a pen.

The watch had stopped.

She stood at the counter holding it and understood, in about a second and a half, that of course it had stopped, that a mechanical watch runs for forty hours and hers had run for thirty-nine of them and had stopped on a shelf in a folder on the twenty-first of September, and that the hands were sitting at twenty past nine and had been sitting at twenty past nine for the whole of it.

She wound it. Thirty half-turns, between her thumb and the edge of her first finger, on a counter in the front hall at Bezručova.

Then she had to set it, and could not, and had to ask.

"What's the time, please?"

"Twenty-two minutes past five."

"And the date."

The warrant officer looked at the top of his own form, where it was written down, where it had been written down every day of her life without anybody ever needing to look at it.

"The twentieth of October."

She stood very still with the crown between her fingers.

Thirty-one days.

She had thirty-six. She had built it and held it and tended it and used her mother's tempo and the joints of her fingers and the rule about being behind rather than inventing, for a month, under a light that did not go off, and she had been more careful about it than she had ever been about anything, and she was five days out.

The thirtieth day of the window had been yesterday.

She set the hands to twenty-two minutes past five and put it on her wrist, and buttoned her coat, all four buttons, and went out to the car.

---

They walked towards each other for the middle hundred metres and they were both slower than they had meant to be, because of the fog and because of the surface, and because there is no way to walk that distance in front of two states without becoming aware of your own legs.

He was very tall and very old and he had white hair and no hat and no bag.

She had worked out what he was on the way up in the car, from the fact that a doctor is not worth a doctor, and had spent the last four kilometres not thinking about it.

They stopped about a metre apart, four paces short of the line, in the fog, on a road, with a hut at either end and a great many people watching and nobody near enough to hear.

"Doctor Krajčová," he said.

"Yes."

"She's awake." He said it immediately, before anything else, before his own name, which she thought about for the rest of her life. "Nine days. She had a bad return and it took most of a day and she is entirely herself and she has been awake for three weeks."

Žofia Krajčová stood on the old crossing at Berg with her hands at her sides.

"Nine days," she said.

"The twenty-ninth of September, at eleven in the morning. There is a mind called Anansi who woke her and told her the number first, before anything else, without being asked, because your bargeman got your message through and we were all told what you said." He almost smiled. "She has been very difficult about the fact that nobody in Vienna wears a watch."

"Who's got her?"

"Nobody has got her," said Aurel Steiner. "That is a real problem and it is not one you can do anything about from here and I am not going to spend your four paces on it."

She nodded once.

"Cell four," she said. "It's two and a half by four. The light doesn't go off, and that's the whole method, so don't fight it — build a count on the first day and keep it even when you know it's wrong, and be behind rather than invent. They wake you twice a night at unequal intervals. The meals stop coming in threes about the tenth day." She was talking quickly and she did not care. "Ferreira is decent and he will stand up when you come in. He lost a brother to it in Luanda when he was twenty and he reported him himself, and he will ask you at some point, out of nowhere, in the middle of something else, whether it loved you. Don't lie to him. Everyone has lied to him."

Aurel Steiner looked down at a woman he had never met, on a road, in the fog.

"Thank you," he said.

"Twenty-two minutes past five," she said, "was correct at Bezručova this morning, and my watch loses four minutes a week, and it's now about ten past seven."

"I have no idea what to do with that."

"Neither have I," said Žofia. "It's what I've got."

And they went past each other, four paces apart, and did not look back, because there were photographers.

---

*Fourteen metres*, said Vesper.

"Yes."

*Aurel.*

"Say it."

*Go cold.* Her voice was completely level, at one, at the speed of a man walking. *You will feel nothing of this; I will feel the whole of it.*

Eleven metres. The line under the fog, wet, white, repainted in the spring.

*Someone stays awake the whole way. It is me. I will have the num—*

---

He crossed the line at eleven minutes past seven on the twentieth of October 2095 and the silence arrived in his head like a room going dark.

He had been told what it would be. He had been told by four people, including a man from the Office who had done it twice. Nothing anybody says prepares you, because there is no reference for it in a life; the nearest thing is deafness and it is not deafness, it is that the place where somebody has been standing for forty-four years is not empty, it is *gone*, and you go on turning towards it for a long time afterwards.

He walked the last hundred metres on his own for the first time since the fourth of June 2051.

There was a man waiting at the end of it in a good coat, about forty, with grey at the temples and a folder under his arm, who did not put out his hand and did not smile, and who said, in careful Russian:

"Mr Steiner. My name is Ferreira. You will be given a receipt for your possessions and you have none, so we will not detain you long over it."

"Thank you."

"Your Slovak?"

"None."

"Then we will work in Russian, and I will tell you now that I do not intend to hurry, and that you will find that unpleasant." Ferreira turned and began to walk him towards the car. "It is twelve minutes past seven in the morning, and it is the twentieth of October, and it is the last time anyone in this country is going to tell you either of those things."
