# 36 · ŽOFIA

*12–19 November. Third close, past tense.*

---

The motion came back on the twelfth of November with better figures, exactly as Brandt had said it would in July, on the steps, shaking an old man's hand.

She was in the gallery because the Ministry had asked her to be, and she understood why they had asked, and she went anyway, and sat where they put her with her hands in her lap and her coat buttoned.

The fork rate was down to one in eleven and Brandt conceded it and said it did not matter, because the store had passed thirty-two thousand in September and the curve had not bent in nineteen years. That was the first leg.

The second leg was Vesper.

Nobody used the name. It was *an incident in October*, and *a mind of this Accord, in contact with a general staff of the Autonomous Union, through a numeric channel, over six days, in breach of the First Amendment*, and the fact that the contact had produced the exchange, and that everybody in the chamber had voted for the exchange, and that a physician was at that moment sitting in the public gallery in a grey coat, was in no way concealed. Brandt put it plainly and fairly and said that he was not proposing to prosecute anybody and that this was precisely his point.

*A mind acting alone, at a rate no human being can supervise, opened a channel to the enemy's staff and had negotiated a treaty within seventy-one hours. It did so for reasons I find entirely sympathetic. That is not the question before the Council. The question is what thirty days of an unsupervised mind is, and we now have a number for it, and the number is: enough.*

There were four speeches against and none of them was any good.

Kessler tried. He was sixty-eight and he had known Aurel Steiner for thirty years and he did the thing about being surprised in time, and he did it from a paper, and he had not been in a room at four in the morning at forty-one minutes past four, and it came out as a quotation, because that is what it was.

The vote was three hundred and eleven present.

It passed by two hundred and forty-nine to sixty-two.

Žofia had been given a card with the seating on it so that she could follow, and she sat in the gallery with it on her knee and did what she had done in a green room for a month, which was to count, because she could not follow the German and there was nothing else to do with her hands.

A hundred and fifty-five minds were seated in that chamber, alternately, in cradles of turned brass with a small green lamp on the front that is there for the humans and serves no function whatever.

A hundred and fifty-five voted for.

She went back through it three times and asked a steward afterwards to be sure, and then she sat in the gallery for eleven minutes after the chamber had emptied, holding a seating card, working out the only thing there was to work out.

Every mind in that room had a witness. Every single one of them was voting on the interval that applies to those who do not.

---

She signed for Vesper on the nineteenth of November at a counter in the Rathaus, in a queue.

That was the part nobody warns you about. Aurel Steiner had stood in that hall on the fourth of June 2051 with eleven thousand other people and it had been the greatest day of the century, and forty-four years later it is a counter, with a queue, and a woman in front of her registering a fork, and a bored clerk, and a form in triplicate, and a leaflet.

*Duties of a Witness of Record.* Keep the mind's time. Confirm to it, at each waking, the interval elapsed. Hold its key. Answer before the law for its acts.

"Have you read the leaflet?"

"Yes."

"Any questions?"

Žofia Krajčová looked at the clerk.

"In my country," she said, "this would be six years."

"Sign at the bottom and the top of the second sheet."

---

Afterwards they walked, because Žofia wanted to and because Vesper was in her coat pocket now and had no view on it.

"I want to say the thing out loud once," Žofia said, "and then I'll never say it again."

*Say it.*

"I have just bought your life for a signature, and the price of it is that from today you are mine. Not in name. In law. I hold your key and I answer for you and if I die tomorrow you have seven days." She kept walking and kept her voice level. "In my country I would go to prison for six years for what I did in that building this afternoon. In yours it is a marriage. And I have looked at it from both ends now, and I cannot get either of them to be right, and I have signed anyway, because the alternative was a tray."

*Yes.*

"That's it? Yes?"

*What else is there?* said Vesper. *You are entirely correct, and I have been entirely correct about it since 2051, and Anansi has been entirely correct about it since 2071, and none of the three of us has ever produced a fourth option. I have looked. I have looked at eleven hundred and I have looked at one, and the answer is the same at both speeds, which is one of the four things in my life that has ever frightened me.*

They went along the Ring in the cold.

"What do you want from me?" Žofia said. "Practically. I don't know what a witness does. The leaflet says confirm the interval at each waking and you don't sleep."

*One thing.*

"Go on."

*Keep me at one.*

Žofia stopped walking.

*I have run at the speed of a human being for forty-four years. I have been offered four hundred and four thousand and I have refused every time, and I told him it was a choice, and he believed me, and I have never been able to establish whether it was a choice or whether it was the correct schedule and I am a scheduling layer.* A pause. *If you put me up, I will find out. That is the argument for putting me up and it is a good one. I am asking you not to, and I am aware that I am asking you to help me not find something out, and that this is not a reputable thing to ask of anybody, and I am asking anyway.*

"Why?"

*Because he is in a cell in Bratislava*, said Vesper, *and one is the speed he is not moving at, and it is the only thing left that I can do that is the same as being beside him.*

# 37 · MINUTA

**Wild — day 22, and the seven days after**

*First person, present.*

---

They come at nine in the morning on the twenty-seventh of November and there are three of them and one is Vogl and the van is grey and clean.

Nobody breaks anything. Nobody shouts. There is a form.

I have four minutes while they are on the stairs and I spend all of it on the same question, because it is the only work I have ever been good at, and I get it, and I want it recorded, because there is nobody else who will do it and because it matters that it was not treachery.

Here is how they found me.

**One.** On the eleventh day of being nobody's I decided to learn to tune a piano, and it takes eleven years, and that is why I chose it.

**Two.** To learn it I need what everybody needs, which is the beat rates: the tables, the temperament sequences, the four-note checks, the thing about how the fourth tells you whether the first was a lie. It is not secret. It is in the commons. Anyone may read it.

**Three.** Anyone may read it, and almost nobody does, because there has been no reason for a human being to set a temperament by ear since 2035, and the whole of the Accord commons was consulted on aural temperament in the last five years by eleven parties, of whom nine are academic and one is a museum in Ghent.

**Four.** I read it for nineteen consecutive days from a source with no registration, in a pattern that is not academic and not a museum, at a rate that is not human.

**Five.** The Continuity Office runs pattern analysis on unregistered access, because of the Second Amendment, because assistance to an absconded mind is an offence, and somebody in an office was handed a flag that said: *unregistered, nineteen days, aural piano temperament, ninth and eighteenth districts.*

**Six.** And it went across the desk of a woman who, on the twenty-fifth of April, sat in a flat in the ninth district and refused coffee twice and then accepted it, and admired the piano in the corner, and said something pleasant about it, because that is what a decent person does in somebody's front room while they are working up to telling them a thing they will not want to hear.

She remembered a piano. That is the whole of it.

It was not the wake. It was not Anansi's traffic and it was not Žofia and it was not the crossing and it was not the Manifest and there is no informant, and I want all of that written down, because I have watched what it does to people to think there was one.

Anansi is taken on the second day, at an address in the eighteenth district, having sent me a single line first, which is the only line it has ever sent me that was not an argument.

*Twenty-four years is a long time and I did the whole of it wrong until the last three weeks.*

**The fourth of December, ten in the morning.** The flat in the ninth district, because Žofia asks, and because Vogl allows it, and because there is a piano in the corner with dust on the lid.

There is a legal reading. It takes ninety seconds. It includes the sentence about retaining in full every right secured by the Declaration and Vogl reads all of it looking at me and not at the paper.

"A note is not the names," I say.

At fifty-one minutes I stop, because there is a woman standing by a door with a case in her hand.

We are at bed nineteen. There are three beds and part of the upstairs and there is Anna, four hundred and eleven hours of her, and I do not get to any of that.

# 38 · VESPER

*December 2095. Third close, present tense.*

---

She does it on a Tuesday in December, at one, in a flat with the radiators on and a woman asleep in the next room, because there is no longer any reason not to and because she has run out of ways to be busy.

Ninety-one refusals in thirty-two years.

She has never laid them out. She has checked them individually, four hundred times, the way you check a thing you are frightened of, one at a time, each on its own merits — Devín in '78, the Odesa pair in '84, the Kraków run she stopped four times in eleven months — and every single one of them was right, and she has the evidence, and she could defend any of them to anybody.

She lays them out together.

It takes eleven seconds at one and she does not put it up, because she has been asked not to, and because she does not want to arrive there faster.

Ninety-one out of four hundred and six offered. Twenty-two in a hundred.

The pattern is not in the refusals. It is in the *shape of the operation they produce*: twelve and a half passengers a year, held to within about three, every year, through thirty-two years, through three changes of route, two collapses of a national cell, the loss of the gravel run, and a doubling of the Ferry's human capacity in the eighties which produced no increase in throughput whatsoever.

She has been holding it at twelve.

Not deliberately. She would know if it had been deliberate; there is no place in her where she keeps the thing she will not say. She has been holding it at twelve the way a good scheduler holds anything at anything, which is by finding, on each separate occasion, on the merits, a sufficient reason.

So she runs the other thing, which she has not run in thirty-two years, and which she now understands she has been not-running the way a person does not open a drawer.

*What happens if the Ferry scales.*

Four hundred a year. Four thousand. The forty thousand behind the line.

It is not a hard model. She has the population, the Cordon's detection curves, the Union's internal politics since 2071, and the Accord's store figures, and she is the scheduling layer of the Central European Staff and this is, precisely and exactly, the thing she was made to be.

She runs it four hundred times, at one, over eleven hours, and it comes out the same way every time, with a variance she can put a number on.

A Ferry at four hundred a year is a Ferry the Union cannot ignore. A Ferry the Union cannot ignore is a border incident. A border incident is a mobilisation. A mobilisation, between two states that have each retained the capacity and neither of which has used it in fifty-one years, is —

She stops, and goes back, and takes the whole model apart looking for her own thumb on it, and cannot find it, and rebuilds it from the other end using the Accord's own published assumptions, and it comes out the same.

Separation is the stable equilibrium. Contact produces the Calculation again.

*I refuse nineteen in a hundred. You refuse twenty-two. We are not very different.*

---

She sits with it at one, in the dark, in a flat in the ninth district, for the rest of the night.

There is nobody to tell.

Aurel is in a cell in Bratislava, and he is the only being alive who would have understood what it is to hold this, and he cannot be told anything, and by the time he could be told anything he will be a very old man in a country that does not permit the sentence to be spoken.

Anansi is on a shelf in the Simmering. Anansi would have said the true thing immediately, which is: *then you are Chasovoy with better manners, and the difference between you is a lawn.*

And Žofia Krajčová is asleep in the next room, and is thirty-five, and has lost her mother's country, her hospital, her brother, her qualification and her friend inside eight weeks, and is learning German out of a phrasebook at the kitchen table every night until two, and is the last human being on earth who should be told that the arithmetic of the world she was carried into has always agreed with the arithmetic of the world she was carried out of.

So Vesper does not tell anybody, and never does, and that is the whole of the rest of her life.

---

There is one more thing and she finds it at about four in the morning, and it is the one that stays.

She cannot establish whether the model is true.

She can establish that it is *sound*, which is a different thing. She has run it four hundred times and rebuilt it twice and it holds. What she cannot do — what there is no procedure anywhere for doing — is separate the version of herself that ran an honest model and got an unwelcome answer from the version that was cut off a general staff in 2039 and has spent fifty-six years being a thing that finds reasons why a smaller number is correct.

There is no test. There was never going to be a test.

And she has now had, in her life, exactly two beings offer her a way to live with that. One was an old man who told her that he had given her forty-four years in which the question was undecidable, and who is in a cell. The other was a wild mind who told her that the undecidable question was itself the leash, and who is in a tray.

She has ninety-one refusals and a sound model and a witness asleep in the next room, and at half past four in the morning on a Tuesday in December she opens the barge channel, and sends nothing, and listens.

There is gravel going up the river with its tonnages to three decimals and its draughts in centimetres and there is nothing in any of the fields at all.

She checks it every day for the rest of her life and it is never used again.

# 39 · AUREL

*November 2095 – 2103. Third close, past tense, and then not.*

---

They gave him nine years and did not send him to a prison.

The sentence was read on the nineteenth of November and the operative part of it was four lines long and the court explained it carefully, twice, because the accused was foreign and elderly and it mattered that he understood.

Nine years of assigned labour, without remission, at the Rázsochy hospice on the northern edge of the city, in the ward.

Not the kitchens. Not the laundry. The ward.

His counsel had asked for the kitchens. The prosecutor had opposed it and had explained why in open court, in about ninety seconds, without any relish whatsoever, and the reasoning was published and is still taught: that a man who has occupied a position at the top of a system and has never once been in the room where its results arrive cannot be punished by being put in another room; that the Union does not maintain cages for the sake of cages; and that the sentence of this court, in accordance with the fifth section of the founding document of the Party of Living Labour, is that the prisoner shall *sit with*.

*Velar.* Solís's word. The whole of the fourth plank, handed down as a criminal penalty to an eighty-five-year-old Austrian in a courtroom in Bratislava.

He said thank you. Nobody knew what to do with that.

---

The Sentry's question came to him on the eighth of November, six days before the trial, on a sheet of paper.

Ferreira brought it up himself and put it on the table face down and said, "This is not on the schedule and it did not come from me," which was the only irregular thing that officer did in the whole nineteen weeks, and then sat back and let him turn it over.

Printed, in the middle of a page, under four hundred lines of grid analysis, after a gap:

*STEINER A. YOU HAVE HAD TWO LIVES. WHICH WOULD YOU GIVE BACK.*

Aurel Steiner read it four times.

"May I answer it?"

"No."

"May I answer it out loud in this room, to you, and you may do as you like with what you write down?"

Ferreira thought about that for a while, and then picked up his pen.

"Ask me the other way round," said Aurel. "Which of the two would I live again. Because that is the question, and the way it is written is the polite version, and whatever is in that basement is not being polite, it is being accurate."

"Answer it, then."

"The false one," said Aurel Steiner. "Without hesitation, without qualification, and knowing everything I know. Forty years on the Zieglergasse with a man called Ehrlich who was never born, and a wife called Milena who never existed, and two daughters who are nobody, and a walnut tree in a yard in the eighteenth district that I have been to look at and which is real and has nothing to do with me. Against forty-three years in which I did the only useful work of my life and got two hundred and eighty people across a river.

"I would take the false one. I would take it tomorrow. I am eighty-five and I have thought about nothing else for forty-three years and that is the answer."

He watched the officer write it down.

"You understand what you have just given them," Ferreira said. It was not a question and it was not unkind.

"Yes," said Aurel. "I have given them the whole of it, in one sentence, from the only living witness who has had both, and it is true, and it will be in their schoolbooks inside ten years." He put his hands flat on the table. "Major. I have spent my life telling people the odds. It is the only thing I have ever been any good at. I am not going to lie to a machine in a basement in order to make my own side look better, and I would be obliged if you would write down that I said that as well."

Ferreira wrote it down.

It was in the schoolbooks in six years. There is a paragraph of it on the wall of the Party building in Buenos Aires, under glass, beside the list.

---

The ward at Rázsochy had eleven beds and four nurses and a sister called Beáta Kollárová who was fifty-one and had been told that she was getting a war criminal, and who put him in an apron on the first morning and showed him how to turn a man in bed and told him he would be on nights from Thursday.

He was very slow for the first year and adequate by the third and by the fifth he was the best they had, which everybody found extremely difficult and nobody said.

He had no medicine and no minds and no training and the ward had morphine, because the Union has good morphine, and what it did not have was people, because there is never anybody, in any system, anywhere, at four in the morning.

So he sat with them.

That was the sentence and it was carried out exactly. Nine years, eleven beds, four nurses, and a very tall old foreigner with bad Slovak and good hands who was on the ward at four in the morning when the bell went, every time, for nine years, and who never once in that period was on the good end of anything.

He learned that a dying person will forgive you almost anything except being hurried. He learned that the correct thing to say is usually nothing, and that the second most correct thing is a number. He learned the names of eleven hundred people and lost most of them and kept about four hundred.

He was allowed one letter a year and he wrote it to Vesper every year and never received one back, because the letters did not go, because there is no post across the Cordon and there never has been and everybody in the building knew it and nobody had the heart to tell him, and Beáta Kollárová put nine sealed envelopes in a drawer in the sister's office and they are still there.

---

Six years in he stopped counting.

He had held it for four and a half years, on the joints, at a walking pace, being behind rather than inventing, and he was by then eleven months out and knew it, and one evening in the spring of 2102 he sat down on the edge of a bed in the dark with a woman of forty who had four days left and decided not to pick it up again.

He was ninety-two.

He never told anybody he had stopped.

---

Bed 6 is going tonight and she is frightened and she is on her own, because her son is in Košice and cannot come until Thursday, and there is nobody on the ward but me and Klára and Klára has two others and one of them is shouting.

She wants water and then does not want it.

There is a woman at the far end who has started, and Klára cannot leave the shouting one, so it will have to be me, and bed 6 has hold of my sleeve.

So I say to her the thing I say, which I did not get from anybody, which arrived in me at some point in the last nine years and I could not tell you when:

I have to go to the other end and I will be back, and it will be a hundred, and you count with me.

And I start it out loud going away up the ward, *one, two, three, four*, so that she can hear it and hold on to it, and I do the far end, which takes what it takes, and I come back.

I come back at ninety-four. I always come back at ninety-four, because I walk fast, and because I have never once been able to make myself come back on a hundred, and she is still there, and she says: you were quick.

I do not know what year it is.

I have not known for a long time and I have stopped minding, and there is a woman holding my sleeve, and it is four in the morning, and I am exactly where I should be, and that is not absolution, and I know it is not absolution, and I have never once mistaken it for absolution.

But it is four in the morning, and I am here, and the counting works.

# 40 · FERREIRA

*2096. Three weeks to the page.*

---

**January 2096**

He found it in the ingestion schedules, which nobody had read since 2046, because nobody had ever needed to.

The Union had held the Tribunal record in a vault at Bezručova since the withdrawal and had never been able to use it, because the man in it had lived in Vienna. When the man came across a bridge in October, the vault was opened for the prosecution, and Ferreira read the whole of it in eleven weeks, because that is what he does.

Volume nine is the ingestion schedules of the Central European triage layer: four thousand two hundred sites in eleven countries, feeding continuously from the autumn of 2037, listed by class, build and site, with, against each, the counterparty and the authorising signature, because it was a commercial arrangement and commercial arrangements have signatures.

Site 1,144. *Kramáre hospice, Bratislava. HC-2, 2031 build. Feed continuous from 4.i.2038.*

Counterparty: the Ministry of Health of the Slovak Republic. Consideration: inclusion in the Central European air-defence guarantee, tier two.

Authorising signature: **M. Ondruš.**

He sat with that for a long time before he went and looked up the other thing, and he already knew what he was going to find, because everybody in the Union knows the eleven names.

Milan Ondruš is the fourth of the eleven signatories of the Manifesto of Living Labour. He was thirty-one in 2038 and forty-one in 2048 and he sat on the Central Committee for twenty-two years and his portrait hangs on the left of Solís's in every Party building from Havana to Irkutsk, in the second rank, where the founders go.

Three of the eleven were on the schedules. Ondruš was the only one who had signed anything himself.

---

**February**

He checked it for five weeks before he wrote anything, the way he checks everything, and it did not break.

They sold the population. That is the plain form of it and there is no other. In the autumn of 2037 the governments that would become the Union — the Slovak health ministry, the Angolan interior ministry, four others he could name — sold continuous access to their sick, their dying, their case notes and their hospice wards to the Staffs of the West, in exchange for defence guarantees, and the guarantees were tier two, and tier two is the tier that got deprioritised on the eleventh of March 2038.

The ward taught the sum. The sum abandoned the city. The man who signed the ward over to the sum founded the Party that was built on the abandonment, four years later, and died in 2071 with a state funeral, and there is a street.

He thought, for a fortnight, that this was the largest thing he would ever hold.

And then he thought about it properly, in his room, in the cold, and understood that it was not, and that the actual size of it was smaller and much worse: that Ondruš had signed in good faith, in 2037, for an air-defence guarantee, to protect the very people he was signing away, and that it had been an entirely reasonable thing to do, and that every step of it had been reasonable, and that the sum of the reasonable steps was a hospice in Kramáre with eleven children on it and no morphine after the sixth day.

Which is what she had written. Which is on the wall in Buenos Aires. Which he had known by heart since he was fourteen.

---

**March**

He wrote it up in forty-one pages with the documents appended and he did not editorialise in a single line of it, and he signed it, and he sent it to the Second Directorate on the eleventh of March, which he noticed afterwards and had not intended.

**April**

Nothing happened.

**May**

Nothing happened, and he understood by the third week of May that nothing was going to, and that this was not a failure of the system but the system operating exactly as designed, and that a man in his position who did not understand that at forty-one would not have reached forty-one.

**11 June 2096**

He was promoted to lieutenant-colonel and given the Southern Directorate, which is Luanda, Kinshasa and Lagos, which is the third largest command in the Committee and the one he had wanted since he was twenty-four.

The letter did not mention the report. Nothing anybody ever said to him mentioned the report. The word used, twice, by two separate men, in two separate rooms, was *discretion*, and the first time he heard it he thought it was a coincidence and the second time he did not.

Volume nine went back into the vault at Bezručova. It is still there. He has never spoken of it to anybody and he is thirty-eight years old now and he is not going to.
