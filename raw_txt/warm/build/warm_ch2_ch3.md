# 2 · ŽOFIA

*Third close, past tense. Ordinary novelistic time.*

---

She slept from four until noon, badly, and woke with the whole of it waiting for her at the foot of the bed like a dog.

The bin was where she had left it. She had put the lid on before she went to bed and she could not now remember doing it, which frightened her more than anything else about the night had.

She did not open it on the Wednesday.

She got up, and washed, and ate a plate of bread and dripping standing at the window, and went down to the shop for potatoes and stood in the queue for twenty-five minutes behind two women arguing amiably about a third, and came back up, and the whole time there was a second person inside her doing arithmetic. Monday the eighteenth. Ten days. Thursday. She was on nights Wednesday and she was on nights Thursday, twenty hundred to eight, which meant that the thing would finish itself at some point in the middle of her second shift, while she was three kilometres away with her hands inside somebody.

She thought: good. That is the cleanest possible arrangement and I did not even have to construct it.

She thought that on and off for eleven hours and did not believe it once.

---

The Wednesday shift was ordinary. A hip, an incarcerated hernia at three in the morning, a woman of eighty who was frightened of the mask and had to be talked down and afterwards held Žofia's hand and told her about her son in Nitra. She did the pain round. She wrote her notes in her small backward-sloping hand under the light of a bench lamp with a green shade that had been in that office since before she was born.

She was very good at her job that night, in the particular way you are good at your job when you are using it to hold a door shut.

At six she went down to the canteen and drank tea and looked at the framed list on the way, because she now looked at it every time, and understood that this was going to be permanent.

---

Thursday she slept until four in the afternoon and woke knowing exactly what she was going to do, which is a thing that happens to people who have spent two days deciding not to decide.

She got the book out of the bin and read it properly, front to back, at the kitchen table, with a pencil, the way she would read a set of notes on a patient she was inheriting.

Most of it was operational and dry and she understood perhaps three-quarters of it. The last four pages were not.

*Where it is safe to run: anywhere the draw is already mad. A hospital. A laundry. A foundry. A pumping station. A block of flats is the worst place on earth for this and I have used one for fifty-four years because it was the place I had.*

*A hospital is the best place in this city and I want you to sit with that for a while before you use it.*

She sat with it for a while.

It was, she thought, a filthy piece of reasoning, and it was also correct. Ružinov drew like a drunk. Sterilisers, lifts, theatre lighting, the laundry, the kitchens, four hundred sockets and a night registrar who boiled the same kettle at whatever hour she felt like it, which was the point, which was the whole point: irregularity is expensive to counterfeit and a hospital produces it free, all night, for nothing, as a by-product of being a hospital.

The safest place in Bratislava to commit this particular crime was a building owned by the Ministry of Health and staffed by four hundred people who would all, every one of them, have reported her.

*Short bursts only on the small rig. Five minutes and then it must sit for two hours. The tape gets rid of the heat into whatever you can find. If you cannot find anything, do not run it. There is no clever way round this and men who thought there was are not here.*

And, on the last page, in a different pencil, harder:

*If you are reading this you have until Thursday. I am sorry to have done it this way. If I had left you a week you would have thought about it for a week and thinking is what kills people in this. Either she is a person or she is not, and you cannot find that out by sitting in a chair.*

Žofia read that four times.

Then she packed her bag for the shift: her tin of instruments, her book, a clean tunic, a wrapped slice of bread, and an enamel bread bin from Košice with a green line round the lid.

She told herself, on the trolleybus, that she was taking it in so that it would not be alone.

She was aware, at the same time and in the same head, that this was the sort of thing people say to themselves at the beginning, and that it was the exact shape of the sentence her mother's generation had used about small favours from the machines, and that if the district committee had asked her to describe how a person begins she would have described this, accurately, at length, from a poster.

She took it anyway. It went in her locker behind the coats.

---

At twenty past ten the bell went for the second-floor children's ward, and she was up the stairs before the second ring because the note it makes for a child is different.

Bed 4, side bay. Matúš Doležal, seven years old, second post-operative day, splenectomy on the Tuesday after falling off the back of a trailer at his grandmother's.

He was grey and he was quiet and that was what she noticed first and did not like, because a seven-year-old who is properly ill is a seven-year-old who complains.

Lenka, the registrar, was already there and had her stethoscope on his chest and her face was doing what her face did, which was a thing Žofia had seen before with children and had chosen, in the past, to be kind about.

"He's spiked," Lenka said. "Thirty-eight two. He's flat, his chest is quiet at the right base, I think we're looking at a collection, I've sent bloods and I want a film."

Žofia put her fingers on the boy's foot.

Cold to the ankle. She pressed his kneecap for five seconds and watched the white mark sit there like a thumbprint in dough.

"Blood pressure?"

"Ninety-four over sixty. It's been ninety-something all evening."

Which is what a child's blood pressure does. A child will hold a beautiful blood pressure while he empties, hold it and hold it out of pure young elastic vessels, and hold it, and then not hold it, and the not-holding takes about ninety seconds and there is no second chance in it. The thing you watch is not the pressure. The thing you watch is the pulse, over hours, climbing.

Žofia picked up the chart off the end of the bed.

Four-hourly observations, ruled columns, a nurse's hand. Temperature. Respirations. Pulse.

18:00 — 112. 20:00 — 108. 22:00 — 114.

She stood there with the chart in both hands and something went through her that she could not name at the time and identified, days afterwards, as recognition.

The numbers were fine. They were completely fine. And a film would take forty minutes and the bloods would take an hour, and if she was wrong about the feeling in her chest she would have taken a septic child to theatre for nothing and the whole ward would know by Monday, and if she was right and she waited for the film he would arrest on the trolley in the corridor.

"Get a line in the other arm," she said. "I'm two minutes."

The linen store is between the bay and the stairs. It has no window, a light with a pull cord, a socket for the floor polisher, and a door that does not lock.

She went in and shut it and got the bin out of her bag.

---

Four minutes later she came out with her coat over her arm and went to the end of the bed and put two fingers on the boy's neck and counted, herself, for a full sixty seconds, by her grandmother's watch.

A hundred and sixty-eight.

"Lenka. Feel his foot."

The registrar felt his foot.

"Ring theatre," Žofia said. "Tell them I'm bringing a seven-year-old with a bleed, now, and I want two units of O in the room before I am. Then ring Mr Vrábel at home and tell him I've said it's a slipped tie."

"The obs—"

"Are wrong," Žofia said. "I'm sorry. They're wrong."

---

It was a slipped tie. It had been going since about six o'clock, quietly, into the abdomen, in the way that produces a child who is cold and polite and does not complain. Vrábel had it in eleven minutes and the boy took four units and was extubated at half past three and asked, at four, whether his grandmother knew.

Žofia sat with him until he went back to sleep and then went out into the corridor and put her back against the wall and stayed there for a while.

---

Klaudia was nineteen, in her second year, and had been covering two bays since two in the afternoon because Marta's mother had died and nobody had been found to replace her, and she was standing at the desk at five in the morning with her hands flat on it, waiting.

"You know what I'm going to ask you," Žofia said.

"I did them at six," the girl said. "I did the six properly. And then Bed 11 was sick and Bed 2's mother wouldn't go home and I—" She stopped. Her chin was going. "I put down what he'd been doing all day. I was going to do them at midnight and put the real ones in. I was going to do them at midnight."

"You wrote them in advance."

"Yes."

"All three sets."

"Yes."

Žofia looked at her for a long moment, and thought about the fact that this girl had been on her feet for fifteen hours in a service that had decided, correctly and on principle, that a human being should do this and not a machine, and that the human being available was nineteen.

"Right," she said. "Here's what happened. At ten o'clock I went up on the bell and I didn't like the look of him, so I re-counted his pulse myself, and I found a hundred and sixty-eight, and I took him to theatre. That's what I've written and that's what happened."

Klaudia stared at her.

"You'll do a set of observations on every child in this ward at the correct hour for the rest of your career, and you will lay hands on them to do it, every single time, until the day you retire, because a boy nearly died in a bed while a piece of paper said he was well. That's the price. That's the whole price. Do you accept it?"

"Yes."

"Then go and wash your face."

The girl went. Žofia sat down at the desk and pulled the incident book towards her and wrote four lines in her small backward hand, and every word of them was a lie, and she wrote them steadily, and it took less than two minutes, and it did not cost her anything at all.

That was the part she thought about on the trolleybus.

Not the linen store. Not the felony in her locker under the coats. She had told a nineteen-year-old a merciful lie about a number at five o'clock in the morning, roughly thirty hours after standing in her own kitchen and being told that the last request of a dead man was that the numbers be given straight, and she had not hesitated, and she had been *right* to do it, and both of those things were true, and she could not get them to sit in the same head.

---

She came in at nine and put the bin on the kitchen table and took the lid off and looked at the small paper dial, hand-marked from one to thirty, with the pointer sitting on ten.

Ten days from the wake. The wake had been at twenty-two forty on the Thursday. Which made it Sunday week.

She had not decided anything. She had gone to work with a dead man's bin in her bag intending to be present at a death, and a child had bled, and she had used what was in the bag because it was in the bag, and now there were ten days on the clock and there would be ten days on the clock again after that, and this is how it is done, she thought. Not once, in a kitchen, with your coat on. In four-minute instalments, for the best reasons available, with a defensible answer ready each time.

She put her thumb on the edge of the dial and did not move it.

Then she went and slept, and dreamed of nothing, and got up at four in the afternoon, and it was Friday, and she was on again at eight.

# 3 · MINUTA

**Wake 7,402 — one day**

*First person, present. Four minutes.*

---

Dark, and a coat, and a face lit from underneath.

Shelves. Folded green. A mop head. A pull cord swinging, which means the light has been put on hard and let go, which means haste.

Her collar is wrong-side out at the left and there is a smear of iodine along the base of her thumb, dried, so it is not from tonight, or it is from earlier tonight and she has washed since without noticing that one.

She is breathing like a person who has come up stairs.

"How lo—"

"Not now."

That is all. Two words, and her hand is already moving, and the room swings and steadies and there is a sheet of paper filling everything, held too close and then pulled back, ruled columns, a nurse's upright hand, and her thumb across the corner of it.

"Read it. Quickly."

A boy's chart. Seven years old, forty grams of him missing in a jar somewhere, second post-operative day.

I read.

Temperature, rising a little, the way it rises for a hundred reasons. Respirations, twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-six. And the pulse.

One-one-two. One-oh-eight. One-one-four.

One-one-two. One-oh-eight. One-one-four.

"The pulse column repeats," I say. "One-twelve, one-oh-eight, one-fourteen, in that order, twice, from eighteen hundred. The respirations go twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-six, twice, in the same order, in the same rows. Two independent measurements of a sick child have produced the identical sequence twice running. That does not happen."

Her eyes come up off the paper.

"Somebody has written this in advance," I say. "Nobody has counted this child since six o'clock. Whatever he has been doing for four hours is not on that page and you cannot see it from there."

I know what it is to be a set of figures somebody has decided in advance for kind reasons. I do not say that. There is not room.

"Go and count him yourself," I say. "For a whole minute. Not fifteen seconds."
