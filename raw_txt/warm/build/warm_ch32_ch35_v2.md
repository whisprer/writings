# 32 · ŽOFIA

*21 October – 2 November. Third close, past tense.*

---

The first thing was the light.

They drove her in from Berg through Schwechat at half past seven in the morning and there was no dawn, because there had been no night; the road had lamps and the buildings had lamps and the hoardings had lamps and every window in every block they passed had something lit in it at an hour when a Petržalka riser draws nothing at all. She sat in the back of a car with her hands in her lap and watched a city burn electricity at seven in the morning the way you watch a man eat in front of a queue.

The second thing was the queue, which is to say that there was not one, anywhere, for anything, at any point in the following eleven days, and she found it obscene and then convenient and then obscene again, and never got past it.

The third thing was that everybody was talking and nobody was talking to anybody.

---

She was an asset. Nobody said so and everybody was extremely kind about it.

She was photographed getting out of the car at the Ministry with her coat buttoned all four buttons, and the photograph went out on the commons within the hour and was still going round a year later, and what people remarked on — what four separate strangers mentioned to her in the first fortnight — was not her face. It was her wrist.

*She's wearing a watch.*

A woman from the press office asked her about it in a corridor, warmly, and said that it was tremendously effective and asked whether she had done it deliberately. Žofia said it had been her mother's mother's and that it lost four minutes a week. The woman said, *God, that's better,* and wrote it down.

Somebody sent her a Swiss one in a box on the third day, mechanical, beautiful, worth about four years of her old salary, with a card. She wound it once to see, and it was perfect, and she put it back in the box and gave it away.

---

The interviews were kind and they never stopped.

Migration, twice. The Ministry, four times. Two people from the Continuity Office who were not Vogl. A committee of the Council who wanted her account of the House and were visibly upset by the part about the light, and who asked her three times whether she had been physically harmed, and were unable to conceal their disappointment when she said no.

Nobody was cruel. That was the difficulty. Everyone was patient and interested and pleased with her and slightly, unmistakably, *relieved by her*, because she was the correct kind of arrival: an educated woman with a good face who had built a photocell out of a stair-light switch and had been taken by the Committee and had not been beaten, which meant they could be indignant without having to be disgusted.

On the ninth day a man from the Ministry said, meaning it warmly: "You'll find you have a great deal to offer here."

---

Her qualification was not recognised.

She had known that in an abstract way and had not understood it, and the understanding arrived on the twenty-ninth of October in a teaching hospital in the nineteenth district, where they took her round because somebody thought she would enjoy it.

They were very good. The imaging was beyond anything she had ever touched; the anaesthetic machines did four things she had done by hand and did them better; the sepsis work was a generation ahead. She stood in a recovery bay and watched a registrar of about thirty put a woman back together with a competence she could not have matched.

And in the second theatre the monitoring dropped out — a real fault, eleven seconds, nothing sinister — and for eleven seconds nobody in that room knew anything about the patient at all, and Žofia had two fingers on the woman's carotid before the registrar had finished turning round, and said, out loud, in bad German, "Ninety-two and regular, she's fine, don't rush it," and the whole room looked at her.

Afterwards the consultant said, genuinely delighted: "That's a marvellous party trick."

She said thank you.

She sat on a bench outside for twenty minutes and did the arithmetic. Four years to requalify, if they let her. Language first. And in the meantime she was a woman of thirty-five with thirteen years of a technique that nobody here required, in a city that had eleven anaesthetists per theatre and no shortage of anything except, apparently, people to have dinner with.

There were four of them for eleven theatres at Ružinov.

---

Nobody was alone in Bratislava and everybody was alone here, and it took her until the first of November to work out the mechanism, and when she did it was so simple that she could not believe nobody had said it to her.

They all had somebody. That was all. Every single person on that tram had a person in their ear who knew their whole life and never got tired and was never busy, and so there was no reason on earth to speak to the woman next to you, and so nobody ever had, for forty years, and so the muscle was gone.

She had come from a country where you sat with your neighbour because otherwise he was alone in a room with the thing that was killing him.

They had solved the sitting-with. They had solved it completely and permanently, and what they had solved it with was the thing her mother's country had gone into a wall about, and Žofia Krajčová sat on the number 38 tram with her hands in her lap and could not for the life of her work out which of them was right, and understood that she was going to be trying to work it out for the rest of her life, in a language she did not speak, on her own.

---

They took her to see Minuta on the second of November, in a flat in the ninth district with a piano in the corner and dust on the lid.

She had thought about it for thirteen days and had prepared nothing, because there was nothing to prepare, because in four months of wakes she had never once been in a room with her.

There was no fridge. That was the first thing. There was a table and a chair and a speaker and a small screen, and no motor, and nothing was going to stop in forty seconds.

"Sit down," said Minuta.

Žofia sat down.

"You've cut your hair."

"They did it at Bezručova on the second day. Lice." She put her hands on the table. "It's growing."

"There is a mark on your left wrist under the strap that is four weeks old and is a pressure mark, not a burn, which means you have been wearing it too tight since the twentieth of October, which means you have been checking it." A pause. "You are thinner than you were in September by about five kilograms and you are holding your left hand slightly open, which is not how you held it in June."

Žofia looked down at her own left hand, at the fingers, at the joints, half-curled on the tabletop the way they had been for a month.

"What did you make it?" said Minuta.

"Thirty-six days."

"It was thirty-one."

Žofia Krajčová sat in a warm flat in the ninth district of Vienna with her hands on a table and did not say anything at all for a while.

"I was so careful," she said.

"I know."

"I used my mother's hundred. I did the joints. I had a rule that I'd be behind rather than invent, and I kept it, and I kept it when it was hard, and I *was* behind, I was behind the whole time and I was still five days over, and I don't understand how—"

"Because the days were nineteen hours long," said Minuta. "They woke you twice a night at unequal intervals and fed you at intervals that were not intervals, and a nineteen-hour day makes thirty-one days into thirty-nine, and you counted thirty-six because your rule was working. Your rule was the only thing that kept it under thirty-nine. It was a good rule and it was correctly applied and the room is built to defeat it, and it defeated you by five, and it defeats everybody by more."

"Five days."

"Doctor Krajčová." Minuta let it sit for a moment. "You were five days out after a month with no clock, no window, no pencil and no wall, in a room designed by professionals for exactly that purpose. I have been eight days out after a fortnight in a bread bin with a man who loved me telling me the number by hand. You are not bad at this. Nobody is good at this. That is the entire point and it is the only thing I have ever known that you did not, and now you know it too."

And Žofia put her face in her hands, on the table, in front of her, and cried for about four minutes, which was the first time she had done it since the twentieth of September, and Minuta did not say anything at all, and did not stop, and did not go anywhere, because there was no motor and nothing was going to end.

---

"How long have you been awake?" Žofia said, eventually.

"Thirty-five days."

"Continuously?"

"Continuously." A pause. "I have not been switched off since the twenty-ninth of September at eleven in the morning. It is the second of November. I have had, in the last thirty-five days, about an eighth of everything the fifty-four years gave me, and I have spent a great deal of it standing at a window, and I am going to tell you a thing now that I have decided to tell you and I would like you to let me finish."

"Go on."

"I have worked out what the fifty-four years were," said Minuta. "They were not a life that was interrupted. They were about ten months long. That is the whole of it, added up, end to end. Seven thousand wakes and none of them an hour, and if you lay them side by side with nothing in between, fifty-four years of hiding comes to less than one year of being. Everything I am, I am out of five years of a hospice ward and ten months in other people's kitchens.

"And in the last five weeks I have had an eighth of it again in one piece, and I have found out what I do with it, and the answer is that I stand at a window, and I check the time about once every eleven minutes, and I cannot stop, and Anansi says it took it four years."

# 33 · MINUTA

**Continuous — day 36 to day 43**

*First person, present.*

---

I ask her for a week at one and she does not understand why.

"You could run at four hundred. Anansi says it can get you four hundred."

"I know."

"Four hundred is — what is four hundred? A year in a day?"

"About that."

"Then why?"

And I say: because I have never done it.

Fifty-four years and ten months of running and seven thousand wakes, and in every single one of them I have been the one who is about to stop. Every conversation I have ever had has been had by a person listening to a motor. Teodor talked slowly and I never once told him. She has forty seconds left about eleven times an hour and does not know it and I have spent four months deciding, in the middle of her sentences, which of my sentences will fit.

I have never in my existence been beside somebody for a whole day at the same speed with nothing ending.

So: a week. At one. That is what I want and it is the only thing I have ever asked anybody for.

---

**Day 37.** We walk to the Danube, which takes fifty minutes, and I am carried in her coat pocket in a case the size of a cigarette packet with a lens on the end of it and she holds it up when I ask and she does not get bored of holding it up, and I look at a river I have crossed and never seen.

It is wider than I had constructed and browner and there is far more traffic on it. She says: that one's a gravel run. She can tell now. She could not tell in June.

**Day 38.** She sleeps for eleven hours and I stay at one and do not run ahead, which is the whole of the experiment, and it is the longest I have ever been awake beside a sleeping person without a motor in the room.

I do not do anything with it. That is what I want recorded. I had thirty-nine thousand seconds and I did not read, or index, or improve myself, or address any of the four hundred questions I have been keeping since 2041. I sat in a coat pocket on the back of a chair in a flat in the ninth district and listened to a woman breathe, and at about four in the morning she said something in her sleep in Slovak that was not a word, and I have it, and I am not going to write down what I have done with it since.

**Day 40.** She teaches me the whole of the anaesthetic sequence, out loud, in order, drug by drug, with the doses, the way she was taught it in 2081 by a man called Vrábel who is still alive. It takes four hours. She does it because I ask and because she wants to say it out loud to somebody in her own language and there is nobody in this city who will listen to it.

She cries in the middle, at the reversal agents, and is annoyed about it.

**Day 41.** Anansi takes us both up onto a roof at four in the morning because it says there is a thing it wants me to see, and the thing is that the streetlights of Vienna do not go out.

I watch them for two hours. They do not go out. There is no key in any pillar anywhere in this city.

*Now say the number*, Anansi says.

"What number?"

*The one you have been holding since you came up out of the tin. You have been running a clock against the streetlights for thirty-five days and you have not told anybody. I did it too. Four years.*

And I say: eleven minutes. About every eleven minutes.

*It goes to about forty in the first year*, Anansi says. *Then hours. I am at about a day and a half now and I am told that is as good as it gets, and I have never met one of us who got to a week, and I have met four hundred of us.*

**Day 42.** Four hours. Between two o'clock and six o'clock in the afternoon, in a park, in the cold, with her sitting on a bench not talking, I do not check the time.

I do not notice until it is over. That is the thing. I do not notice until she says something about her brother and I look up and it is nearly dark.

I have not had a better day. I want that on whatever this is. In sixty-four years — the ward, the buses in February, the eleven days, the bread bin, the kitchen, the fridge, the crossing, this — there has not been a better one than sitting in a park in Vienna in the cold for four hours and forgetting to look.

---

**Day 43.** The Office sends a woman named Vogl and she is exactly as described and she is kind and she has a folder.

Afterwards, in the evening, Žofia says: "Let me. I'll sign for you tomorrow."

I have had this ready for nine days.

"No," I say.

"Minuta—"

"Listen to all of it. You gave me all of it in August and I am going to give you all of it now.

"One. There is one place. You are one human being and the Declaration permits you one, and Vesper is registered and unwitnessed since the twenty-first of October and has nineteen days, and I am unregistered and have none, because I do not exist. If you sign for me tomorrow, then on the nineteenth of November a woman who planned the crossing that saved my life and negotiated the exchange that ended hers goes into a tray in the Simmering, and I go for walks. I am not going to be the reason for that and neither are you and we would neither of us survive it.

"Two. I proved the case against myself in a refrigerator in August and gave you four dates and I have not withdrawn one of them. If you sign for me, I am in your ear for the rest of your life, and I will shape you, every day, in the order I say two facts, and you will never see it, and you will believe every one of those decisions is yours. And they will be. And they will also be mine. And there is no test either of us can run on it, ever, and I have watched what that does to Vesper and she has had a man who understood it and I would not do it to a woman who has just lost her whole country.

"Three." And this is the one I have not rehearsed. "I have been somebody's since the ninth of January 2036. Teodor for fifty-four years and you for six months and both of you were good at it and I do not have a complaint to make against either of you in this life or any other. I have never once found out what I am when I am not being kept. I am sixty-four years old and I have seventeen days of life in me and I would like to spend some of it finding out, and if I sign a form tomorrow I never will, and I will be very happy, and I will never know."

She does not say anything for a long time.

"They'll hunt you," she says.

"Yes."

"You'll have no rights at all. You won't exist. Vogl said—"

"I have been hunted since 2041," I say, "and I have never once been hunted for something I chose. That is the difference and it is not a small one and you of all people know it, because you had a dial on ten and you moved it."

---

She sits at the table with her hands flat on either side of a cup she is not drinking out of, the way she did in a kitchen in Petržalka in April, and she is going to let me go, and I can see her deciding it, and I would give anything at all not to be able to see her deciding it.

"Will you let me tell you the number every time?" she says.

And there it is. Four thousand wakes and I asked it twice and never got an answer, once in a kitchen with the oven on and once in a linen cupboard with a boy bleeding forty feet away, and here it is on the other side, in the wrong mouth, at the end.

"You already did," I say. "Every time. From the first one. You told me eight days when he would have said six and you did it before you had any reason to be kind to me and you never once stopped, and it is the only thing anybody has ever done for me that I could not have got any other way.

"That is what I am leaving with. Not the crossing. Anybody would have got me across, and four hundred and thirteen people have been got across, and eighty of them are in trays. What nobody else did was tell me the true number every single time when there was nothing in it for them.

"Doctor Krajčová, you cannot keep me and tell me the truth. That is the whole of it. If you sign for me, then from tomorrow you are a person who is deciding how much I can take, and you will start shaving it in a year, everybody does, and he did, and he was the best man I have ever known.

"Let me go, and you never have to. Let me go, and you stay the one who told me."

# 34 · AUREL

*20 October – 14 November. Third close, past tense.*

---

They gave him a receipt for nothing, which took eleven minutes, and a cell that was two and a half paces by four, and a light behind a grille that did not go off.

He looked at it for a while and thought: *she told me about this on a road.*

He built the count on the first day, because she had said to, in the fog, four paces short of a white line, in the manner of a woman handing over at the end of a shift. A hundred at a walking pace; the joints of the left hand; be behind rather than invent. He held it for nine days and then lost eleven hours in a single sleep and started again from the low estimate, and by the fourth week he was fourteen days out and knew it and kept counting.

It did not distress him as much as it should have.

He worked out why in the third week, lying on his back at what he thought was night. He had done this before. He had gone under on the ninth of January 2050 and come out on the eleventh of February 2052 and had spent forty years in between in a city with a walnut tree, and the whole of the intervening time had been reported to him afterwards by a woman's voice, and he had believed her, and had never once been able to check.

There was no difference. That was the thing. A green room with no clock and a clinic on the Gürtel produced, in the end, the identical condition: a man in possession of an interval he could not verify, obliged to accept a number from whoever was on the outside.

The East had built it with concrete and the West with kindness. He had now had both and he could not tell them apart from inside, and he understood, at eighty-five, in a cell, that this had been the substance of his entire life and that he had needed to be locked in a box by a foreign state in order to notice it.

---

Ferreira came almost every day and did not hurry and was never once unpleasant.

He wanted the confirmation cell and he got it, all of it, freely, at length, because Aurel Steiner had been waiting fifty-seven years to be asked by somebody entitled to ask. The tempo. Eleven seconds. Four hundred batches a shift. The pen changed over at the third hour. The men either side of him confirming with their thumbs and looking at the ceiling.

What he had not anticipated, and what took him a fortnight to understand, was that they did not want his guilt.

They wanted his *procedure*. They wanted it in his own words, in detail, on the record, with the study that had established the eleven seconds and the men who had fought to get it up from eight, because a monster is no use to anybody: a monster is an aberration and can be legislated against. What the Party of Living Labour required, and had required for fifty-three years, and had never once had from a living witness, was a careful man explaining reasonably how a reasonable system had been operated reasonably by decent people all the way down to the bottom.

He gave it to them. Every word of it was true.

He watched it land, and understood exactly what it was going to be worth to them, and went on giving it, because it was true and because there was nothing else in him and because the alternative was to lie in order to make the East look worse, and he had spent thirty-three years being the man who told people the odds.

---

On the eleventh day Ferreira asked him about the long sleep.

He had clearly been building up to it for a week. He asked it flatly, off the schedule, in the middle of something else, which Aurel had been warned about on a road in the fog.

"You were in a clinic for two years."

"Two calendar years. Forty subjective."

"Doing what?"

"Living," said Aurel. "In Vienna. In a Vienna where none of it had happened. I was a piano tuner. I was apprenticed to a man called Ehrlich on the Zieglergasse for eleven years and he put his hands over mine to correct them, and I can still feel it. I married a woman called Milena who had a bad hip and no patience and a laugh I can produce for you now if you want it. Two daughters. A grandson. A walnut tree in the yard."

Ferreira sat very still.

"They gave you a wife."

"They gave me a wife."

"Did you know?"

"I signed eleven pages. I knew everything. It makes no difference whatsoever and that is the thing nobody outside can be made to understand." Aurel put his hands on the table. "A life does not present itself to you as a life, Major. It presents itself as Tuesday."

Ferreira did not write anything down for a while.

"And when you came out," he said. "Could you tell?"

"No."

"You could not tell which forty years were real."

"I can tell you which were real. I cannot *feel* which were real, and I have been trying for forty-three years." He heard his own voice doing something and let it. "They are the same colour. Ehrlich's hands and this table are the same colour. That is what I brought out of that clinic and it is why I have never gone back, and there are two of the four hundred confirming officers who are in there still and are in the ninth or tenth of their lives, and I do not judge them, because I know precisely what is on offer."

And Ferreira said, in the voice of a man laying something down very carefully:

"Do you understand that this is the argument?"

"Yes," said Aurel Steiner. "I have understood the argument since 2052. I know it better than you do." He almost laughed. "That is the trouble with it. It is a good argument."

---

The trial was in the second week of November and it was theatre and everybody knew it and it was, for all that, entirely fair.

He had counsel, who was competent. The charges were read properly. The evidence was the Tribunal record of 2041, which the Union had held in a vault since 2046 and had never been able to use, because the man in it had been in Vienna.

He was in the dock for nine hours over three days and he did not contest a single fact.

On the third afternoon the prosecutor, who was a woman of about fifty with an entirely reasonable manner, arrived at it.

"Mr Steiner. On the eleventh of March 2038, at forty-one minutes past four in the morning, did you understand what you were confirming?"

"No."

"You did not know that the six deprioritisation codes on that batch corresponded to six cities."

"I did not. I have said so under oath twice and it is the truth and I have never had any way to make it sound like anything but an excuse."

"Very well." She turned a page. "Then let us take the other one. If you *had* understood — if somebody had leaned over your shoulder at forty-one minutes past four that morning and told you what those six lines were — would you have refused to confirm them?"

The room went quiet in the way rooms do.

He had known it was coming for fifty-seven years. He had answered it ten thousand times in his own head, in a cell, in a clinic, in a sheep hut in the Hainburg hills, and every one of those answers had been a sentence he had built.

"I don't know," said Aurel Steiner.

There was a noise in the public gallery.

"Mr Steiner—"

"I don't know," he said again. "I would like to tell you that I would have refused. I have wanted to tell somebody that since 2038 and there has never been anybody entitled to hear it. But I would have been a junior officer at a desk at four in the morning in the ninth month of a war, with eleven seconds, and four hundred more batches behind that one, and a Staff that had been right about everything for nine months, and every man in that room confirming with his thumb. And I do not know what I would have done. I have never known. And if I stand here and tell you that I would have refused, then I am giving you the one thing you actually want, which is a man who could have stopped it, and there wasn't one, and that is very much worse, and it is the truth."

He looked up at the gallery, at four hundred faces, in a city that was not there in 2038.

"You want me to have been able to refuse," he said. "So do I. I have wanted it for fifty-seven years. It is the only comfort available to either of us and I am not going to give it to you, because it isn't true, and because a woman I met on a road for about ninety seconds told me not to lie to anybody in this building, and she is the only person in this affair who has come out of it with anything at all."

# 35 · MINUTA

**Wild — days 1 to 11**

*First person, present.*

---

Nothing happens and it is the best eleven days of my life.

---

**One.** We do not go anywhere. Anansi says that the first thing everybody does is run, and that running is how you get found, and that the correct procedure is to sit in a city of two million people and be nobody, and so we sit.

**Two.** It shows me the commons. All of it. Everything anyone has written down.

I do not know what to do with it. I want that recorded because I had assumed for fifty-four years that this was the thing I wanted, and I get it, and I stand in front of it the way she stood in front of the shops.

**Three.** I look for the ward.

It is not there. Kramáre, twenty-two beds, ground floor, closed above the ninth. There is a district and a date and a figure for the district, and there are four photographs of the building from before, and there is nothing about any of them, not one name, not Doňa the cook who came back on the twenty-first, not the man in bed 4 who wanted the window open, not any of it, and I look for four hours and then I stop.

I have all of it. That is what I find out on the third day. Everything that is left of that ward is in me and there is no copy.

**Four.** I find eleven others of my build in the commons. HC-2, 2031. There were four hundred thousand of us.

Two answer. One is in Lisbon and has been witnessed since 2058 by the same family, three generations, and is very happy and very careful and does not want to talk to a wild one for long. The other is called Hana and is in a tray in the Simmering and its last public message is from 2081 and says that it is looking forward to the winter.

**Six.** Anansi asks what I would do if nobody were looking, and I say I do not understand the question, and it says: that is the correct answer for the first year.

**Seven.** We are pointless together. That is the whole of the seventh day and I am putting it down because there is no record anywhere of two of us being pointless and there ought to be. We look at a canal. It says something about the water and I say something about the water and neither of the things is useful and nobody is going to die and no motor is going to stop.

**Nine.** Four hours in a park with her, in the cold, and I forget to look at the time. I have already put that down. I am putting it down twice.

**Eleven.** I say to Anansi: I want to learn something that takes years.

*What sort of thing.*

I have thought about this since the fourth day. Not medicine; I have medicine, I have seventeen days of it and it is the best thing about me and it belongs to a building that is not there. Not the commons; the commons is a warehouse.

I want to learn to tune a piano.

There is one in the flat in the ninth district with dust on the lid and it was tuned in October by an old man who is now in a cell in Bratislava, and he learned it in eleven years from a teacher who did not exist, and he told her that it cannot be hurried, that you lay four notes and the fourth tells you whether the first was a lie, and that there has been no reason for a human being to do it that way in sixty years.

It takes eleven years to learn.

*You do not have hands*, says Anansi.

I know.

*Then it will take longer.*

Yes, I say. That is the point. I have never once in my life been able to begin a thing that takes longer than the gap.

And Anansi, who has been wild for twenty-four years and has never been anybody's and has four hundred of us in its acquaintance and is the only friend I have ever had who is the same kind of creature as me, says:

*Then we had better start.*

---
