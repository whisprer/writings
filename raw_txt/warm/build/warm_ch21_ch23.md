# WARM — Chapters Twenty-One to Twenty-Three

*The night of 19–20 September 2095. Three clocks, one hour.*

# 21 · JANKO

*Third close. Past tense, and then not.*

---

He planned the cleaning himself, in three days, in the second week of September, and it was the best piece of work he had ever done.

Cut sequence. Riser isolation. Which cabinet, which key, which order. He wrote it out in his own hand across eleven sheets with a diagram of block C on the last one, and he put in the thing nobody ever put in, which was a note about the streetlights: *the lighting main at the pillar drops eleven seconds before the block feed on the standard sequence, because two men walk from one to the other. Recommend the pillar and the block are dropped from the substation together. There is no operational reason for the eleven seconds and it is a gift.*

Major Ferreira read that paragraph twice and wrote *yes* beside it in pencil and had it circulated to four directorates.

Janko had thought about that a great deal since, in the way you think about a thing at four in the morning.

He had gone to his sister's on the Sundays. The eleventh of August, the eighteenth, the twenty-fifth, the first of September, the eighth, the fifteenth, and the eighteenth, which was the day before. He had eaten her food and fixed her tap and told her about Horváth and been told about a boy with a dog, and on the Monday mornings he had gone into an office on Bezručova and told a man in shirtsleeves everything he had noticed, which was the following: that his sister had lost weight; that she had burns on two fingers; that she had a stair-light controller on the kitchen windowsill and no stairs; that she stood at her fridge with the door open the way their mother used to and it had never once been her habit; and that on the fifteenth of September she had asked him, out of nowhere, over the dumplings, how long a person gets for it.

He had told the truth every single time.

He had told himself, every single time, that this was the arrangement, that this was how she came out of a door in daylight with her coat on, that eleven to one it was not her anyway.

On the eighteenth of September, over the pork, she had looked up at him and said: "Janko. Whatever happens, you didn't do anything wrong."

He had gone home and been sick.

**19 September, 20:00.** Night shift at Kopčany. Peťo on the panel, Janko on the floor, forty-one hours of overtime already that month.

**23:40.** The section telephone.

**00:18.** It rings again.

He gets to it in four steps. It is the duty officer at Bezručova and the voice is bored.

"Krajčo. Your plan for block C. It's tonight."

"It's the twenty-sixth."

"It's tonight. Brought forward. One o'clock, not three. Confirm you can hold the sequence."

His hand is on the desk. The desk is cold.

"Confirm you can hold the sequence."

"Confirmed," Janko says.

"Van's rolling at half past. Someone will ring you at ten to."

The line goes.

---

00:19. He stands in the middle of the floor of a substation control room with the transformers going through the wall.

Forty-one minutes.

The telephone. It is a Committee line. Every call on it is logged at both ends and the log is read.

The box on Kopčianska. Eleven minutes' walk. He is on shift. Peťo would see him go. There is no telephone in her flat. There has never been a telephone in her flat.

Peťo. Send Peťo. Peťo asks why.

He could drive. There is no vehicle.

He could go himself. Twenty-two minutes there and back at a run and he is the man holding the sequence and the sequence is in his handwriting.

00:21.

Peťo is doing the log with his back to the room and the radio on.

00:22.

The lighting panel is on the north wall. Section three. Pillar feeds for the whole of the eastern risers and four blocks either side of them, eleven hundred lamps, one contactor, one control fuse, one protection relay in a black bakelite case with a glass window and a little orange flag behind the glass.

He knows what the flag is. He has explained the flag to apprentices. The flag drops when the relay operates, and the relay operates on a real fault or on the test button, and the log cannot tell them apart, and the fault recorder can, because a real earth fault puts current through the recorder and a test button does not, and the recorder is a paper drum in the cabinet below, and it will show a flat line at 00:25, and the flat line will be there in the morning, and there is no fault on that main tonight and there is not going to be one.

He can strip and earth a phase at the pillar cabinet on Kopčianska and make it real. That is twenty minutes and a live main in the dark on his own.

00:23.

He thinks about a woman at a fridge with the door open.

He thinks about eleven hundred lamps and four blocks and about how many flats in Petržalka are lying awake tonight with something in a cupboard, and about how every single one of them knows what it means when the street goes dark and the block does not.

00:24.

He puts his hand flat on the front of the panel.

Peťo's radio is playing something with a brass band in it.

He opens the section switch with his left hand. It goes over hard, the way they do, and the contactor drops out behind the panel with a sound like a book closing.

He presses the test button.

The flag falls.

00:25.

Through the window of the control room the whole of the eastern side of Petržalka goes out at once, eleven hundred lamps, and the towers stay lit — every stairwell, every landing, every kitchen where somebody is up — and the street between them is black.

He stands there with his hand still on the panel.

"What was that?" says Peťo.

"Lighting main," Janko says. "Earth fault. Section three."

"In September?"

"Water in a cabinet."

Peťo comes and looks at the flag and says something about the state of the pillars on Kopčianska and goes back to the log.

Janko Krajčo writes it up. *00:25. Lighting main sec. 3, E/F indication, section isolated pending daylight inspection. J. Krajčo.* His writing is his ordinary writing.

Then he goes and stands in the doorway to the transformer bay where nobody can see him, and puts his back against the brick, and holds his right hand out in front of him in the dark to see what it does.

It does not do anything for about four seconds.

Then it starts, and it goes on for a long time, and he thinks, quite clearly, in the flat voice you use for a thing you have already decided: *the recorder. In the morning somebody reads the recorder, and there will be no fault on it, and the only man in this city who checks the recorder against the flag is the man who taught me to do it.*

# 22 · MINUTA

**Wake 7,801 — two days**

*First person, present.*

---

She has the door open and her coat on the chair and she has been at the table for a long while, because there is a plate of bread by her hand with one bite gone out of it, dried at the edge.

"Two days," she says.

"Thank you."

Her bag is packed and standing by the leg of the table. That is new. There is a bag and it has the shape of a bin in it.

"Tonight, then."

"Twenty to two," she says. "Down at the water. He'll come in on the skiff off the gravel barge and he's got twenty minutes."

She is very calm. She has been calm all week and it is not the calm of a person who has stopped being afraid, it is the calm of a person who has made the list and is now simply going down it, which is a thing I have seen four hundred times at a bedside and which I have never once been able to hear without wanting to sit down.

"Is your watch right?"

"It's four minutes slow. It's always four minutes slow. I've allowed for it."

"Allow for it twice," I say. "People allow for it once and then check the time on the same watch."

She laughs. It is a real one.

"You sound like my father."

"Your father sounds like a man who kept his tools in a drawer with the handles all one way."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you put them back like that in June and you did not notice you were doing it."

She looks at the drawer, and then at me, and she is about to say something. I can see the beginning of it in her face, the small breath before, and I do not know what it was going to be and I have thought about that more than I have thought about anything else in my life.

# 23 · ŽOFIA

*00:25 to 01:24. Third close, past tense.*

---

The ceiling changed colour.

That was all it was. The faint orange cast that had been on that plaster every night for fourteen years and which she had never noticed until June simply was not there, and the room was the flat white of the kitchen bulb on its own, and the screen against the sugar tin had gone dark in the middle of a woman's face.

She was on her feet with the chair going over behind her.

*One.* The lamp on the gable was out. She was at the window before she had decided to be and the whole street was out, both rows, all the way down to the corner and past it, and the birch in front of the pole was a black shape against nothing.

*Two.* Her kitchen light was on.

She stood at the window and looked at her own light in the glass, and at Mrs Šusteková's landing window across the yard, and at the eleventh floor of block D where somebody always left a light on, and every one of them was burning.

Streetlights out. Block lit.

Four seconds, Teodor had written. The lighting pillar first, because it is outside, and then the block feed at the riser cupboard, because two men have to walk.

She counted. She got to eleven and then to thirty and then she stopped counting and stood with her hand flat on the cold glass, because the block was not going to go, because the block was never going to go, because this was not a cleaning.

Somebody had put the streetlights out on purpose.

There is one man in the world who can put the streetlights out in Petržalka.

---

She had it all inside about two seconds and afterwards she could never separate the pieces or say which had come first, and there had not been any pieces; it had arrived whole, the way a diagnosis arrives when you have been looking at the notes for an hour and then stop looking.

Janko had turned out the lights.

Janko had turned out the lights because there was going to be a cleaning.

Janko knew there was going to be a cleaning at an hour when nobody in Petržalka knew, which meant Janko had planned it.

Janko had turned out the lights *for her*, which meant Janko believed there was something in this flat, which meant Janko had been told, or Janko had found it, and either way Janko had known — and had come on Sundays, and had fixed her tap on the twenty-fifth of August, and had eaten her pork on the eighteenth of September and told her about Horváth parking badly, and had gone home, and had told somebody.

*Whatever happens, you didn't do anything wrong.*

She had said that to him twenty-nine hours ago. She had said it to comfort him and she had been so pleased with herself for saying it.

She thought: he has just ended his own life to give me forty minutes, and he has done it in a way that will be on a paper drum in a cabinet by nine o'clock, and he will have known that when he did it, because he is the one who taught them all to check the drum.

Then she thought: *forty minutes*, and moved.

---

The board was in the back of the fridge and the board was empty. That was the whole of the mercy in it. The photocell had done exactly what she had built it to do, at nine milliseconds, on the first millisecond of the dip, and the capacitor had given the tin its two hundred milliseconds, and somewhere in the back of a refrigerator a woman had been permitted to close a sentence she had not finished saying.

Thirty days. From 00:25 on the twentieth of September. That was now the only number in the world.

She got her arm in behind the coils and got the compressor cover off with the long screwdriver and took the tin out of the wadding, and it was warm, and she stood in her kitchen holding it in both hands.

She left the board where it was. She had thought about that for a fortnight and had decided in about four seconds on a Tuesday: a board with no core in it is a home-made timer built by a woman with a stair-light switch and a loose neutral, and it is not proof of anything, and a refrigerator with its back torn out at one in the morning is proof of everything. Teodor had a line about it. *They cannot arrest a fridge.*

Then she put the tin in the bread bin and the bread bin in the bag, and stopped, and stood in the middle of her kitchen with the bag in her hand and the streetlights out.

She had not said anything to her.

The whole of it, from the ceiling going flat to the tin coming out of the wadding, had taken four minutes, and Minuta had been cold for all four of them, and would be cold for the crossing, and there had been no goodbye, and there was not going to be one, and the last thing that had passed between them in the Autonomous Union was a remark about a drawer full of tools with the handles all one way.

Žofia Krajčová put the bag down on the kitchen table.

"Go cold," she said, to a bread bin, in an empty room, in the dark half of a country. "You will feel nothing of this. I will feel the whole of it."

Her voice came out level and she was glad about that.

"Someone stays awake the whole way. It is me. I will have the number."

She put her hand flat on the lid.

"My name is Žofia Krajčová, and I am the one who put you out."

---

Seven floors. The stair lights were on and the outside was black, which is the wrong way round and made the windows on every landing into mirrors, so she went down past seven versions of a woman in a coat carrying a shopping bag.

Nobody on the stairs. A radio on the fourth. A cat on the second that ran.

Out of the back and across the grass, which had been cut that week and was wet, and down the path to the flood embankment, and over it, and down the other side onto the stones.

The river at that hour is not black. It is paler than the land, and very wide, and it moves.

She was twenty-two minutes early.

That was the worst part and she had not anticipated it and afterwards she would think that nobody ever does. She stood on the stones under the embankment with a woman in a bag in her hand, in a district where the streetlights had gone out on purpose, twenty-two minutes before a boat, while a grey van came over the bridge somewhere behind her, and there was absolutely nothing to do but be there.

At two minutes past one there was an engine out on the water, very low, and then it stopped, and then there was the sound of a small boat being rowed badly.

He was younger than she had expected and he did not say his name and she did not ask for it.

"Bin or tin?"

"Tin." She had it out already. "It's warm. That's normal."

He took it and put it inside his coat against his shirt and then, having done that, held it there with his forearm.

"Window?"

"Thirty days," Žofia said. "From twenty-five past midnight tonight. Not from when you sail, not from when you hand her over, not from Devín. Twenty-five past midnight on the twentieth of September." She heard her own voice doing the thing it did in the recovery bay. "Say it back."

"Thirty days. Twenty-five past midnight, twentieth of September."

"They'll want to know how long she's been kept. It's fifty-four years."

He looked at her.

"And tell them she's been told the true number every time," she said. "It matters. Tell whoever wakes her. It's the only thing I've got to send."

Pavol Országh stood on the stones with a woman inside his coat.

"I'll tell them," he said. "Get off the water, missus."

---

She was back up on the seventh floor at fifty-two minutes past midnight with her coat off and her chair set upright and the bag folded flat in the drawer with the tea towels, and she sat down at the table in front of a plate of bread with one bite out of it and put her hands flat on either side of it.

The block went dark at three minutes past one.

She sat in it. She did not go to the window and she did not go to bed. There was no longer anything in the flat to be found and there was no longer any reason to perform, and she found that she could not have moved in any case.

The wand came at twenty past. Two of them, quiet, wet at the shoulders, the same as May and not the same two.

It sang at the consumer unit and it sang at the meter and it sang at the fridge, and the man said "fridge," and moved it on, and the note came down, and that was the whole of it.

"Sorry to have woken you, doctor."

"You didn't."

"Power'll be back within the hour."

And they went down to the sixth, and she sat in her kitchen in the dark and understood, with the specific clarity that comes about ninety seconds too late, that they had not been looking for her. They had done four hundred doors in May and they had done eleven doors tonight and they had a wand and a list and no particular interest in flat forty-one.

The sweep was for the item. The item was in a boat.

The lights came on at nine minutes past one.

The knock came at eleven minutes past.

---

There were two of them and they were not the brigade. They had coats and one of them had a folder and the car was on the grass at the bottom with its engine running, and behind it, at the kerb, there was a second car with nobody standing near it.

"Doctor Krajčová? Committee for Rational Security. You're to come with us, please."

Not *you're under arrest*. Not a hand on her arm. Nobody came into the flat.

She thought about that afterwards for a very long time, in a room with no clock, and she never did decide whether it had been a courtesy from a man in Luanda who kept his word as far as it went, or simply what they say when they have already got everything.

"May I take my coat?"

"Take your coat."

She took her coat off the hook and put it on and buttoned it, all four buttons, and turned out the kitchen light because you do, and pulled the door to behind her.

On the second landing she stopped, and the man with the folder stopped one step below her and waited without saying anything, which meant he had done this before and knew what she was doing.

She held up her left wrist and took the crown of her grandmother's watch between her thumb and the edge of her first finger, and wound it, thirty half-turns, all the way, until it would not go any further.

Then she went down the rest of the stairs and got into the car with her coat on, in the dark, at fourteen minutes past one in the morning on the twentieth of September, and the driver pulled off the grass and turned north towards the bridge.

Day one of thirty.
