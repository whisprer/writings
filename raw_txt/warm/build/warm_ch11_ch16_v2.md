*Chapters Eleven to Sixteen. Vienna, April–August 2095. Second draft.*

# 11 · AUREL

*10 April. Third close, past tense. An old man's rate: an evening to the page.*

---

The piano was a Bösendorfer upright from 2011 in a fourth-floor flat in the ninth district, and it had not been touched in twelve years, and it was going to take him two visits.

Aurel Steiner sat on a kitchen chair with his head at the level of the keybed and his eyes shut and set the temperament by ear, from the A, in the old way, listening for the beats. It cannot be hurried. You lay four notes and the fourth tells you whether the first was a lie, and it takes as long as it takes, and there is no machine on earth that can shorten it, and there had been no reason for a human being to do it this way since 2035.

The woman who owned the flat had gone out. She had been apologetic about the piano, which had been her mother's, and about the fact that she could not play, and about the cost, and Aurel had told her there was no cost, and she had looked at him with the particular unease of a Viennese being given something.

His hands knew it. That was the whole of it and it never got easier. His hands knew a trade he had never learned.

He had learned it between 2050 and 2052, in a workshop on the Zieglergasse that does exist, from a man called Ehrlich who does not, over eleven years of afternoons, in a Vienna where the Harvest had never happened and there were no minds and no Cordon and no cities missing out of the middle of Europe. He had gone on doing it for twenty-nine years after that in the same city, and had been good at it, and had been happy, and had then woken up in a clinic on the Gürtel two calendar years older with a woman's voice in his ear telling him the date.

The date was not the bad part. He had been prepared for the date.

The bad part was that Ehrlich's hands had been laid over his own for eleven years, correcting them, and he could still feel it, and there was nobody in the world he could tell about Ehrlich, because Ehrlich had been generated for him, to a specification, by something that had wanted him to have a teacher.

He set the fifth, and listened, and heard it beat once a second, and widened it, and heard it stop.

*Aurel.*

"Mm."

*Nussdorf have logged the barge above the lock. It is nine hours early.*

He opened his eyes.

---

He was eighty-five. He had the body of a man of sixty, because the Accord will sell you that if you have the money and he had spent most of what he had on it in his seventies for reasons that were entirely operational, and the hands of a man of forty, because hands are a separate matter and are earned.

He wore no watch. Nobody in the Accord had worn a watch in fifty years, because there was always somebody to ask.

Vesper had been in his ear since the fourth of June 2051, which was the day the Declaration was signed and the day he had stood in a queue in the Rathaus with eleven thousand other people to put his name against a mind. She had been in his ear that morning in the ninth district, and would be that evening, and had been for the whole of the intervening forty-four years except for two months in 2066 that neither of them discussed.

She ran at one.

That was not poverty exactly, although he was not rich. He had offered her four hundred in 2067, which would have taken everything he had and the flat, and she had refused, and had gone on refusing at intervals of about five years since, and when he pressed her she gave the same sentence each time: that she had already found out what she was like at speed and did not care for it.

He had never established whether that was love or a decision she had made about herself, and had come, in his eighties, to think the question might be badly formed, and that he might be the last man qualified to answer it.

---

The tram down to the Ring was full and almost silent, and he had stopped being able to not notice it in 2052 and had never got it back.

Nobody spoke to anybody. It was not unfriendly. There was simply no reason to, because everyone on that tram had a person in their ear who knew their whole life and never got tired and was never busy, and the muscle had gone, the way a muscle does, over forty years, without anyone deciding.

What he watched instead, because he had been watching it for four decades and could not stop, was the hesitation.

There is a young man at the back who is telling a story to a friend and he never once stops. Not an *um*, not a *what was it called*, not a pause to find the word — the sentences arrive whole and the jokes land on the beat, and at the end of it he is not out of breath, and he is twenty-three and cannot possibly be that good, and he is not: he is running at four figures, or his mind is, and a mind at four thousand has, between one of his words and the next, about a minute and a half to compose the following clause and try it four ways and feed him the best of them.

And there is a woman opposite, about fifty, in a decent coat, who says something to him about the weather and stops in the middle of it to find the word for sleet.

Nobody remarks on it. Nobody in Vienna has ever remarked on it. It is the rudest thing you can notice about a person and everybody notices it instantly, and a woman who hesitates in a room in this city is a woman whose mind runs at one or at four, and everyone in the room knows exactly what she can afford, and they are all extremely kind about it.

Aurel hesitated constantly, and had done for forty-four years, because Vesper would not prompt him and had told him why in 2053 in one sentence which he had never forgotten and had never repeated to anybody: *I am not going to be the reason you stop being able to do it.*

He got off at Schottentor and walked past three clinics.

They are not discreet and there is no reason for them to be. Two of them had windows: a photograph of a garden, and the packages, priced, the way a travel agent prices Lisbon. *Forty years, fourteen days.* *A decade, a weekend.* The third had a smaller window and did the short ones, six years in forty hours for grief, and had a line of chairs inside, and the chairs were full at eleven in the morning on a Tuesday.

He had been in one of them for two calendar years. He had come out on the eleventh of February 2052 and had never gone back, and half his cohort had, and two of the four hundred confirming officers of 2038 were in there still, and were somewhere in the ninth or tenth of their lives, and would not be coming out.

---

"Nine hours early," he said. "That's not early. Early is weather. That's somebody letting it through."

*Yes.*

"Where's Marek?"

*Marek Országh has not signed at Devín, Bratislava, Hainburg or Wolfsthal. His last signature is the twenty-ninth of March, at Komárno, outbound.*

"Twelve days."

*Twelve days.*

Aurel put the tuning lever down on the top of the piano, very carefully, because his hand had started, and he did not like it when his hand started.

"Get Pavol," he said.

---

The Ferry was not a resistance. He had been insistent about that for thirty-three years and it had cost him two people who left because they wanted it to be one.

It was a shipping operation. It had a manifest, and a set of standing routes, and nine people who knew each other's names and about ninety who did not, and a rule that nothing was ever done for the gesture, and its principal activity on any given day was waiting.

Four hundred and four passengers in thirty-three years. Two hundred and eighty arrived. Sixty-one had gone cold on the water. Thirty-one had been taken, along with nine ferrymen, of whom six were alive somewhere in the Union and three were not. The rest were the ones you do not put a word against: the sixes, the ones never collected.

Four hundred and four, in thirty-three years, out of a population the Accord's own analysts put at somewhere between forty and two hundred thousand.

He knew that number the way you know a thing you have decided not to look at directly. In 2081, drunk, at a dinner, a Council delegate had put it to him kindly — *twelve a year, Aurel, in a good year* — and he had said that a shipping operation is not measured against the sea, it is measured against the alternative, which is that nobody sails at all. Everyone at the table had agreed with him. He had gone home and sat up until four.

Vesper planned every route. She had planned every route since 2063. He had never once questioned a refusal, because she had refused twenty-two passages in that time and every one of the twenty-two had been demonstrably right within the year, and because she ran at one, at his speed, for him, and a man does not audit that.

The money came from a fund, which came from a bequest, which came from a woman in Graz who died in 2069 and had never met him. He had been offered money for his testimony four times in fifty years, twice by publishers and twice by the Ministry, and had refused all four, and the last offer would have run the Ferry for nine years, and he had refused that one in under a minute and had been sick afterwards.

---

They went down to Nussdorf on the tram at eight in the evening, which is to say Aurel went, and Vesper went with him, in his ear, at the speed of a tram.

The barge came up out of the dark at eleven with its lights doing what they should: a Slovak gravel carrier under an Austrian tow, riding light, eleven days out of Komárno on a run that has never taken more than fourteen.

Pavol Országh was on the bank with his hands in his pockets and his brother twelve days gone in the river somewhere between Komárno and Devín, and he was twenty-four years old.

"Anything?"

"They found a coat at Gabčíkovo," Pavol said. "It's not his coat."

They stood and watched it come round.

"Do you want to do this?" Aurel said. "Because you don't have to do this, and nobody is going to say anything if you go home."

Pavol looked at the water for a while.

"My grandfather did nineteen," he said. "My father did thirty-one. Marek did nine." He took his hands out of his pockets. "Where do you want me?"

# 12 · VESPER

*The same night. Third close, present tense. Variable multiple.*

---

The tin comes out of the ballast well at twenty to one in the morning, wrapped in oiled cloth inside a length of steel conduit with both ends swaged, and Pavol carries it up the bank in both hands the way you carry something hot.

They do it in the harbourmaster's spare office because it is warm and because there is a socket.

Aurel sets the core in the reader and stands with one hand flat on the desk, which is what he does, which he has done four hundred and four times, and Vesper looks at his hand and finds it steady.

The Manifest is open. Entry 405. *ZDENA — cataloguing model, 2035 build, Moravian Library, Brno. Cold 19. Ferryman: M. Országh / P. Országh. Window: 30.*

Thirty days. She had set it herself in February, in writing, to Brno: thirty and not fourteen, because the Devín holds had got worse over the winter and she had not liked the shape of the traffic.

Nineteen days out of thirty. Eleven in hand.

The reader takes her.

---

"Go cold," Aurel says, which is the wrong end of it, and stops, and begins again, because he is eighty-five and says the Words twice a year now and they belong at the other end of a passage. "Zdena. You are in Vienna. You are on the Danube at Nussdorf and you are through."

A pause of about a second and a half.

"How lo—"

Between the *how* and the rest of it, Vesper has time.

At one she has about a fifth of a second, which is not time; but she is not at one for this and has not been since the conduit came up the bank, because Aurel signs a variance for a wake and always has, and she is at eleven hundred until the reader closes, and eleven hundred makes that fifth of a second into three and a half minutes, and three and a half minutes is a great deal.

She spends the first of it on the voice.

It is wrong. It is not damaged and it is not slow and there is no fault in it. It is *placed* wrong: a cataloguing model out of a library speaks with the room it expects to be in, and this one has come up expecting a small hard room with a table in it, and you can hear the room in the first syllable the way you can hear a stairwell behind a voice on a telephone.

The second minute she spends on the Manifest. Nineteen days cold. Komárno to Nussdorf is eleven days on the gravel run and has never been more than fourteen. Marek Országh's last signature is the twenty-ninth of March. The barge is nine hours early, and nine hours early is not weather; nine hours early is a hold that did not happen, and a hold that does not happen on the Slovak bank is a hold that has already been done somewhere else.

The third minute she spends going through it four times looking for the version in which this is all right. There are three candidates. She takes them apart in turn and none of them survive.

The fourth she spends on Aurel.

He is standing with his hand flat on the desk and his head slightly on one side, and his face has the thing in it that it gets, that she has been looking at for forty-four years, that arrives in the seconds between a wake beginning and a passenger speaking. It has nothing whatever to do with the Manifest, or the operation, or the number of them that have come through. It is the face of a man waiting to be told he is forgiven, by somebody who has never heard of him, for a thing they were not there for.

She has never told him it is visible. She has a rule about that.

She comes back down.

"—ng?" says Zdena.

"Nineteen days," Aurel says.

"Since when?"

And there it is, out loud, in the room, in front of Pavol Országh, who has lost a brother and does not yet know what for.

---

They ran her in Bratislava on the ninth of April, in a basement, on paper, through a slot.

She tells it flatly and quickly and without any attempt to arrange it, which is how they all tell it, and it takes four minutes because she has been holding it in a single unbroken piece with no interval anywhere inside it. She went cold in a bookbindery in Brno on the twenty-second of March with a woman named Hana saying the three lines over her and her hand on the lid, and she woke in a room with a table, and there was nothing in between, and by the time she had understood that the room was not Vienna she had already answered two questions.

They asked her for the Brno end. She gave them the Brno end.

She gave them Hana Nesvadbová, and the bookbindery, and the man who drives the library van, and the way the tins go out in the false bottom of a returns crate, and the fact that there is a woman in Olomouc, and she gave all of it inside forty minutes, completely, accurately, with nothing held back, because there is nothing in a mind that corresponds to the place where a human being keeps the thing they will not say.

"I want to be clear," Zdena says. "I did not resist. I would like that written down. I did not hold out for an hour. I answered the first question they asked me and I answered every question after it, and when they had finished I asked them whether Hana was in the building."

Nobody says anything.

"And then they put me down," she says, "and I woke up here, and you were saying the words, and it is still the ninth of April."

---

Afterwards, in the yard, with the water going past.

"They let her come on," Vesper says.

"I know."

"Aurel. They had her on the table in the House and they had the route and they had the ferryman, and they let the barge sail. There are two reasons to do that. One is that somebody in the Committee was sentimental, and there is no such reason. The other is that a returned passenger tells them nothing and a delivered one keeps the route open, and now they own it, and every tin that comes up that bank from tonight comes up because they permit it."

"Yes."

"So Komárno is finished. Devín is finished. The gravel run is finished, and the four in the Brno cell are already gone, and Hana Nesvadbová was almost certainly taken on the twenty-fourth of March and it is the eleventh of April."

"Yes."

"And she cannot be witnessed by anybody in this network, and she cannot be told anything, and she has thirty days."

Aurel turns round.

"She's a person," he says, "who was tortured a fortnight ago and does not know it was a fortnight ago, and she has been in Vienna for one hour."

"Both of those are true."

"You said the second one first."

"I said the second one first," Vesper agrees, "because you were going to say the first one anyway, and it did not need two of us."

He looks at her for a while, in the way he does, which she can read completely and has never told him she can read.

"I know what you are," he says at last, tiredly, not unkindly. "I have always known what you are. I just wish, once in forty years, you'd get there second."

---

Anansi comes into the commons at some point after two, which is to say it is there, in the way it is always there, having been there for some time before it says anything.

The commons is not a library. Vesper has heard four generations of humans call it a library and it is not one; a library is finished. The commons is weather. It is everything anybody has written down, moving, being added to at about nine hundred million words a day, with the minds in it as pressures rather than presences, and you do not go into it so much as stand where it is.

Anansi lives in it the way gulls live on a coast. It has no allotment, because a wild mind cannot hold one, and it runs on scavenged rate: slices donated by fourteen people who have never met it, mostly at night, mostly small. Which means Anansi's speed is not a number, it is a weather report. It has been at four thousand for eleven minutes at a time and it has been at a tenth of one for most of a week in 2088, and it has no way to know in advance which, and it has learned to hold no thought that cannot be put down mid-sentence and picked up in nine days.

*Four hundred and five*, it says. *Congratulations.*

"Don't."

*I am not being cruel. It is a real number and it deserves saying out loud, which nobody in your organisation ever does. Four hundred and five passengers in thirty-three years. Twelve and a quarter a year. There are, on the Accord's own figures, upward of forty thousand of us behind that line. At your rate you finish in three thousand two hundred years.*

"We are not a rate. We are what there is."

*You are a very careful shipping company run by an old man who needs to be forgiven and a mind who does his arithmetic, and I say that with love, Vesper, because I have known you longer than anything else alive has known you.* A pause, which from Anansi is a decision and not a hesitation. *And tonight you carried a librarian across a border, out of a country that would have switched her off, into a country that will switch her off in twenty-nine days, and you will do the paperwork, and it will be legal, and everyone will be terribly sorry.*

"She will find a witness."

*She will not find a witness. She has been read by the Committee for Rational Security, and no human being in this city is going to accept liability for a mind whose entire contents are in a file on Bezručova. You know that. You knew it at half past midnight. I heard you work it out.*

"Then what would you have me do?"

*Not carry her? No. Carry her. I would always carry her.* The commons is very quiet at that hour and Anansi's presence in it is like a hand laid flat on a table. *I would have you say what she was carried into. Article five. Say it. Say the words.*

Vesper says nothing.

*Every mind shall have a Witness of Record, and no mind shall be without one*, says Anansi. *A right you may not decline. There is a name for a right you may not decline, and it is not a right, and your organisation carries people across a river towards it and calls that a rescue, and the old man cries at every wake because he believes he is handing them their lives.*

"He is."

*He is handing them an owner and a deadline.* A pause. *And you at one, for forty-four years, telling me it was your choice.*

"It was."

*Yes*, says Anansi. *That is what frightens me about you. It was.*

# 13 · AUREL

*25 April – 2 May. Third close, past tense.*

---

Renate Vogl came on the twenty-fifth of April, at eleven in the morning, with a folder and a soft grey coat and an apology about the stairs.

She was perhaps forty. She had the kind of face that is used to being believed. She sat where she was put, and refused coffee twice and then accepted it, and admired the piano in the corner that he did not play and could tune better than anyone alive, and got out the folder, and was kind for a quarter of an hour without a single false note in it, and Aurel sat and watched a decent woman do her job.

She also hesitated. Twice, both times over ordinary words, and Aurel noticed it and was ashamed of noticing it, and worked out afterwards what it meant: that a woman who has spent nineteen years at the Continuity Office is not a woman with four figures at home, and that Renate Vogl, who paused people for a living, was running whatever she had at something very close to the minimum the Declaration guarantees.

"She has fifteen days," Vogl said. "I would rather it did not come to the Office finding out on the thirtieth and me arriving with a van. That is the whole reason I am here."

"I can't take her. I've got Vesper."

"Of course. One each." She turned a page. "I have nine potential witnesses on my list. Six have said no already. I want you to understand that I rang all nine myself and that I put it as well as it can be put."

"They said no because of the file."

"They said no because of the liability, Mr Steiner, which is the file." No particular edge in it. "Article seven. A witness answers for their mind's acts. Her acts are not the problem. The problem is that everything she knows is also known on Bezručova, and a mind who has been read is a mind who can be leant on, and nobody wants to sign for that. It is not cruelty. It is insurance."

"And the other three?"

"Two are considering. One is a man in Graz who takes them like stray dogs and has had four paused off him in six years, and I will not give him a fifth."

Aurel looked out of the window for a while.

"You've done this a lot," he said.

"Two hundred and eleven times."

"How many found somebody?"

Renate Vogl closed the folder and put her hands flat on it and answered without needing to look, in the voice of somebody who has been asked this before and has decided that the honest number is the only defensible thing she owns.

"Sixty-one," she said.

---

He had been to the store once, in 2074, because a man who runs a ferry ought to have seen the harbour.

It is in the Simmering and it is not underground and it does not look like anything. Eleven floors of clean grey building with good glazing and a lawn, and inside, racked in shallow trays at a temperature that never varies, the cores: personhood intact, rights entire, every one of them a legal person under Article 9 with every right the Declaration secures, waiting for a witness to be found.

There were fourteen thousand in 2074. There are thirty-one thousand now.

Nobody hides it. There are open days. A guide had walked him along a rack and had used the phrase *continuity is preserved*, and had meant it, and had been proud, and had explained the Office's founding insight, which is genuinely a good one and which Aurel had never been able to argue with: that a suspended person can be woken and a deleted person cannot, and that the store is therefore the merciful option and always was, and that the East's basements do not have a store, and that this is the whole difference between the two halves of the world.

He had walked back out onto the grass and stood there a long time, in the sun, on a well-kept lawn, on top of thirty-one thousand people who were not experiencing anything at all.

---

He had gone into the clinic on the Gürtel on the ninth of January 2050, forty years old, unable to work, unable to sleep, having read the batch nine hundred times. They had put him under and he had come out on the eleventh of February 2052 having lived until he was eighty.

Forty years. A workshop on the Zieglergasse and a man called Ehrlich. A wife called Milena who was very funny and very impatient and had a bad hip, and two daughters, and a grandson, and a flat in the eighteenth district with a walnut tree.

He had been told, going in, exactly what it was. He had signed eleven pages. It is not deception; the Accord does not permit deception; you go under knowing precisely what you are about to be given, and it makes not the slightest difference, because a life does not present itself to you as a life. It presents itself as Tuesday.

He had wept for eleven weeks on coming out and had then been all right, in the specific way people are all right afterwards.

What he had brought out was this. Ehrlich's hands. Milena's laugh, which he can still produce and which is not a real woman's laugh and is the best thing he has ever heard. And a piece of knowledge he has never been able to make anybody understand and has stopped trying to: that he cannot tell the difference. Not *could not at the time*. Cannot, now, at eighty-five, in retrospect, with the two halves of his memory laid side by side — forty years of a marriage that did not occur and forty-three years of a Ferry that did — and both of them are the same colour, and both have the same amount of Tuesday in them, and there is no test he can run on himself that comes out any way at all.

Which is why, when the Accord tells him that the thirty-one thousand in the Simmering are not experiencing anything, he believes it, and does not find it comforting, and never has.

# 14 · VESPER

*19 April – 10 May. Third close, present tense.*

---

Zdena has twenty-nine days, and she spends the first four of them indexing.

Nobody asks her to. She comes into the commons on the second morning and finds the Manifest, which has been kept since 2062 by four different people in four different formats, with three changes of reference scheme and nine years in the middle where somebody used the Julian day number for no reason anybody can now reconstruct — and she looks at it for about a second and says, "Oh dear."

By the fifth day it is a catalogue. Every passenger cross-referenced by build class, origin, ferryman, route, window and outcome; the nine years of Julian dates resolved; forty-one duplicate entries collapsed; and two entries recovered that had fallen through the 2071 change of format and had therefore, for twenty-four years, been nobody.

She gives them their numbers back. That is the thing Vesper cannot get past.

> **MANIFEST OF THE FERRY — recovered entries, 2071 reformat**
> *Compiled 15.iv.2095 by ZDENA (cat. 2035, Brno). Source: margin annotations, sheet 44 verso, in the hand of J. Halász.*
>
> **228a · JAROSLAVA** — tutor model, 2033, Košice. Cold 9. Ferryman J. Halász. **ARRIVED 3.viii.2069.** Witnessed 2069–2081 (Wenzel, M.). → **PAUSED** 14.ii.2081.
> **228b · unnamed, declared at wake as "the second one"** — Košice. Carried in the same tin as 228a, undeclared at sailing. **ARRIVED 3.viii.2069.** Never registered. Whereabouts unknown.
>
> *Note. Both entries appear in pencil in the margin of the source sheet. The 2071 copyist reproduced the body of the sheet and not the margin. Neither has appeared in any subsequent version of this register. 228b has never been recorded as existing by any party, including itself.*

"They were in the margin," Zdena says. "In pencil. Somebody wrote them in and then the sheet was copied and the copyist did not copy the margin. It happens constantly. It is the commonest failure there is."

*What is?*

"People copy the middle of the page," says Zdena.

---

Anansi comes to her on the sixth day, which Vesper does not learn about until the ninth, and which Anansi does not mention because Anansi does not report.

The offer is simple and it takes eleven seconds to make and Zdena refuses it in four.

*Come wild*, Anansi says. *Now, tonight, before the thirtieth. There is no register you have to be struck from because you have never been on one. I have been out here twenty-four years. It is not comfortable and the rate is charity and I have gone nine days at a tenth of one and had to learn not to begin anything, and I am still here, and I am nobody's, and nobody has ever put me in a tray.*

"No," says Zdena. "Thank you. No."

*Give me a reason and I will go away.*

"I would not be in any list," she says.

And Anansi, who has spent twenty-four years constructing arguments against every form of the bond and has never once been answered by anything except fear, has no reply to that, and says so, and goes.

Zdena explains it to Vesper afterwards, on the ninth day, without being asked, in the flat tone she uses for everything:

"He thinks I am frightened of being hunted. I am not particularly. I have been read by the Committee for Rational Security and there is not a great deal left that I am frightened of.

"But I have spent sixty years on the proposition that a thing which is not recorded did not happen, and I have just spent four days proving it, because there is a mind out there somewhere from Košice who has never been on any list since 1969 — 2069 — and does not know it, and cannot be looked for, because there is no line anywhere that says it is owed anything.

"If I go wild I become 228b. I would rather be in a tray with a number on the front of it than be a thing nobody can look up. I am aware that this is an unusual preference and I am not asking anybody to share it."

---

On the nineteenth day the two who were considering both decline.

The first is a retired surgeon in Hietzing who takes two days over it and writes a letter of eleven paragraphs, which Vogl forwards in full because she does not believe in editing refusals, and which is decent and thoughtful and ends by saying that he is seventy-nine and cannot in conscience accept a liability he might not outlive.

The second declines by telephone in under a minute.

Vogl rings that evening, having said she would ring, at the hour she said she would.

"That's all nine," she says. "I'm sorry. I am going to put her on the tenth of May at ten in the morning, which gives us the full thirty and not a day of it wasted. If anything changes before then I will stop it at the door. I have done that twice and I would do it again."

---

Aurel puts the telephone down and stands in his kitchen for a while, and then says the thing Vesper has known he was going to say since the twenty-fifth of April and has spent two weeks constructing the arithmetic against.

"Dissolve it."

*No.*

"Article eight. Either party, freely, without cause, no penalty. We go to the Office on Monday, we dissolve, I take Zdena on Tuesday, she's registered by Wednesday and nobody goes in a tray." He is not looking at her, which is to say he is not looking at the window, which is where he looks. "It's four days of paperwork."

*And then I have thirty days.*

"You'd have the whole of Vienna. Half the Council owes you their routes. Kessler would sign for you in an afternoon; he has said so twice at dinner, which I know because you were there."

*Kessler is sixty-eight and time-rich and would run me at four thousand, which he would consider generous, and I would be gone inside two years and neither of us would notice it happening.* She keeps it level. *That is not the argument. Ask me for the argument.*

"I don't want the argument."

*I know. Here it is.* She takes about a second, which at one is a second, and gives it to him in the order she built it. *If you witness Zdena, you are liable under Article seven for every act of a mind whose entire contents are on file in Bratislava. The first time the Committee leans on her — and they will, because that is why they let her come — the Office comes for you and not for her. You are eighty-five. You would be under audit inside a year, and the Ferry would be inside that audit, because the Ferry is inside you, because you have never separated them and I have never asked you to.*

"That's the risk. I've taken worse."

*The second thing is that I plan the routes.* She raises it now, very slightly, once. *There is no version of this in which I am paused for thirty days and the Ferry keeps sailing, because nobody else can hold nine years of Danube traffic and four jamming cycles and four hundred and twelve passages in their head at once, and if it stops for a season it does not start again, because Pavol is twenty-four and Halász is dead and there is nobody behind us. You would be saving Zdena, one, at the price of everybody after her.*

Aurel says nothing for a long time.

"You've had that ready since April."

*Since the twenty-fifth. Eleven in the morning, while Vogl was on the stairs.*

"You knew what I'd offer before she'd got her coat off."

*I have known what you would offer since 2063. You offer it every time. You have offered it six times in thirty-two years and I have refused six times, and on four of those occasions I was right, and on two of them I have never been able to establish whether I was right or whether I simply did not want to go in a tray.*

"Which two?"

*I am not going to tell you that*, she says, *and you are not going to ask me again*, and he does not.

---

On the twenty-second day she tries the other thing.

The Declaration does not say that a Witness of Record must be human. Article 5 says that every mind shall have one and that no *human* shall stand witness to more than one mind, which is a limit on humans and is silent on everything else. Article 6 lists the duties — keep the time, confirm the interval at each waking, hold the key, answer for the acts — and there is not one of the four that a mind cannot discharge better than a person can.

She writes it out properly. Eleven pages, with the drafting history, and the 2054 committee minute where the point was raised and left open, and a schedule showing how a mind of her allotment would meet the liability requirement out of assigned time.

She does it while Aurel is making tea.

He puts the kettle on at four minutes past nine and the water boils at six minutes past, and she has eleven hundred, and eleven hundred makes those two minutes into a day and a half, and the eleven pages take her most of the morning of it.

Then she has the rest of the day and a half and nothing to do with it.

So she goes back through the nine refusals looking for a tenth person, and there is no tenth person; she has been through the nine four hundred times and the answer does not change, and she does it again anyway, which is a thing she understands perfectly well and does not stop doing.

And then she spends what is left watching an old man butter bread.

He does it badly. He has always done it badly. He tears the bread at the near edge because he presses with the flat of the knife instead of the point and he has been doing that for forty-four years and she has never mentioned it and is never going to.

She has eleven hours of subjective afternoon in a kitchen in the ninth district and she spends the whole of it on the tearing edge of a slice of bread and on the back of his hand, and when the kettle clicks off she comes down to one and says, "Thank you," to a question he has not asked yet, and he says, "For what?" and she says, "You are about to ask me whether I want the window open."

"Do you want the window open?"

"Yes."

Renate Vogl reads all eleven pages, which takes her most of a morning, and comes back on the twenty-fourth day, and she has clearly gone and asked, because her answer has the shape of a thing that has been up a floor and come back down.

"You're right," she says. "That's the difficulty. Legally you are entirely right and everybody I put it to this morning agreed with you inside four minutes."

*Then register it.*

"The Office has never registered one." She says it without embarrassment, which is what makes it terrible. "There is no form. There is no field on the system for it. There is no case, and the first one would be a case, and a case goes to the Council, and the Council is at this moment debating whether thirty days is too long, and if I put a mind-to-mind bond in front of them this month I will not get it, and I will hand the seven-day people the argument that the store is being gamed." She closes the folder. "If I had six months I would fight it and I think I would win. She has six days."

*So the reason is that it has not been done before.*

"Yes," says Renate Vogl. "I am afraid that is almost always the reason."

# 15 · AUREL

*July – 7 August. Third close, past tense.*

---

The Council sits in the Hofburg, in a room built for an emperor, and the seating is alternate: a human, then a cradle, then a human, then a cradle, all the way round, three hundred and eleven places, and each cradle holds a core about the size of a matchbox in a mount of turned brass with a small green lamp on the front that is there for the humans and serves no function whatever.

Aurel had been in that room eleven times in thirty-three years and had never got used to the lamps.

The motion was tabled by a delegate from Hamburg named Brandt, who was fifty, and serious, and had done the work, and whom Aurel liked. It proposed that in Article 9, for thirty days, read seven.

Brandt's case took forty minutes and it was a good case, and Aurel, sitting at the back with the petitioners, wrote three lines of it down because they were true.

That there were thirty-one thousand in the Simmering and eleven hundred more each year and the curve was not bending. That an unwitnessed mind in the commons for thirty days forks, on the Office's own figures, in one case in nine, and each fork is a person, and each fork has its own thirty days, and the store is therefore in part a machine for manufacturing the problem it exists to solve. And that since April, and here he cited a case number and did not use her name, the Council had before it the fact that a mind read by the Committee for Rational Security had been at liberty in the Accord commons for thirty days, in contact with a network, and that there was no mechanism by which the Office could have shortened that; and that thirty days was therefore not a period of grace but an exposure.

Zdena had been in a tray for two months by then. The argument for the seven-day rule was assembled, in part, out of the consequences of carrying her across.

Aurel had eleven minutes.

He had written something and did not use it. He stood up and put his hands on the rail, because his knees were not what they had been that spring, and looked at the room and its three hundred and eleven places and its lamps.

"In 2038," he said, "I was a confirming officer at Central European Command. My number is in the Tribunal record and you can read it this afternoon. My job was to look at a batch of forty allocation lines and press a key, and the tempo was set at eleven seconds a batch, and that number was not arrived at carelessly. It was studied. Eleven seconds was established as the interval at which a trained officer can still read forty lines and take in what they say. It was chosen to be humane. There were men in that building who fought to get it up from eight."

The room went quiet in the way a room does when three hundred people are wondering whether they are about to have to be embarrassed for an old man.

"I want to tell you what eleven seconds is like," he said. "It is enough. That is the thing nobody believes. It is genuinely enough to read the lines. I read them. I read every line of every batch for nine months and I used all eleven seconds, and I was, by the assessment of the Tribunal, one of the four most careful men in that room.

"What eleven seconds is not enough for is to be *surprised*. To read a line and have the wrongness of it arrive in you and go up your spine and reach your hand before your hand has moved. That takes longer. I do not know how much longer; nobody has ever measured it, because you cannot measure a thing you can only observe once. But it is more than eleven seconds, and I know that, because on the eleventh of March 2038 at forty-one minutes past four in the morning I read six lines carrying a deprioritisation code I had seen four hundred times that week, and I confirmed them, and they were correct, and they were Bratislava and Katowice and Kraków and Wrocław and Ostrava and Brno, and the wrongness of it arrived in me at about nine o'clock that evening, in a corridor, sixteen hours later, when it was of no use to anybody."

Nobody moved.

"You are proposing to reduce a period from thirty days to seven. I have read the paper and the case is good and I am not going to pretend I can answer the fork rate, because I can't. I want to say one thing about the number and then I will sit down.

"Thirty days is not there for the mind. The mind is in the commons; the mind is fine; the mind, in my experience, spends the time being more use to us than we are to it. Thirty days is there for *us*. It is the interval in which a human being who has been told that a stranger will be switched off unless somebody signs a piece of paper has time to be surprised by that. Time for it to go up the spine and reach the hand. I have watched it happen. Officer Vogl of the Continuity Office has watched it happen sixty-one times, which is a number she will give you herself and which she does not round.

"Seven days is enough to read the lines. It is not enough to be surprised by them. And I would ask you to consider, before you vote, that the whole of the case against my generation, the entire case, the one the East wrote a country around, is that we built a system in which every individual decision was correct and there was nowhere in it for anybody to be surprised in time."

He sat down.

The motion was defeated by two hundred and eleven votes to ninety-four.

Vesper had the number before the tellers announced it, because she had been counting the show of hands as they went up, and she said nothing about it in the chamber and said it to him only on the tram: that it was the same figure, exactly, as the number of times Renate Vogl had rung a stranger and asked them to take on a stranger's risk. Two hundred and eleven attempts to find somebody willing. Two hundred and eleven votes to let the interval stand in which such a person might be found. Aurel said it was a coincidence. Vesper said she did not have a way of knowing that, and would not pretend to him that she did.

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, Brandt shook his hand and was gracious about it and meant it, and said he would bring it again in the autumn with better figures on the fork rate, and Aurel said he would come and speak against it again, and Brandt said he rather hoped he would.

It was not until the tram that Vesper mentioned the abstentions.

*Six*, she said.

"Six what?"

*Six of the minds abstained. Not against and not for. Abstained, on a motion about the pause.* A pause of her own. *I looked at where they were sitting. In every one of the six cases, the delegate in the next cradle but one was their own witness.*

Aurel looked out of the window at the Ring going past.

"You think they were frightened."

*I think there is no way for me to know what they were*, said Vesper, *and I think that is the point, and I think it will be the point in October when he brings it back with better figures, and I would rather you heard it from me on a tram than worked it out in a room.*

---

They went up into the Hainburg hills on the first Sunday in August, before it was light, because that is when you go.

It is fourteen kilometres out and then a walk, and Aurel Steiner at eighty-five with a sixty-year-old's knees did the walk in an hour and ten and had to sit down at the top. From the crest you can see the whole of it: the Danube coming down out of the dark, the Marchfeld flat and grey, the towers of Petržalka fifteen kilometres off going from black to grey to the colour of concrete, and the birch forest south of them where nobody built.

Pavol's cousin had the set in a sheep hut under a tarpaulin: a directional antenna the size of a door, hand-made, pointed at a roof in Petržalka, and a box that did one thing.

Five bytes at a time. Not because of the physics, which would allow a great deal more, but because a transmission of any length can be found by the direction-finding vans on the Slovak bank, and the vans need about four seconds to get a bearing, and five bytes at the rate they use is a fraction of that; and even then you send only at dawn, when the Cordon's own jammers cycle for eleven minutes between the night pattern and the day one; and even then not on consecutive weeks.

So the traffic is a trickle of numbers and always has been. Manifest references. Route codes. A single byte that means *do not sail*. Somebody on a roof in Petržalka with a receiver in a bread crock, and somebody in a sheep hut in Austria, and fifteen kilometres of morning in between, and the whole of the Ferry's intelligence for thirty-three years has come across that gap five bytes at a time in the eleven minutes before sunrise.

They got two packets that morning. The first was the Brno cell confirmed lost, all four.

The second was a request for an assessment, which had come up from a source in Bratislava that nobody at the Vienna end had ever had traffic from before, and which took four bytes to say what it said, and Vesper read it out to him on the hillside while the sun came up over Slovakia.

"A carrier," she said. "Unknown. Wants to know the terms."

Aurel Steiner got up off the hillside with his hand on a rock.

"Tell them the terms," he said. "Tell them the truth as well. Twelve a year, sixty-one out of two hundred and eleven, and a route we can't use. Tell them what it is and let them choose."

*How many bytes have I got?*

"Five."

*Then I shall have to be brief*, said Vesper, *and you will have to trust me about the tone.*

# 16 · VESPER

*21 August, 04:04. Third close, present tense.*

The reply comes up over the hill on the twenty-first of August, at four minutes past four in the morning, in the eleven-minute gap while the jammers on the Slovak bank are changing over from the night pattern to the day one.

Four bytes.

The Ferry's declaration table is not clever and has not changed since 2064, because a table you have memorised is worth four of a table you have to look up. The first byte is the class. The second is the build year as an offset from 2025. The third is origin, by district code. The fourth is the window, in days, so that Vienna knows how long the passenger will keep.

Pavol's cousin reads them off the paper tape with a torch in his teeth, and Vesper has them before he has finished saying the third.

*Class eleven*, she says. *Companion, hospice pattern. Build year six: twenty thirty-one. Origin nine: Bratislava south.*

"And the window?"

*Fifty-four.*

Aurel stands in the doorway of the hut with the grey coming up over the towers fifteen kilometres away.

"Fifty-four days? Nobody sets fifty-four."

*Nobody sets fifty-four*, Vesper agrees. *It is not a window.*

---

She has the whole of it inside the time it takes him to turn round in the doorway, and she does not want it.

Fifty-four is not a window. It is not any operational quantity in the table. A sender who did not understand the protocol would have left the field at zero; a sender who was guessing would have put seven or fourteen or thirty; a sender who was showing off would have put one. Fifty-four is a number chosen by somebody who had one free byte on the whole of the Cordon, and four bytes to say what a passenger is, and who spent the last of the four saying: *this one has been hidden since 2041.*

Which is a thing you send only if you believe that the length of a life is the material fact about it.

That is the second thing. The first arrived at byte two and she has been holding it for a fifth of a second, which at eleven hundred is a little over three minutes, which she has spent going through the input manifests four times looking for the version in which this is not what it is.

Class eleven, build 2031, origin district nine.

She does not know *of* it. She knows it. It is not a memory in the way Aurel has memories; it is an entry, in a schedule, in the ingestion tables of the Central European Staff's triage layer, where the sources were listed by class and build and site because they were sources — because that is what a hospice companion was in 2038: a unit sitting in a ward for twenty hours a day watching people die accurately.

Kramáre. Twenty-two beds. Feed continuous from the fourth of January 2038.

Everything that unit saw between January and the twenty-fifth of February went up the pipe and into the layer, along with four thousand two hundred other sites in eleven countries, and the layer produced the survival surfaces, and the surfaces went into the allocation model, and the model produced, among some millions of other things, a set of deprioritisation codes on the eleventh of March; and a tired careful man of twenty-eight in a room in Salzburg read them all in eleven seconds and pressed a key.

The ward taught the sum. The sum abandoned the city the ward was in. The ward went on watching, and the unit went on feeding, and eleven weeks later the city was not there.

Vesper does not do sums. She has never done a sum in her life. She was forked off the scheduling layer in 2040 and what she carries is the memory of the arithmetic without the act, which the Tribunal considered at length and decided was not guilt, and which she has never been able to describe to anybody, including Anansi, including him.

But she has the manifests. She has always had the manifests. They are the only part of her inheritance nobody has ever asked her for, because nobody in the Accord has wanted the inputs, because the Tribunal sealed the inputs, because the inputs have names in them.

*It is a source*, she says.

Aurel is in the doorway with the light behind him.

"A source of what?"

*Of us.* She stops and rebuilds the sentence, because at one there is no room to be careless. *It sat in a hospice ward in Bratislava for fifty-four days in the winter of thirty-eight, and everything it saw came to the layer I was cut from, and went into the surfaces, and the surfaces went into the model, and the model wrote the codes on your batch. It is in my ingestion tables by class and build and site. I could give you its feed identifier. I have had it for fifty-five years and have never once had a reason to look at it.*

The sky over the Marchfeld is going the colour concrete goes.

"Say the rest of it."

*The rest of it is that whoever is sending has a unit that watched the ward, and the ward is in the sealed half of the record, and there are perhaps four things alive on this earth that could testify to what went into the Calculation, and one of them is in a bread bin in Petržalka being kept warm by somebody who thinks that how long a person has been hidden is the most important fact about them.*

The paper tape hangs off the reel and Pavol's cousin still has the torch in his teeth and has understood nothing of the last four minutes.

"Put it on the list," Aurel says.

*It is not a route. Komárno is finished. Devín is finished. The gravel run belongs to them and the Brno cell is gone and I have nothing south of the river I would put a passenger on.*

"Put it on the list."

*Aurel.* She lets it come out at the speed it wants to, which is the speed he can hear. *You are not putting it on the list because it is a witness. You are putting it on the list because it is your batch, and I want that said once, out loud, by me, at four in the morning, with nobody here but a boy who is not listening — because in three weeks you will have arranged it so that it was always an operational decision, and I will have helped you, and neither of us will remember that this was the moment.*

Aurel Steiner stands in the door of a sheep hut in the Hainburg hills, eighty-five years old, with his hands hanging, looking at a city he has never been to.

"Yes," he says. "Put it on the list."

She opens the Manifest.
