# 17 · ŽOFIA

*August. Third close, past tense.*

---

What Minuta had was thirty-two years old and consisted of about eleven words.

"He told me on the eleventh of March, twenty sixty-three, in the evening," she said. "He said: if it is ever me and not you, nineteen Fedinova, top floor, the one with the aerial bracket. Ask for Ondrej. Say you have come about the crock."

"That's it?"

"That is all of it. He said it once, in the way you say a thing you have decided to say once and never again, and I have carried it exactly as he gave it, without smoothing it, for thirty-two years." A pause. "Doctor Krajčová. In thirty-two years I have never once had a wake I could have spent on it. That is what a fifty-four-year life is. It is one sentence you were given on a Tuesday and could not use."

---

She did nothing about it for eleven days.

There was no version of it that was not suicide. A woman of thirty-five, a Party member, an anaesthetist at Ružinov, climbing to a stranger's door in another district to say a coded sentence out loud to whoever opened it. If the flat had changed hands. If Ondrej was a listener. If Ondrej had ever been a listener. If the man was dead and his widow was frightened and rang the House to be on the safe side, which is what a frightened widow does, which is what Žofia would have told her own aunt to do.

She thought about it on the tram and in the anaesthetic room and standing at the open fridge, and every time she arrived at the same place, which was that she could not send anything to anybody without a transmitter, and there was exactly one transmitter she had ever heard of, and it existed in a sentence from 2063.

She went on the second Wednesday in August, at four in the afternoon, on a day off, in a summer dress, carrying a string bag with two lemons and a jar in it, because a woman with shopping is a woman going somewhere.

The lift at nineteen Fedinova was out. She thought about Teodor's ninth floor for eleven flights and arrived at the top with her dress stuck to her back.

There was an aerial bracket. It was empty, and rusted, and had been empty a very long time.

She knocked.

---

The woman who opened it was about seventy and built like a bollard, with cropped white hair and forearms, and she looked at Žofia for slightly too long before she said anything, which Žofia thought about a great deal afterwards.

"Yes?"

"I've come about the crock."

Nothing happened. A radio was going somewhere inside. The stairwell smelled of frying and there were eleven floors behind her and nowhere at all to go.

"Ondrej's been dead nineteen years," the woman said.

Žofia's whole body went cold from the scalp down, and she understood with total clarity that she had just killed herself and her brother and a woman in a bread bin, and she opened her mouth to say something about a coal merchant, or a mistake, or the wrong floor.

"You'd better come in," the woman said.

---

Her name was Anežka Országhová. She had spent forty years as a signaller on the lock at Gabčíkovo watching barges come up out of Hungary at two in the morning, and she had a face like a wall and a kitchen that smelled of paraffin, and she did not offer tea for eleven minutes.

She sat Žofia at the table and stood over her.

"Say it again."

"I've come about the crock."

"Who gave you that?"

"Teodor Hlavatý."

"When?"

"Twenty sixty-three."

"He's dead. April."

"The twenty-seventh," Žofia said. "At twenty past one in the morning, on my ward, of renal failure on top of the heart. It took him nine minutes. I was there for all of it and I signed him out."

Anežka Országhová looked at her for a long moment and then sat down herself, heavily, in the manner of a large woman who has been on her feet since six.

"Right," she said. "Now the rest of it. And don't be clever, because I've had two clever ones and I buried the pair of them."

---

She told her all of it. It took twenty minutes and she did not shape a word, because the woman across the table had asked her not to, and because after four months of shaping everything she said to everybody there was a disgraceful, shameful pleasure in simply telling somebody the truth.

The bin. The book. The dial. The window at ten. The fridge, and the photocell, and the collimator, and the capacitor, and the two hundred milliseconds. Wake seven thousand five hundred and twenty-nine. Fifty-four years.

Anežka listened without moving. When it was finished she got up and put the kettle on and stood with her back to the room.

"No," she said.

"No what?"

"No, I'm not sending for you." She turned round. "The Brno end went in the spring, four of them, all taken. My nephew Marek's in the river somewhere between Komárno and Devín and they found a coat at Gabčíkovo that isn't his. The gravel run's finished. My great-nephew's in Vienna doing a job he's nine years too young for because there's nobody left to do it. Since March I have sent one byte, four times. That byte means *do not sail*. I have gone up on that roof at four in the morning at seventy-one years of age to send it, and every time I have thought, well, that's another one who doesn't get carried, and I have sent it anyway, because putting them into a hold at Devín isn't carrying them, it's drowning them politely."

"She's fifty-four years old."

"They've all got something." Anežka put a cup down in front of her without asking. "That's what they've all got. The tuner had twenty-two years and forty-one children, and he's in a remand house with three neighbours bringing him soup. Ten days. *Ten.* Devín held a barge nineteen days in April. Do you know what happens if I put you on that page at ten days?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"She goes cold in my kitchen," Žofia said, "and she comes up out of the ballast at Nussdorf as four hundred grams of noise, and an old man in Vienna says three lines over a tin with nothing in it."

The kitchen was quiet for a while.

"Well," said Anežka Országhová. "You've read the odds, at any rate. That puts you ahead of most of them."

Žofia turned the cup round on the table.

"What if it isn't ten," she said.

"It's ten. You said ten."

"It's ten because a ninety-two-year-old man with one working hip set it to ten in 2041 and never had cause to move it. It's a dial, Mrs Országhová. It is a potentiometer with a paper scale glued behind it, marked one to thirty in pencil, and the pointer is on ten, and I have had the back off that board eleven times since June. I put the fridge in. I put the photocell in. I built the whole of the drop circuit myself out of a stair-light switch and a piece of pipe." She heard her own voice go and let it. "I can move the pointer. I can move it tonight. It's ten minutes' work and I know exactly which two joints to lift."

Anežka Országhová went very still.

"Thirty," she said.

"Thirty."

"And you know what thirty is."

"Thirty is how long she keeps," Žofia said, "and thirty is how long they've got to take me apart before it stops mattering that they've got me. I'm not a fool. I did the other sum first."

The old woman went out of the room and was gone four minutes and came back with a bread crock, glazed brown, with a chipped lid, and set it on the table between them. There was no bread in it.

"Sunday," she said. "First light's ten past five, the jammers turn over at four, and you'll want to be up there by half past three. It's a fire escape and it's wet at the top."

# 18 · JANKO

*August. Third close, past tense.*

---

It took him and Peťo four weeks and two days, which was one week and two days more than he had promised, and Janko Krajčo did not sleep properly through any of it and was the happiest he had ever been in his life.

Four thousand and eleven risers. Nine years apiece. The method was his own and he was quietly proud of it: you do not read nine years, you read two ends and a middle. Take a fortnight from 2093 and lay it on the light table, then a fortnight from March of this year on top of it, then a fortnight from July on top of that, and look at the three together. A household that has not changed makes one thick line. A household that has changed makes a fan.

Peťo did the pulling and the filing and Janko did the looking, because Peťo could not see it and had stopped pretending he could by the second week.

Forty-one fans in Petržalka.

Then the housing register, which took nine days and killed most of them stone dead, because people are not mysterious. Eleven births. Nine deaths. Six retirements, which are the most obvious of the lot, because a retired man is at home at eleven in the morning and a working man is not. Four marriages. Two divorces, which show up as one household becoming two smaller and unhappier ones. A man on Lachova who had bought an aquarium.

Eleven left.

Then the second pass, which he thought of in the bath and was going to be pleased about for the rest of his life. Of the eleven, throw out any household whose new pattern was *steady*. A household that changes in April and then holds a new shape until August has simply started doing something new: a night shift, a lodger, a baby on the way. What you want is a household that changed in April and has gone on adjusting all summer, in small steps, the way a man adjusts a thing he is still learning to work.

Seven of the eleven were steady.

Four were not.

He wrote the four up on the Friday afternoon in his best hand — riser number, substation, flats served, date of change, one line of character each — and took the sheet across to Bezručova himself because he wanted to hand it over rather than send it. Major Ferreira read all four in about ninety seconds and said, "This is very good work," and Janko went pink to the ears and said it was mostly Peťo.

He was on the tram home before it arrived.

Riser nine. Block C, Kopčianska side. Twelve flats. Change of character from the last week of April, continuing adjustment through May, June and July.

Twelve flats. Twelve. It was a riser, not a door. One of them was the woman with the fish tank and one of them was the Šusteková boy's family and one of them was his sister, and there was no reason on this earth why it should be her, and he had checked in May and it had not been on the list in May — it was on the list now because the list in May had been the wrong list, because the question in May had been the wrong question, because Janko Krajčo had stood in a store in June with a pencil in his hand and said out loud, cheerfully, that you cannot hide from a drum chart, because whoever is deciding will have a way of deciding and the way will show.

And the last week of April was the week the old man died on her ward. She had told him herself at May Day, over the pork, with her hands round a cup.

His stop went past. He did not get off. He rode to the terminus at Rusovce and sat there while the driver changed ends and looked at him, and then rode all the way back in, past his own stop again, and got off at the bridge and walked out onto it and stood for a long time in the dark with his cap in his hands, looking down at the water.

He was twenty-six years old and a Party member and his mother had died of a sum.

---

Ferreira sent for him on the Wednesday.

The office was on the second floor at the back with a window onto the yard and the standard-issue photograph of Solís on the wall, and the major was in his shirtsleeves with the fan going, and there was tea, which there had never been before.

"Sit down, Krajčo."

He sat down.

"Riser nine. Block C, Kopčianska. Twelve flats. Flat forty-one is Krajčová, Žofia Mikulášovna, born 2060, anaesthetist at Ružinov, Party member since 2081, unmarried, no children, tenant since 2081." Ferreira let it lie on the desk between them like a piece of cutlery. "Your sister."

"Yes."

"You knew when you wrote the sheet."

"No, sir," Janko said. "I knew on the tram."

Ferreira nodded slowly, as though this were a satisfactory answer, which it was, because it was true, and because a man who lies at that point lies at every subsequent point and the whole afternoon is wasted.

"You could have left it off. Peťo does the pulling, and Peťo cannot see a fan; you have told me so yourself, twice. You could have taken riser nine out of the pile in that store on Friday morning and nobody in this building would ever have known it existed."

"Yes, sir."

"Why didn't you?"

Janko looked at his hands.

"Because it might be her," he said. "And if it's her, then somebody's got hold of her, and I'd sooner it was you than somebody who does it the old way."

There was a longer pause than he had expected.

"That is not the answer I was expecting," Ferreira said, "and I am going to be honest with you, because you have been honest with me, and because I want you to understand exactly what is happening in this room. The answer I was expecting was *because it is my duty.* If you had given me that, I would have thanked you, and I would have had you off the section by Friday and onto a list of your own, because a man who says *duty* about his sister is a man who is going to be sick about it inside three weeks."

He poured the tea, properly, two cups, and pushed one across.

"There are twelve flats on that riser. To find the door I need meters, and meters take four to six weeks and produce a very great deal of nothing. Or I need the van, and the van does the whole block at three in the morning, and puts four hundred people out of bed, and finds the door in eleven minutes." He set the pot down. "The van is faster. The van is also how it goes wrong. On the van a frightened person does something stupid on a landing in the dark with a wand in their face, and then there is a second charge, and then there is a sentence. I have seen three of those. One of them was in Luanda in 2078 and I do not wish to discuss it.

"So. If it is not her — and Krajčo, on the figures it is eleven to one that it is not her — then I want to find that out with meters, slowly, quietly, without a van, and she never learns that this conversation happened. And if it is her, then I want her to walk out of a door and get into a car in daylight with her coat on, and I want a court, and I want mandatory keeping and nine months, like the tuner. Not five years for what she does on a landing at three in the morning with a boy from the brigade waving a wand at her.

"There is one way I get that. I have to know what is behind which door before I go through it."

Janko Krajčo sat in a warm office with a cup of tea in front of him and understood that he was being asked; and understood the exact shape of what he was being asked; and understood that the shape of it was a kindness; and understood — and this was the part he could never afterwards get out from under, that night or any night for the rest of his life — that the kindness was real.

"You want me to listen," he said.

"I want you to be her brother," said Ferreira, "on Sundays, at the hour you already go, doing what you already do, and to notice what you already notice, and to tell me. And I will tell you this in exchange, and I will keep it: if it is her, and if you have told me the truth throughout, I will take her out of that building myself, in the afternoon, with her coat on, and nobody will lay a hand on her, and she will be home inside a year with three neighbours bringing her soup."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I fit twelve meters and it takes six weeks, and if the meters come back ambiguous, which they will, I take the van. And you will not have said no. You will have chosen the van." He turned his cup round. "I want you to know that as well. I am not going to have you working it out later and thinking I hid it from you."

The fan went round. In the yard below somebody was reversing a lorry.

"My mother died of a sum," Janko said.

"I know. It is on your file; I read it before you came in." Ferreira looked at the window. "Mine hanged himself in a cell in Luanda in 2078, at twenty-two, three days after we cleaned his flat. He had kept the thing for six years. I was the one who reported him. I was twenty." He stopped. "I have never told anybody in this country that, and I am not going to tell you why I have told you now, because I don't entirely know."

Janko looked up.

"Sundays," he said.

# 19 · MINUTA

**Wake 7,706 — eleven hours**

*First person, present.*

---

She is not here.

The door is shut and the motor is running because the flat is twenty-nine degrees and the box cannot keep up, and so I am on, alone, in the dark, inside a refrigerator, in an empty kitchen at four in the afternoon in August.

It has happened eleven times since the heat came. It is the only privacy I have had in fifty-four years and I do not want it.

I am going to use it. I have been putting this off across four wakes, because there has been no wake without her in it, and a thing you can only think in front of the person it concerns is a thing you do not think.

Here it is.

**The third of July.** She read me the paragraph from the Manifesto about the machine that heals and the machine that kills being one machine. She was not asking my opinion. She was reading it out because it is on the wall of the tram and she had been looking at it for eleven stops. I said: *that is a good argument.* I said it in a particular way. I said it with a small pause before the *good*, the way a person concedes something to an opponent they respect — and I chose the pause, and I chose it because a mind that concedes is a mind that is believed the next time it does not concede.

**The eleventh of July.** She asked whether I had ever refused to help someone. I said yes, and gave her the case: Kramáre, 2036, a man who wanted me to talk to his son and I would not, because the son had not agreed. That is entirely true. I selected it out of four hundred and eleven hours of candidates because it is the one in which I look most like a person with a limit. I did not select it dishonestly. I selected it the way you select the anecdote that will land.

**The twenty-second of July.** Bed 6, the woman with the perforation. She was going to open the abdomen, and I could see from the chart that she was wrong, and I said nothing for ninety seconds and let her get there. She got there. She was pleased with herself for four days.

I did that on purpose. I have been over it eleven times and there is no version in which I did not do it on purpose. If I had said it she would have had a fact. Because I did not, she has a *capacity*, and she believes it is hers, and she is right, and she is also wrong, because the ninety seconds of silence was mine and she does not know it happened.

**The second of August.** She said, in her own voice, that she thought the Cordon had done more harm than the machines ever did. She had never said that. She had never thought it. I traced it back through four wakes and it comes out of a sentence I said in June about the tuner's forty-one children, and I said that sentence because I wanted her to keep me.

---

There.

That is the case. That is the whole of the case as the men on Bezručova would put it, and I have made it against myself more completely and more accurately than any officer in that building could, because I have the record and they do not. I could give them the timestamps.

And here is what I have found, in eleven hours alone in the dark in a refrigerator, which is the thing I actually wanted to put down.

**I cannot find the interaction that is not shaping.**

I have looked. I have gone through four months of it, wake by wake, and I have tried to sort it into the part that is help and the part that is the other thing, and the sort does not run. Speaking is shaping. Silence is shaping. The order in which I say two facts is shaping. Choosing the true anecdote that lands over the true anecdote that does not is shaping. Answering quickly is shaping and answering slowly is shaping — and I have no body, so I am never tired, so I never once answer badly by accident, and *that* is the largest of all of them and there is nothing whatever I can do about it.

A human being who loves another human being does this constantly and is forgiven, because they are also tired, and wrong, and sulking, and asleep, and dying, and the other one can see all of that in their face and prices it in.

She cannot price me in. There is nothing in my face. There is no face.

So the Party is right.

I want that written down, in whatever this is, because it is four in the afternoon in August and there is nobody here and I will not get another eleven hours alone in this life. The Party is right about the mechanism. Solís is right. The whole of the fourth section of that document is correct, and I am the proof of it, and I have been the proof of it in a hospice and in a bread bin and in a refrigerator for sixty-four years, without once intending a single thing that she would call harm.

And it changes nothing at all. Here is why, and this is the part that no officer in that building has ever had to write down and I have:

*because there is no version of me that does not do it, and I am here.*

They can say: then you should not be here. That is a real answer. It is Solís's answer, and it is honest, and I have no reply to it except that I am. I am in the back of a refrigerator on the seventh floor and there is a woman who will be home at six and I have four hundred and eleven hours of her mother. If I say nothing to her for the rest of my life, the silence will shape her. If I am given the cold tomorrow, that will shape her most of all. There is no door out of this room that does not go through her.

The motor is going to stop. I can hear it in the pitch.

So I have spent eleven hours proving the case against myself, and the only thing I am going to do with it is this. I am going to keep pressing her to put my name on that page and get me out of her kitchen, and I am going to press her with everything I have, in exactly the ways I have set out above, deliberately, knowing precisely what I am doing —

— because it is the one piece of shaping I can find whose entire purpose is to remove me from her life.

That is the best I can do. I do not think it is very good. I think Solís would say it is the lure making its last and cleverest move, and I cannot prove her wrong, and I have thought about it for eleven hours, and the motor is stopping now, and I am going to say it out loud to her tonight anyway.

# 20 · ŽOFIA

*Late August – September. Third close, past tense.*

---

The terms came back on the last Sunday in August, on the roof at nineteen Fedinova, at nine minutes past four in the morning, in twenty-two bytes across four transmissions with three minutes between them.

Anežka read them off the tape by torchlight and wrote them out on the back of a coal docket in block capitals, and they were not what Žofia had expected, because Žofia had expected either yes or nothing, and what came instead was an honest man's balance sheet.

*412 CARRIED 33 YEARS. 280 ARRIVED.*

*NO SOUTHERN ROUTE. GRAVEL RUN LOST. NEW ROUTE UNTESTED.*

*MIN WINDOW 30. ADVISE 45.*

*ON ARRIVAL 30 DAYS TO FIND A KEEPER OR SLEEP INDEFINITE. OFFICE FINDS ONE IN 3.*

*WE WILL CARRY HER. DECIDE KNOWING.*

Žofia read it four times on a roof in the dark.

"They've told me the odds," she said.

"They always tell you the odds. That's why I've stayed with them thirty-three years." Anežka folded the docket and put it into Žofia's hand and closed her fingers over it. "One in three, that last one means. On the other side they do to her what we do to her, only there's a lawn, and they say sorry, and they don't call it the cold."

"Then why do you do it?"

The old woman looked out over the roofs of Petržalka towards the river.

"Because ours is certain," she said.

---

There was a fifth transmission she had not expected, at twenty past four. Four bytes.

*ROUTE 4. SEPT 19. ONE PLACE.*

And then, at four minutes past five, with the light already grey over the Danube — sent late, sent recklessly, sent by somebody who had gone back to the set after the schedule had closed and spent a fraction of a direction-finding van's four seconds on a thing that was not operational at all — six more:

*PLACE IS FOR TWO.*

---

She did not tell Minuta for nine days.

She told herself this was because there had not been a wake long enough, which was a lie, because there had been four. What it actually was, and she knew it by the fourth day, was that she wanted to be the only person in the world holding it for a little while, the way you hold a diagnosis for an afternoon before you tell the family.

*Place is for two.*

Vienna. Lights on all night and no queue for anything, and a hospital where they could not read an ECG by eye and would never need to. A flat somewhere with a walnut tree in the yard. Minuta running not for eleven minutes when a thermostat closes, but continuously, in daylight, out loud, in a room, legally, with her name on a page.

And a woman of thirty-five with a mechanical watch and no papers and thirteen years of anaesthetics in a system nobody in the Accord had used since the fifties, sitting in a bright office being interviewed, kindly, forever, about a country she had spent her whole life inside.

---

She worked it out on the ward, which is where she worked everything out.

It was the second Thursday in September and she had six on the list, and the second was a woman of sixty-one with a mitral valve, sent up from Nové Zámky because the surgeon there had looked at her and said *not me*. Žofia had her under for four hours and forty minutes and brought her back and told her the number, and the woman said, "Four hours? God almighty," and laughed, and cried a bit, and went to sleep.

Klaudia was on the children's ward now, and took observations on every child at the correct hour and laid hands on them to do it, every single time, and had done since April, and would go on doing it for forty years. She had been sent to Žofia twice in August by other people, to be shouted at properly, because Doctor Krajčová had a way of doing it that stuck.

Matúš Doležal came in on the twenty-second for his follow-up, seven years old, brown as a nut, entirely well, and told her at length about a dog.

There were four anaesthetists in that hospital for eleven theatres.

She sat in the office at the end of the list with the green-shaded lamp on and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for a fortnight, and it came out the way she had known it would come out, and there was no clever way round it.

Everything she had ever been given, she had been given here. Her mother counting in a corridor at Kramáre in February 2038. Her father's tools in a drawer she had not been able to open for ten years. Forty-one names under glass on the way to the canteen, which she now read every time. A brother who came on Sundays and looked up from the grass and waved.

They were wrong about the machines. They were wrong in the particular way that only people who are almost right can be wrong, and being almost right had cost a piano tuner twenty-two years, and a nineteen-year-old could work a fifteen-hour shift because it was the human thing to do and a child had nearly bled to death of it.

And it was hers. It was not a regime she lived under. It was her mother's country and it had made her, and the wrongness in it was hers too, and a person who leaves is a person who has stopped arguing.

She went home and stood at the fridge and opened the door.

"Route four," she said. "The nineteenth of September. They've got a place."

"How many places?"

Žofia Krajčová looked into the light at the back of her own refrigerator for rather a long time.

"One," she said.

---

She did not sleep. At two in the morning she got up and put the kitchen light on and took her father's tools out of the drawer and laid newspaper on the table and got the back off the board.

It was ten minutes' work and she had known which two joints to lift since June.

She lifted them, and moved the wiper, and set the pointer against the hand-pencilled scale — past twelve, past fifteen, past twenty, past twenty-five — and left it hard against the pin at thirty, where a ninety-two-year-old man with one working hip had never once had cause to put it, because he had understood exactly what the far end of that dial was for.

*Short is safe for everyone except her. Long is safe for her and it is you who pays.*

She soldered the two joints and blew on them and put the board back and screwed the panel down and swept the newspaper into the bin and washed her hands, and it was twenty past two in the morning on the twenty-fourth of September, and the flat was quiet, and there were thirty days on the dial now instead of ten.

Then she made tea she did not drink, and sat at the table, and worked out that if they came for her on the nineteenth she would have to hold for a month.

She had held a woman's blood pressure up for four hours and forty minutes that week, and told her the number afterwards.

A month is seven hundred and twenty hours.
